# Concept art: rules for figures and casts

Distilled from the standard teaching texts:
- Loomis, *Figure Drawing for All It's Worth*;
- Hampton, *Figure Drawing: Design and Invention*;
- Bridgman, *Constructive Anatomy*;
- Bancroft, *Creating Characters with Personality*;
- Stanchfield, *Drawn to Life*.

These are working rules, not laws. Check every figure against the measured ranges (proportions.md).

## Build order (whole shape first)

1. **Line of action.** One curve through the body (Stanchfield). Find it before anything else, even in an idle
   pose.
2. **Masses.** Draw three boxes or eggs: ribcage, pelvis, head. Tilt them against each other (contrapposto) and
   join them with the spine.
3. **Landmarks.** Place them from the measured fractions (proportions.md): shoulder 0.82, navel 0.60, hip joint
   0.51, knee 0.28 of stature.
4. **Limbs.** Draw them as tapered cylinders. Keep straights against curves: one side of a limb straight, the
   other curved, and alternate down the limb (Loomis, Hampton).
5. **Then** costume, gear, wear and detail.

## Silhouette

- **Fill it black.** A character must read as a black shape at the distance the player sees it from.
  - In a shooter that is often 20 to 100 m, where a person is 30 to 150 pixels tall.
  - Faces are gone at that range. What remains is stature, mass, shoulder-to-hip shape, posture, the head's
    shape (hat, helmet, hair) and one or two big props.
- **Each group of a cast needs its own silhouette rule.** For example: one group wide at the top, one narrow
  and upright, one asymmetric and bent. The player should be able to tell a guard from a labourer at 50 m with
  no colour.
- **Negative space matters:** the gap between arm and body, between the legs, under a hat brim.

## Shape language (Bancroft and others)

| shape | reads as | body cues |
|---|---|---|
| square, rectangle | stable, strong, stubborn, institutional | wide shoulders and waist, straight sides, heavy feet, square head and jaw |
| circle | soft, friendly, safe, sometimes comic | round mass, short limbs, big head relative to body |
| triangle (point up) | heroic, active | wide shoulders, narrow hips, the V |
| triangle (point down) | grounded, settled, older, or a pear shape | narrow shoulders, wide hips |
| sharp, thin, angular | dangerous, nervous, hungry | long thin limbs, sharp joints, narrow head |

Combine one main shape with a secondary one, never all of them on one figure.

## Exaggeration budget

- **Push 1 or 2 traits per character, and keep the rest near the median.**
  - A tall thin man with a normal head.
  - A short wide woman with long arms.
  - Pushing everything reads as a cartoon. Pushing nothing reads as a mannequin.
- **For realism, stay inside p5 to p95** (proportions.md). Use p1/p99 for a few notable people, and beyond
  that only for a reason.
- **Games often enlarge heads and hands about 5 to 10%** so they read at distance and in first person. Do it
  for the whole cast, or it reads as a deformity on one person.

## Variety in a crowd

- **Vary stature and build separately.** Build is mass and breadth; it is weakly tied to height (r 0.2 to 0.4).
- **Allometry:** tall people are leggier and smaller-headed, short people the reverse. A short person is not a
  scaled-down tall one (proportions.md, "Allometry").
- **Spread:** across 10 adults of one sex the tallest is about 12% taller than the shortest; mass can
  differ by 50%.
- **Mix ages, and posture with them.** The young stand straight and loose. The old lean their heads forward and
  round their shoulders. Tired people let their arms hang and their weight sink.

## Story in the body

- **Work shapes bodies:**
  - Heavy lifting builds upper-body mass and thicker hands and forearms.
  - Desk work softens the waist and rounds the shoulders.
  - Hunger shows as thinness and visible joints, and in the face first.
- **Costume tells job and status** (Bancroft).
  - Gear sits where the job needs it: a tool belt, a pack, gloves.
  - Wear sits where the body touches the world: knees, elbows, cuffs, shoulder straps.
- **Posture is the cheapest emotion.** The same body slumped, upright, or leaning forward says three different
  things. Before you change the model, change the pose.

## Values and colour

- **Three values per figure:** dark, mid and light. Group them, so the clothing reads as one or two big shapes,
  not many small ones.
- **Put the accent (the brightest or most saturated colour) where you want the eye:** the face, the hands, the
  faction badge.
- **Faction colour** goes in one big shape (jacket, helmet) and one small repeat (cuffs, a patch). Don't spread
  it everywhere.
