# Erode first, mask by slope, light softly

Crysis, FUEL, MotorStorm and the LEGO games all made big terrain look good with **offline data rather than shader tech**: a non-tiling painted colour map with colourless high-frequency detail on top, texture layers gated by slope and altitude, baked soft sun shadow and sky-occlusion darkening, fog whose colour is sampled from the sky's horizon, real-world elevation data shaping the large forms, hand-authored meshes for the skyline, and a deliberately quiet ground so placed objects carry the eye. Every one of those moves fits Unreal Engine 2's TerrainInfo model, which is a square power-of-two heightmap (256×256 is the documented sweet spot) with up to 32 alpha-blended layers whose masks are forced to the same resolution as the heightmap, per-vertex baked lighting, side-projected layers for cliffs, scattered DecoLayer meshes, and a DistanceFog cull ([UDN CreatingTerrain](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/CreatingTerrain.html)). The part none of the games had to solve and your generator does is **shape**: noise of any flavour produces a plausible power spectrum but no drainage network, and the one published realism function fit to human judgement (PTRM) rates noise and thermal-only terrain far below real DEMs precisely because it lacks valleys, ridges and hollows ([PTRM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04610)). The fix is a three-stage simulation that runs in seconds at 256×256 in NumPy: stream-power incision with uplift for the valley layout, batched droplet erosion for gullies, and thermal slumping with per-material angles of repose and height-banded hardness for cliffs, ledges and scree. The same simulation state (wear, deposit, debris, flow accumulation, slope, elevation) is then the direct source of the UE2 layer masks and the rock-mesh and grass placement rules, which is what CryEngine's painter and Gaea's Flow/Wear/Deposits outputs do by hand. Fidelity is scored against public-domain SRTM and USGS 3DEP tiles using geomorphon class fractions, Horton bifurcation ratios, slope-histogram distances and the hypsometric integral, with photo-side checks (DINOv2 embeddings, skyline spectra) against CC0/NPS photographs as a tie-breaker; the ordered implementation plan and a toolbox of code and papers close the report.

## The four games won with painted colour, gated layers and baked light

Crysis is the best-documented case and the clearest statement of the recipe. Its terrain is an ordinary heightmap at **2 m per sample with a default 256 m height range**, with the largest shipped level about 4 km on a side after Crytek abandoned a 21 km design as not worth the streaming effort ([Mittring, SIGGRAPH 2007 course notes](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2007/Mittring-Finding_NextGen_CryEngine2(Siggraph07%20Course%20Notes).pdf); [CryENGINE 3 Cookbook sample chapter](https://design.tutsplus.com/articles/cryengine-3-cookbook-sample-chapter-terrain-sculpting--cg-10607)). What made it read as rich was the **macro/detail split**: each layer pairs a low-frequency Surface colour texture, baked into one huge non-tiling map (16384² recommended for shipping), with a tiling Detail material that is deliberately a **grey high-pass diffuse plus normal map tiled at 0.25–0.5**, so colour never tiles and the repeating detail carries no colour pattern a viewer can latch onto ([World of Level Design](https://www.worldofleveldesign.com/categories/cryengine-3-sdk/cryengine-3-sdk-06-terrain-layer-texture-painting.php); [Terrain.Layer shader docs](https://docs.cryengine.com/display/SDKDOC2/Terrain.Layer+Shader); [polycount](https://polycount.com/discussion/127279/confused-about-terrain-textures)). The detail layer is faded out beyond a distance that ranges from 64 units on the Low preset to 2048–4096 on Very High, so far hills show only calm painted colour ([TweakGuides mirror](https://vandalfsen.me/tweakguides/Crysis_11.html)). Layers are constrained by **per-layer Altitude (metres) and Slope (degrees) masks, with the documented cliff example at 55°–90°**, and vegetation groups carry the same Density, Elevation and Slope filters plus Align-to-Terrain and a "Use Terrain Color" option that tints grass from the ground colour so its fade line disappears ([CryENGINE 3 Manual](https://www.cryengine.com/docs/static/engines/cryengine-3/categories/1114113/pages/15008665); [Vegetation Creation docs](https://docs.cryengine.com/display/SDKDOC2/Vegetation+Creation)). Rocks are placed through the very same vegetation painter with a high slope range, so "steep means rock" is a group filter, not a runtime shader.

Crysis's lighting contributions are equally low-frequency and equally bakeable. Terrain self-shadow is a **variance shadow map cast by the terrain alone** because ordinary shadow maps "gave us a consistent look that wasn't soft enough"; ambient darkening comes from a **"terrain sky accessibility" term computed during the surface-texture bake** that darkens the ground under tree groups and in valleys; and the sky uses a Nishita single-scattering solve whose horizon samples are averaged so that **fog colour equals the sky colour at the horizon**, which is why distant mountains in fog blend into the sky instead of sitting in front of it ([Mittring 2007 notes §8.5.3.8](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2007/Mittring-Finding_NextGen_CryEngine2(Siggraph07%20Course%20Notes).pdf); [narby.net Sandbox 2 tutorial](https://www.narby.net/?page_id=40); [Wenzel, SIGGRAPH 2006 ch. 6](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2006/Chapter6-Real-time%20Atmospheric%20Effects%20in%20Games.pdf)). Wenzel singles out cloud shadows, cast in a single screen pass, for breaking "up the regularity of shading giving a more natural look", and a Sandbox tutorial recommends a sun at 10am or 2pm to avoid unnatural straight-down shadows ([Wenzel 2006 §6.9.1](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2006/Chapter6-Real-time%20Atmospheric%20Effects%20in%20Games.pdf); [narby.net](https://www.narby.net/?page_id=39)). Vegetation shading uses a per-vertex hemisphere ambient, `lerp(ambient*0.5, ambient, saturate(normal.z*0.5+0.5))`, and stores precomputed AO in vertex alpha ([GPU Gems 3 ch. 16](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems3/part-iii-rendering/chapter-16-vegetation-procedural-animation-and-shading-crysis)). Roughly four fifths of what makes Crysis terrain look nice is therefore data a 2003 engine can carry: a painted colour map, slope/altitude masks, baked soft shadow and AO, scatter rules, and a fog colour taken from the sky.

FUEL shows what carries at the other end of the scale, where nothing is painted. Codemasters stated that its **14,400 km² world was built from satellite data of the western USA and generated deterministically at runtime** ("Generated doesn't mean random though, it's the same result every time") with a 40 km draw distance where the horizon is real drivable land, and that storing it conventionally would have taken "about four Blu-rays" ([VideoGamer](https://www.videogamer.com/previews/fuel-interview/); [PCG Wiki summary of the RPS interview](http://pcg.wikidot.com/pcg-games:fuel); [Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/110300-largest-playable-area-in-a-racing-videogame)). Asobo published no technical account, so the internals come from Shamus Young's observational analysis: a coarse base heightmap defining ranges and water bodies, **small repeated detail patches layered on top** (he spotted the same horseshoe ridge recurring kilometres apart at a different scale and texture), vegetation and roads disguising the repetition, and a **hierarchical road network of backbone highways, a loose grid of secondary roads and dirt tracks between them** with emergent bends, no overpasses anywhere, and blurred texture seams at junctions ([Twenty Sided: Fuel: Terrain](https://www.shamusyoung.com/twentysidedtale/?p=5267); [Twenty Sided: Fuel: Roads](https://www.shamusyoung.com/twentysidedtale/?p=5237)). Reviewers praised the biomes, palette, weather and day-night cycle and panned the near-field: low-resolution textures, tree pop-in and "the same pre-fab building, placed in many different locations" with identical sub-arrangements ([Twenty Sided: Final Thoughts](https://www.shamusyoung.com/twentysidedtale/?p=5356); [bit-tech review](https://bit-tech.net/reviews/gaming/playstation/fuel-review/3/)). The lesson for a generator is where variety comes from: **real-DEM-shaped macro relief and zone-level art direction (palette, sky, weather) were credited; prefab repetition was the single most-noticed flaw**. FUEL's documented contemporaries confirm the shape of the method. Far Cry 2's tools let designers place rules ("a type of tree", a road spline) and the terrain and vegetation re-flow automatically, with a production target of building a square kilometre in a day; Just Cause 2 used 33×33-vertex terrain cells, procedural shader splatting by painted masks, spline decals for roads and mask-driven mesh decoration ([Game Developer: MIGS Far Cry 2](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/migs-i-far-cry-2-i-s-guay-on-the-importance-of-procedural-content); [Game Developer: Behind the Scenes of Far Cry 2](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/in-depth-behind-the-scenes-of-i-far-cry-2-i-); [GameDev Pensieve on Just Cause 2](https://www.gamedevpensieve.com/graphics/3d/3d_landscape/3d_landscape_example)). Guay's remark that a purely algorithmic sky was "a total failure", replaced by a procedural skybox under artistic oversight, is a warning that applies to palette as much as to clouds.

MotorStorm and LEGO Star Wars contribute the art-direction half. Evolution chose Monument Valley for its mesas and buttes and **built the landscape as hand-modelled polygon meshes from HD footage, concept art, Google Earth elevation data and photographs**, then "terraformed" corners that were too tight for the speed; the palette came from **ten hours of sunrise and sunset aerial footage shot in 10-bit RGB** for its "dark orange to burnt red" colouring, and the studio shipped ungraded frame grabs in-game to prove the look was real ([Definition Magazine, Making of MotorStorm](https://definitionmagazine.com/features/making-of-motorstorm/)). Producer Simon Benson's placement rule is worth quoting as a generator rule: "If we select a piece of vegetation we'll ensure it's planted in the appropriate pebbly ground or surrounded by suitable plants" ([Definition Magazine](https://definitionmagazine.com/features/making-of-motorstorm/)). The memorable silhouette, in other words, was authored geometry with a reference photo in hand, and the driveable floor was the only part that behaved like a heightfield. The LEGO games are undocumented on the engine side, but the stated philosophy, "We are delivering imagination and not simulation", and the fact that a TT concept artist's job was to "provide mood and color palettes and sketch out lighting conditions" per level point at the same discipline: pick four to six colours per level, give the ground a desaturated member of the set, and let placed objects carry saturation ([Starwarz.com interview with Jonathan Smith](https://starwarz.com/tbone/interview-with-jonathan-smith-giant-interactive-tt-games-lego-star-wars-original-posting-march-15-2005/); [Creative Bloq on Traveller's Tales](https://www.creativebloq.com/3d/travellers-tales-4099123)). Across the four games the common thread is that **shape and lighting beat texture detail, big shapes are meshes or real DEMs rather than noise, layers are few and clearly separated, and the look is decided from one real place at one hour of the day before anything is built**.

## UE2 terrain is a 256² grid where masks equal geometry resolution

Unreal II's terrain is the UT2003 TerrainInfo system (Unreal II is build 2226), and its data model is simpler than it looks from the editor. A TerrainInfo is one square power-of-two heightmap texture (P8 or G16, 16-bit when converted) with one quad per heightmap pixel, scaled by `TerrainScale`, default **(64, 64, 64) UU**, so a 256×256 map is 255 quads of 64 UU per side; UDN notes that textures "up to 2048*2048" are theoretically allowed but that **"Most of the times textures with dimensions 256*256 will do fine"**, and community reports put crashes at 768×768 and in the Smooth tool at 512×512, with the standing advice to enlarge the quads rather than the heightmap ([UDN CreatingTerrain](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/CreatingTerrain.html); [UnrealWiki: Using The Terrain Tool](https://beyondunrealwiki.github.io/pages/using-the-terrain-tool.html); [UnCodex: Engine.TerrainInfo](http://ericdives.com/UT2004-UnCodex/Source_engine/terraininfo.html)). The vertical range is the full 16-bit span scaled by Z: UDN's displacement-floor trick states that with Z=1 "the tiles will be 256 units high", so the default Z=64 spans 16384 UU and the recommended initial fill of 32768 is the midpoint ([UDN EditingTerrainLayers](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/EditingTerrainLayers.html)). This is exactly CryEngine's documented trade of resolution times metres-per-unit, and it means a generator that wants Crysis-scale views must spend the 256 cells on a larger quad size and put near detail into textures and meshes rather than vertices.

The layer system is where the engine dictates the generator's design. There are **32 layers, each a Material plus an RGBA8 alpha map, blended top-down in a contiguous stack** (an empty slot terminates it, and Layer[0] is forced opaque), and the alpha map **must match the heightmap's dimensions**, otherwise "the texture may only appear as spots on a regular grid" ([UDN EditingTerrainLayers](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/EditingTerrainLayers.html); [UDN CreatingTerrain](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/CreatingTerrain.html)). Blending resolution therefore equals geometric resolution, which has two consequences: splat boundaries can never be crisper than a quad, and every layer mask is literally a function on the same 256² grid as the heightmap, so slope, height, curvature and erosion-state masks can be written straight into alpha maps with no resampling. Each layer carries `UScale/VScale`, `UPan/VPan`, `TextureRotation`, a `TextureMapAxis` (XY from above, XZ or YZ from the side) and a `LayerRotation` rotator that projects a layer "in directions other than just straight down", which UDN recommends specifically for cliffs so the texture "won't stretch like it normally would if projected from directly above"; mappers restate this as three copies of a rock layer on three axes ([UDN EditingTerrainLayers](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/EditingTerrainLayers.html); [UT99.org thread](https://ut99.org/viewtopic.php?t=15108)). The documented anti-tiling move is simple: **"Two textures with alpha blending on top of each other, with different scaling and rotation generally don't tile badly"**, and the editor's Noise brush "create[s] random patches of the texture inside your brush" to speckle alpha edges while Smooth softens them ([UnrealWiki: Terrain](https://beyondunrealwiki.github.io/pages/terrain.html); [UDN EditingTerrainLayers](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/EditingTerrainLayers.html)). The layer input is typed `Material`, so Shaders, Combiners and TexModifiers are legal and the Texture class has its own `Detail`/`DetailScale` properties, but no source confirms that detail or specular channels render on terrain in any UE2 build, and there are no normal maps; the proven path is plain tiling textures ([UnrealWiki: Texture](https://beyondunrealwiki.github.io/pages/texture.html); [UnrealWiki: Creating A Material](https://beyondunrealwiki.github.io/pages/creating-a-material.html)).

Lighting is per vertex and that fixes the softness budget. `bStaticLighting=true` is the default and the class stores a `VertexLightMap` and `VertexColors`; the wiki is blunt that "Shadows become more unsharp when the heightmap gets smaller" and that "Objects smaller than terrain quads won't cast visible shadows" ([UnCodex TerrainInfo](http://ericdives.com/UT2004-UnCodex/Source_engine/terraininfo.html); [UnrealWiki: Using The Terrain Tool](https://beyondunrealwiki.github.io/pages/using-the-terrain-tool.html)). Sunlight is a parallel light whose rotation sets direction; its rays are stopped by every non-Fake-Backdrop wall, sunlights light only their own zone, and multiple coloured suns are allowed ([UDN AdditionalTerrainTips](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/AdditionalTerrainTips.html)). Static meshes are vertex-lit too, so low-poly rocks get light gaps unless tessellated or built as connected pieces ([Hourences: Vertex Lighting](http://www.hourences.com/tutorials/vtx-lighting.htm)). The useful reading is that Crysis's two low-frequency lighting terms, blurry terrain self-shadow and sky-accessibility darkening, are blurry in UE2 for free: a low sun plus a dim bluish second sun from the opposite side gives crease darkening at vertex resolution, and anything sharper must be painted into a dark "grime" layer or carried by the colour map. DecoLayers inherit the lighting of the quad they sit on, so grass in a sun-shadowed hollow darkens automatically ([UDN CreatingDecoLayers](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/CreatingDecoLayers.html)).

DecoLayers are the scatter system and they are grass, not trees. UDN states that their static meshes "do not impede players, so using them for trees isn't realistic, but DecoLayers are better used for grass, flowers and ferns"; each layer has a DensityMap (white = densest), a ScaleMap (R and G width and length, B height), a ColorMap (must be RGBA8 "otherwise all decorations will become black"), a `ScaleMultiplier` min/max per axis, `FadeoutRadius` (example Min 500, Max 1200 UU), `MaxPerQuad`, `Seed`, `AlignToTerrain` ("useful for such things as rocks and debris, but when used on trees and grass it can look out of place") and a `DrawOrder` where SORT_BackToFront fixes alpha sorting ([UDN CreatingDecoLayers](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/CreatingDecoLayers.html)). The wiki's performance rule is the one to encode: for continuous cover at distance "consider using relatively sparse density maps, but let things fade away further", and for dense near grass fade it out sooner ([UnrealWiki: Terrain Decoration Layer](https://unrealarchive.org/wikis/unreal-wiki/Legacy:Terrain_Decoration_Layer.html)). Unreal II differs from UT2004 in ways that matter to a script: its DecorationLayer struct **lacks `RandomYaw`, `LitDirectional`, `DisregardTerrainLighting` and `DetailMode`** and its TerrainLayer lacks KFriction/KRestitution, while it adds `bNonColliding` and a global config `DecoLayerDensity` default 1.0, so grass clumps will all face the same way and take terrain vertex light unless you ship several DecoLayers with different seeds and pre-rotated mesh variants ([UnCodex: Unreal II Engine.TerrainInfo](https://greatemerald.eu/uncodex/U2/engine/terraininfo.html)). The Unreal II editor also "cannot be created 'on the fly'" heightmaps, its 16-bit conversion is flaky with BMP (PCX recommended), terrain "always display[s] in Dynamic Light mode" and shows black without a light, and imported heightmaps often sit far too low ([OldUnreal: Unreal 2 editor thread](https://www.oldunreal.com/phpBB3/viewtopic.php?t=4351)). Since your pipeline already writes textures into the package, these editor quirks are bypassed; the one validation worth doing is checking your G16 writer against a heightmap exported from UnrealEd, because Epic's G16 "is not actually a valid BMP file" ([Gildor forum](https://www.gildor.org/smf/index.php?topic=667.0)).

Three further engine facts shape the plan. UnrealScript's only runtime terrain call is `PokeTerrain(WorldLocation, Radius, MaxDepth)`, so everything else is authored data ([UnCodex TerrainInfo](http://ericdives.com/UT2004-UnCodex/Source_engine/terraininfo.html)). "You can have as many TerrainInfo Actors as you want in a zone" and they simply intersect, which the wiki summarises as support for "multiple terrains of varying scales within single level", so a coarse far-hills terrain with large quads can sit under a fine play-area terrain, which is the UE2 equivalent of CryEngine's lowest LOD plus baked colour at the horizon ([UDN AdditionalTerrainTips](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/AdditionalTerrainTips.html); [UnrealWiki: Terrain](https://beyondunrealwiki.github.io/pages/terrain.html)). And draw distance is DistanceFog: "all polygons of the terrain that are behind this distance will not be displayed", `DistanceFogEndMin` ties to the player's draw-distance slider, and UDN tells you to match the skybox fog colour so culled geometry is not visible, which is Wenzel's horizon-matching rule stated for a 2003 renderer ([UDN AdditionalTerrainTips](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/AdditionalTerrainTips.html)). Hidden terrain goes behind antiportals ("Drop one under a hill, and boom: sudden smoothness") and steep or holed terrain behind the stock cliff meshes (ArboreaLandscape, AntalusStatic, CB-Desert1, IceCliffs) ([Ian Chapin](https://www.ianchapin.com/blog/ut2004-maps); [UT99.org thread](https://ut99.org/viewtopic.php?t=15108)).

## Noise passes the spectrum test and fails the realism test; erosion fixes it

The reason generated hills look fake is now quantified. Natural topography has a power spectrum close to `k^-2` and fBm with gain 0.5 per octave reproduces it ("measured mountain profiles confirm -9dB/octave"), but Pelletier showed that random-phase `k^-2` surfaces lack river networks and that therefore "the power spectrum is not a complete representation" ([Inigo Quilez, fBm](https://iquilezles.org/articles/fbm/); [Pelletier, Why is topography fractal?](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/9705033)). PTRM made that perceptual: in a 70-person two-alternative study rendered from a 45° camera, real SRTM terrains scored around **0.76 while a thermal-erosion synthetic scored 0.51 and synthetic fluvial and coastal terrains 0.23–0.24**, the gap explained by real terrain's "strong presence of valleys, ridges, and hollows" that synthetic terrains lack ([PTRM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04610)). So noise is the base, not the product, and the generator's parameters must be tuned against drainage and landform statistics, not against spectra.

Noise still sets the landform family and the published starting values are concrete. fBm uses gain `G = 2^-H`, lacunarity about 2 ("lacunarity is a non-issue, as we almost always leave it set at a value very close to 2.0"), and Musgrave argues uniform roughness is itself unrealistic because "Real mountains ... rise out of flatter terrain and have rolling foothills", motivating multifractals where higher octaves are scaled by the lower-frequency value; his hybrid multifractal starts at **H 0.25, offset 0.7**, and the ridged multifractal at **H 1.0, offset 1.0, gain 2.0** ([Musgrave, Procedural Fractal Terrains](https://www.classes.cs.uchicago.edu/archive/2015/fall/23700-1/final-project/MusgraveTerrain00.pdf); [musgrave.c](https://engineering.purdue.edu/~ebertd/texture/1stEdition/musgrave/musgrave.c)). Red Blob's recipe adds the practical controls: octave amplitudes `[1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125]`, a redistribution `pow(e*1.2, exponent)` with exponent 1–3 to push mid-elevations into valleys, ridged noise `2*(0.5 - abs(0.5 - noise))` applied only on high ground by multiplying higher octaves by the accumulated elevation, and terraces `round(e*n)/n` with n 2–15 for mesas ([Red Blob Games](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/terrain-from-noise/)). Olsen's measured best base was **two thirds smoothed diamond-square plus one third Voronoi (c1 = -1, c2 = 1), then a domain-warp of magnitude 0.25**, which maximised his slope-based erosion score, and dandrino's README describes the same warp ("fBm offset by another fBm noise map") as giving a tectonically deformed look ([Olsen 2004](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf); [terrain-erosion-3-ways](https://github.com/dandrino/terrain-erosion-3-ways)). Even better than any noise for the macro shape is FUEL's move: a real DEM tile of the landform class you want, resampled to 256² and amplified with seeded noise, which guarantees the large-scale drainage pattern is real before simulation starts.

Three erosion families are implementable in a few hundred NumPy lines and they do different jobs. **Stream power with uplift** lays out dendritic valleys and a steady-state range: `dh/dt = U - K A^m S^n` on a D8 drainage graph, with the common exponent ratio m/n = 0.5, a priority-flood fill first so every cell drains (an O(N log N) algorithm whose "pseudocode is only 20 lines"), and Braun and Willett's implicit per-node update for unconditional stability ([Kwang & Parker 2017](https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/5/807/2017/); [Barnes, Lehman, Mulla 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0098300413001337); [Braun & Willett 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169555X12004618)). Kwang and Parker's warning matters for a 256² map: with hillslope diffusion neglected, m/n = 0.5 makes the landscape "invariant to horizontal stretching", so a diffusion term `D * laplacian(h)` is what gives relief its scale. Cordonnier et al. built the first graphics method combining uplift and stream power from a painted uplift map and Schott et al. 2023 made it interactive with inverse modelling from real DEMs, with code on GitHub ([Cordonnier 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/publications/cordonnier2016large/); [Schott 2023](https://aparis69.github.io/public_html/projects/schott2023_Uplift.html)). **Droplet erosion** then cuts the gullies that read as "eroded" at close range. Lague's port of Beyer's algorithm ships with erosionRadius 3, inertia 0.05, sedimentCapacityFactor 4, minSedimentCapacity 0.01, erodeSpeed 0.3, depositSpeed 0.3, evaporateSpeed 0.01, gravity 4 and maxDropletLifetime 30; erodr's defaults are 70,000 particles, ttl 32, radius 2, inertia 0.3, capacity 8, erosion 0.7, deposition 0.3, so treat the two as a range ([SebLague Erosion.cs](https://github.com/SebLague/Hydraulic-Erosion/blob/master/Assets/Scripts/Erosion.cs); [henrikglass/erodr](https://github.com/henrikglass/erodr)). McDonald's hydrology variant adds the two maps a texturer wants, a time-averaged stream map and pools, and couples deposition to vegetation density, though he notes the particle model "is not capable of simulating actual fluid flow and rivers" ([Procedural Hydrology](https://nickmcd.me/2020/04/15/procedural-hydrology/); [Simple Particle-Based Hydraulic Erosion](https://nickmcd.me/2020/04/10/simple-particle-based-hydraulic-erosion/)). The **virtual-pipe shallow-water model** is the third option when you want ponding and lakes; Jákó and Tóth publish a complete table (dt 0.02, rain Kr 0.012, evaporation Ke 0.015, pipe area A 20, g 9.81, capacity Kc 1, suspension Ks 0.5, deposition Kd 1, hardness softening Kh 5, max erosion depth 10, thermal rate Kt 0.15, talus coefficients Ka 0.8 and Ki 0.1) with results shown after 1000 iterations and single-thread CPU cost of 102–325 ms per iteration at 256² ([Jákó & Tóth 2011](https://old.cescg.org/CESCG-2011/papers/TUBudapest-Jako-Balazs.pdf)).

Thermal erosion and strata produce the cliffs, ledges and scree that make hills look like rock rather than dough. Olsen's rule is ten lines: for each lower neighbour with height difference above a talus threshold T, move `c*(d_max - T)` of material proportionally, with **c = 0.5 ("higher values may cause oscillation"), T = 4/N, and "no more than 50 iterations ... are needed"** ([Olsen 2004](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf)). Materials set T via `T = dx * tan(angle)`: dry sand rests at 34°, soil at 30–45°, and talus slopes "most commonly rest at approximately 33-37°" with a low-angle basal concavity; alluvial fans average about 5° and rarely exceed 10°; beach faces run from 0.6° for very fine sand to 40° for boulders ([Wikipedia, Angle of repose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_repose); [Talus slopes](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/371031125_Talus_slopes); [Wikipedia, Alluvial fan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alluvial_fan); [Bujan et al. 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0025322718304365)). Strata come from a per-cell hardness `R` that both slows erosion and raises the talus angle, `tan(alpha_min) = R*Ka + Ki` in Jákó's formulation; Št'ava's model carries up to four material layers with slippage per layer, and Cordonnier 2017 uses bedrock, granular, vegetation and dead-vegetation layers with erosion and vegetation feeding back on each other ([Jákó & Tóth 2011](https://old.cescg.org/CESCG-2011/papers/TUBudapest-Jako-Balazs.pdf); [Št'ava 2008](https://cgg.mff.cuni.cz/~jaroslav/papers/2008-sca-erosim/2008-sca-erosiom-fin.pdf); [Cordonnier 2017](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/publications/cordonnier2017authoring/)). Making hardness a banded function of height (three to six bands, hard bands thin, optionally on a tilted coordinate for dipping beds) is enough: soft bands retreat into slopes and feed scree, hard bands survive as ledges, and a thick cap near the plateau top yields a mesa. Houdini's thermal node frames the same thing as a Cut Angle beyond which cutting stops and a Repose Angle of "30-45° for realistic dirt or rock" ([HeightField Erode Thermal](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/nodes/sop/heightfield_erode_thermal.html)).

The payoff of simulating rather than filtering is that the simulation state is the texture. Gaea's Erosion node outputs Wear ("portions where erosion removes sediments"), Deposits ("resting position of those sediments") and Flow, and its documentation says outright that "By using the masks indicating areas of wear, deposition, and flow, you can create much more interesting terrain textures than a typical height and slope distribution"; World Machine exposes the same Flow/Wear/Deposition masks and Houdini separates hydraulic sediment from thermal debris ([Gaea Erosion](https://docs.quadspinner.com/Reference/Erosion/Erosion.html); [World Machine Erosion](https://help.world-machine.com/topic/device-erosion/); [HeightField Erode 3.0](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/nodes/sop/heightfield_erode.html)). Geomorphons add a purely geometric landform classification (flat, peak, ridge, shoulder, spur, slope, hollow, footslope, valley, pit) from eight line-of-sight directions, with GRASS defaults of search 3 cells and flatness 1° ([r.geomorphon](https://grass.osgeo.org/grass-stable/manuals/r.geomorphon.html)). Those outputs map one-to-one onto UE2 alpha maps because the alpha maps share the heightmap grid.

## Score shape against public DEMs and look against CC0 photos

The reference data is free and the licences are simple. USGS 3DEP and National Map data are "free and in the public domain. There are no restrictions"; "all SRTM data are in the public domain"; Copernicus GLO-30 is free worldwide but requires its specific DLR/Airbus attribution notice ([USGS FAQ](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-are-terms-uselicensing-map-services-and-data-national-map); [data.gov SRTM GL1](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/nasa-shuttle-radar-topography-mission-global-1-arc-second-v003); [Copernicus DEM licence](https://dataspace.copernicus.eu/sites/default/files/media/files/2025-06/copernicus_contributing_mission_data_access_v2_cop_dem_licenses.pdf)). SRTM and GLO-30 are 30 m, so 256 cells cover about 7.7 km; for a map a few kilometres across use 3DEP 1/3 arc-second (about 10 m) or 1 m lidar so the cell scale matches, because slope statistics shift with DEM resolution (one area measured 26.9–39.9° at 100 m and 33–40.7° at 10 m) ([ESRI knowledge base](https://support.esri.com/en-us/knowledge-base/how-to-calculate-the-average-slope-angle-of-an-area-usi-000029992)). PTRM built its real set from SRTM by geomorphic class (aeolian, coastal, fluvial, glacial, slope), and 20–50 tiles per class you intend to generate is the right size ([PTRM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04610)). For photos, Wikimedia Commons CC0 and public-domain files are the cleanest (each file page carries its own tag and Commons rejects any -NC or -ND licence), National Park Service photographs are public domain with third-party exceptions, and Unsplash and Pexels permit free modification and use without attribution but forbid compiling their content into a competing service, so use them for offline statistics only and never redistribute them ([Commons:Licensing](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Licensing); [NPS disclaimer](https://www.nps.gov/aboutus/disclaimer.htm); [Unsplash License](https://unsplash.com/license); [Pexels License](https://www.pexels.com/license/)).

The DEM-side metrics have published target ranges and all run in under a second per map. The radially averaged power spectrum should fall with an exponent near 2 in one dimension (global fits give beta 1.88, Hurst about 0.55), but since estimators disagree on absolute fractal dimension (2.5 by Turcotte's spectral route, 2.2–2.4 by box-counting), compare generated against reference **with the same estimator** rather than against a textbook number ([Nature Sci. Rep. 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep34005); [arXiv 1611.06190](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.06190)). Slope histograms of real landforms are "mostly unimodal ... with a long tail and right skewness", approximately normal in uplifting ranges and exponential in depositional areas ([Montgomery 2001](https://earth.geology.yale.edu/~ajs/2001/Apr_May/qn10t100432.pdf)). Drainage networks extracted by priority-flood fill, D8 routing and a flow-accumulation threshold should give **Horton bifurcation ratios of 3–5 and length ratios of 1.6–2.4** ([Horton laws, NPG 2014](https://npg.copernicus.org/articles/21/1007/2014/npg-21-1007-2014.pdf)). The hypsometric integral `(mean - min)/(max - min)` classifies the landscape as young (>= 0.6), **mature (0.3–0.6)** or old (< 0.3) ([opengeology.in](https://opengeology.in/hypsometric-curve-and-integral/)). The strongest single number is PTRM itself, a linear regression on the ten normalised geomorphon fractions with R² = 0.72 against the human study: `PTRM = (-38.02 + 3.55*G_depression + 1.75*G_summit + 25.12*G_flat + 9.61*G_valley + 7.59*G_ridge + 6.71*G_hollow + 9.02*G_spur + 7.31*G_shoulder + 28.95*G_slope + 7.63*G_footslope) / 69.96` ([PTRM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04610)). PTRM rewards the slope and flat fractions heavily and was fit on 45° renders, so pair it with an earth-mover's distance between the generated and reference ten-bin geomorphon histograms, which is the discriminating feature the authors found. Argudo et al. 2025 survey the whole metric family with reference implementations and a correlation matrix showing which cheap metrics proxy expensive ones ([CGF 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cgf.70080); [oargudo/terrain-descriptors](https://github.com/oargudo/terrain-descriptors)).

Photo comparison must be distributional, because no photograph matches a generated map pixel for pixel. Render each map from eight to sixteen fixed viewpoints with fixed neutral shading, render the reference DEM tiles identically (which removes the shader-versus-photo domain gap for the shape question), embed everything with a small pretrained network, and compute KID, which is unbiased and "more reliable for smaller sample sizes" than FID ([Rethinking FID, CVPR 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Jayasumana_Rethinking_FID_Towards_a_Better_Evaluation_Metric_for_Image_Generation_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)). DINOv2-small embeds about 1000 images in 12 seconds on the CPU path described in a FAISS tutorial and is the recommended backbone for image-to-image similarity ([Image similarity with DINOv2 and FAISS](https://medium.com/aimonks/image-similarity-with-dinov2-and-faiss-741744bc5804)). LPIPS (`pip install lpips`, 'alex' or 'vgg', runs on CPU) and DISTS (in `piq`) are pairwise and belong only in A/B tests of two parameter settings rendered from identical viewpoints of the same seed ([lpips on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/lpips/); [piq on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/piq/0.5.1/)). No published terrain paper scores heightmaps against photographs this way, so treat the photo tier as a sanity check on texture-mask thresholds and palette, and the DEM tier as the optimiser's objective; a cheap no-network photo statistic worth adding is the skyline: extract silhouettes from near-ground renders and from sky-segmented photos and compare their spectral slope and local-angle histograms. PTRM's finding that perceived realism is driven by landform presence means a photo-embedding comparison will pick up the same thing the geomorphon test already measures, which is why the DEM metrics should carry the weight.

## The ordered plan for the Unreal II generator

The plan below assumes the existing pipeline (heightmap, layer masks and static meshes written to the package by script) and adds the formation model, the mask derivation, the placement rules, the lighting setup and the scoring loop. Parameter values are starting points taken from the cited sources; everything is in normalised grid units (height 0–1, cell size 1) unless stated.

1. **Fix the reference first.** Pick one real landform class and one hour of the day, as Evolution did with Monument Valley at sunrise and sunset ([Definition Magazine](https://definitionmagazine.com/features/making-of-motorstorm/)). Download 20–50 public-domain DEM tiles of that class (SRTM GL1 or Copernicus GLO-30 at 30 m for maps 5–8 km across, USGS 3DEP 1/3 arc-second or 1 m lidar for maps 1–3 km across), resample each to 256² and store them as `.npy` with their relief in metres. Collect 200+ CC0/PD photographs of the same class from Wikimedia Commons categories and NPS galleries, and derive from them a palette of four to six colours plus the sun azimuth and elevation you will use throughout. Decide the quad scale from the DEM: a 256² map covering the same ground as a tile means `TerrainScale.X = Y = ground_extent_UU / 255`, and a Z such that the 16-bit range comfortably exceeds the reference relief with the mean near 32768 ([UDN CreatingTerrain](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/CreatingTerrain.html)).

2. **Build the macro base.** Either take a reference tile directly (FUEL's method) and add seeded detail, or synthesise: fBm with lacunarity 2, gain 0.5, 5–6 octaves for rolling hills followed by `pow(e*1.2, 1.5–2.0)`; Musgrave hybrid multifractal at H 0.25, offset 0.7 for foothills rising to rough peaks; ridged `1-|noise|` with amplitude gated by elevation for alpine crests; terraces `round(e*n)/n` with n 3–6 for mesas; and Olsen's blend of two thirds smoothed diamond-square plus one third Voronoi (c1 = -1, c2 = 1) with a domain warp of magnitude 0.1–0.25 of the map for folded, tectonic-looking shapes ([Red Blob Games](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/terrain-from-noise/); [Musgrave](https://www.classes.cs.uchicago.edu/archive/2015/fall/23700-1/final-project/MusgraveTerrain00.pdf); [Olsen 2004](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf)). Add a smooth uplift map `U` (a Gaussian blob or a painted mask) that defines where the range will be. Record the base's PTRM and geomorphon histogram now so you can see what each later stage buys.

3. **Lay out the valleys with stream power.** Loop 200–1000 steps of: priority-flood fill; D8 receivers; drainage area `A` by accumulating cells in descending height order (one `argsort`); then the Braun–Willett implicit update for n = 1, `h_i = (h_i + dt*U + dt*K*A^m*h_rcv/dx) / (1 + dt*K*A^m/dx)` processed from outlets upward, with m = 0.5 and a hillslope diffusion term `D * laplacian(h)` so relief acquires a scale ([Braun & Willett 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169555X12004618); [Kwang & Parker 2017](https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/5/807/2017/)). K and D are free knobs in normalised units; tune them by the Horton ratios and geomorphon EMD in step 9, not by geological values. Keep `A` and the D8 graph; they are the flow-accumulation mask.

4. **Cut gullies with batched droplets.** Vectorise across droplets per step: 50,000–100,000 droplets, lifetime 30–32, erosion brush radius 2–3, inertia 0.05–0.3, capacity factor 4–8, erode and deposit speeds 0.3 (raise erosion to 0.7 for harsher badlands), evaporation 0.01–0.02, gravity 1–4, min slope 0.0001–0.01 ([SebLague Erosion.cs](https://github.com/SebLague/Hydraulic-Erosion/blob/master/Assets/Scripts/Erosion.cs); [henrikglass/erodr](https://github.com/henrikglass/erodr)). Accumulate a visit-count stream map (decay 0.01 per cycle as in McDonald) and a deposit map; force deposition where slope falls below 2–3° to make flat valley floors matching stream-flow fan slopes, and run a final base-level pass (Gaea's idea) that fills cells below a chosen elevation until slope is 1–3°, or 1–5° at sea level for beaches ([Procedural Hydrology](https://nickmcd.me/2020/04/15/procedural-hydrology/); [Gaea Erosion](https://docs.quadspinner.com/Reference/Erosion/Erosion.html); [Wikipedia, Alluvial fan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alluvial_fan)). Where a high-accumulation channel exits onto low slope, stamp a cone of slope 3–8° with radius growing with upstream area for an alluvial fan.

5. **Slump with thermal erosion under a hardness field.** Define hardness `R(x,y)` as a banded function of height (3–6 bands, hard bands thin, a thick hard cap near the plateau for mesas, optionally on a tilted coordinate for dipping strata) and multiply both droplet erosion and thermal volume by `(1 - R)`-style softness. Run 50 iterations of Olsen's rule with c = 0.5 and a per-cell threshold `T = dx * tan(angle)` where `tan(angle) = R*0.8 + 0.1` (Jákó's Ka and Ki), which lands sand at ~34°, soil and scree at 30–37° and hard rock at effectively cliff angles ([Olsen 2004](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf); [Jákó & Tóth 2011](https://old.cescg.org/CESCG-2011/papers/TUBudapest-Jako-Balazs.pdf); [Wikipedia, Angle of repose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_repose)). Keep the moved-volume map; it is the debris (scree) mask. If you want lakes, replace step 4 with the pipe model using Jákó's full table (dt 0.02, Kr 0.012, Ke 0.015, A 20, Kc 1, Ks 0.5, Kd 1) for ~1000 iterations, lowering Kc and Ks if 256² over-channelises.

6. **Derive the layer masks from the simulation state and write them as the alpha maps.** Compute slope (gradient), curvature (Laplacian), elevation bands and geomorphon class on the same 256² grid, blend with `smoothstep` edges and a little noise to mimic the editor's Noise brush, and write each mask as an RGBA8 alpha of exactly the heightmap's dimensions ([UDN EditingTerrainLayers](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/EditingTerrainLayers.html)). Keep the stack short, four to six layers with clearly separated albedo values (MotorStorm's dark wet, mid dry, pale dust, warm rock) rather than many similar ones, and tile each layer at a different UScale and TextureRotation so pairs do not tile visibly ([UnrealWiki: Terrain](https://beyondunrealwiki.github.io/pages/terrain.html)). The mapping:

| UE2 layer (top of stack = drawn first) | Mask source | Starting threshold | Projection / tiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Layer[0] base colour (macro, non-tiling) | Baked colour map: palette by elevation band and moisture (flow accumulation), with baked sky-accessibility AO and soft sun shadow multiplied in | always opaque | TEXMAPAXIS_XY, UScale so the texture stretches once over the terrain |
| grey detail (modulate, colourless high-pass) | everywhere, mip chain flattened to grey so it fades with distance | opacity 1 | XY, world period 2–4 m (Crysis tiling 0.25–0.5) |
| grass / soil | hydraulic deposit thickness > threshold AND slope < 25°, boosted where geomorphon = valley/hollow/footslope | slope < 25° | XY, different UScale and rotation from detail |
| dirt / wet ground | flow accumulation (or droplet visit count) above the 90th percentile, dilated 1–2 cells; darker albedo | top 5–10 % of cells | XY |
| scree / talus | thermal debris volume > threshold, slope 30–37° | debris > 25th percentile of nonzero | XY |
| rock (cliff) | cumulative wear high AND slope > 35°, hard-band cells regardless of wear | slope > 35° soft, > 55° hard (Crysis cliff rule) | three copies on TEXMAPAXIS_XY, XZ, YZ or one with LayerRotation so it does not stretch |
| sand / beach | elevation within a band around water level AND slope < 5° | band = water ± 2 % of relief | XY |
| snow / bare high | elevation above a band, reduced on slope > 45° | top 15 % of relief | XY |

   Sources for the thresholds: the 55°–90° cliff example and 0.25–0.5 detail tiling ([CryENGINE 3 Manual](https://www.cryengine.com/docs/static/engines/cryengine-3/categories/1114113/pages/15008665); [polycount](https://polycount.com/discussion/127279/confused-about-terrain-textures)); the side-projection rule ([UDN EditingTerrainLayers](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/EditingTerrainLayers.html)); the Wear/Flow/Deposits vocabulary ([Gaea Erosion](https://docs.quadspinner.com/Reference/Erosion/Erosion.html)); talus and fan angles as cited in step 5. The base colour map is the single highest-value item: it is CryEngine's 16k surface texture reduced to one 1024²–2048² texture stretched over the terrain, with colour decided by your palette and the AO and shadow pre-multiplied so even the fixed-function path and the far hills show them.

7. **Place rock meshes and decoration layers by the same rules.** Reproduce CryEngine's vegetation-group filters offline: for each group sample candidate points at a Density spacing (10–50 m in the CE3 tutorial), reject by Slope and Elevation range, random-rotate, scale by Size ± SizeVar (0.1–0.25), and align to the terrain normal ([World of Level Design, Add Vegetation](https://www.worldofleveldesign.com/categories/cryengine-3-sdk/cryengine-3-sdk-08-add-vegetation-foliage-trees.php); [narby.net](https://www.narby.net/?page_id=40)). Boulders and cliff pieces become static-mesh actors on cells where the rock mask is high and slope exceeds 35°, aligned to the slope normal and sunk slightly, using the stock cliff packages or your own meshes tessellated enough for vertex lighting; cover every visibility hole and every stretched steep face with them, as UT2004 mappers did ([UT99.org thread](https://ut99.org/viewtopic.php?t=15108); [Hourences: Vertex Lighting](http://www.hourences.com/tutorials/vtx-lighting.htm)). Tie each prop set to one ground layer (Benson's "appropriate pebbly ground" rule) so layers read as places. Grass, ferns and small debris go into DecoLayers: write the grass mask as the DensityMap (white densest), a ScaleMap with R/G width and B height, and a ColorMap sampled from the base colour map so grass inherits the ground colour (Crysis's "Use Terrain Color"); set `FadeoutRadius` around Min 500 / Max 1200 UU for dense near grass and a sparser second layer that fades much further for distant cover; use `AlignToTerrain` only for rocks and debris; ship three or four grass DecoLayers with different Seeds and pre-rotated mesh variants because Unreal II lacks `RandomYaw` ([UDN CreatingDecoLayers](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/CreatingDecoLayers.html); [UnrealWiki: Terrain Decoration Layer](https://unrealarchive.org/wikis/unreal-wiki/Legacy:Terrain_Decoration_Layer.html); [UnCodex: Unreal II TerrainInfo](https://greatemerald.eu/uncodex/U2/engine/terraininfo.html)). Suppress grass under dense tree groups and on the rock and scree masks. Randomise every prefab compound's sub-elements and rotation; FUEL's identical farmsteads were its most-noticed flaw ([Twenty Sided: Final Thoughts](https://www.shamusyoung.com/twentysidedtale/?p=5356)).

8. **Light and fog from the reference.** One Sunlight at the elevation and azimuth measured from your photos (a mid-morning or mid-afternoon angle, long shadows, never straight down), warm colour from the palette; a second, dim, cool Sunlight from the opposite hemisphere to lift shadowed slopes toward sky colour and give crease darkening at vertex resolution; ZoneInfo ambient low once the suns are in ([UDN AdditionalTerrainTips](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/AdditionalTerrainTips.html); [narby.net](https://www.narby.net/?page_id=39)). Bake sky-accessibility AO (a hemisphere horizon test per cell, 8–16 directions, cheap at 256²) and a blurred terrain self-shadow into the base colour map rather than relying on vertex light alone. Set DistanceFog colour to the skybox's horizon band and the fog start well inside the draw range, so culled sectors and far hills dissolve into the sky exactly as Wenzel's horizon-average rule and UDN's "match the skybox fog color" advice prescribe ([Wenzel 2006](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2006/Chapter6-Real-time%20Atmospheric%20Effects%20in%20Games.pdf); [UDN AdditionalTerrainTips](http://web.archive.org/web/2004id_/http://udn.epicgames.com/Two/AdditionalTerrainTips.html)). Add a slow-panning dark cloud texture as a Projector or a top layer to break shading regularity, and sun-coloured low-alpha dust emitters plus a lens flare if the map is dusty; those are camera and particle effects, not terrain. Put antiportals under major hills and, if the map needs a far horizon, a second coarse TerrainInfo with large quads carrying only the base colour layer ([Ian Chapin](https://www.ianchapin.com/blog/ut2004-maps); [UnrealWiki: Terrain](https://beyondunrealwiki.github.io/pages/terrain.html)).

9. **Close the fidelity loop.** Implement `score_dem(h)` returning: radial PSD slope over k = 4–64 cycles per map; slope histogram (36 bins) and plan/profile curvature histograms with `scipy.stats.wasserstein_distance` to the pooled reference; drainage via priority flood, D8 and accumulation threshold at 0.5–1 % of cells, Strahler order, Horton Rb and Rl by log-linear fit (target 3–5 and 1.6–2.4) and drainage density; hypsometric integral (target 0.3–0.6 for mature hills); geomorphon fractions (search 3, flat 1°), PTRM from the published coefficients and EMD to the reference geomorphon histogram ([Horton laws](https://npg.copernicus.org/articles/21/1007/2014/npg-21-1007-2014.pdf); [opengeology.in](https://opengeology.in/hypsometric-curve-and-integral/); [r.geomorphon](https://grass.osgeo.org/grass-stable/manuals/r.geomorphon.html); [PTRM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04610)). Combine as a weighted sum of normalised distances with geomorphon EMD and Horton terms weighted highest and the spectrum lowest. Sweep K, D, droplet count, capacity, erode and deposit speeds, thermal angles and hardness band count over a handful of seeds, keep settings that move the generated distributions toward the reference class, and log the PTRM of base, post-stream-power, post-droplet and post-thermal so each stage's contribution is visible. Then run `score_look`: render 8–16 fixed views (four azimuths, two elevations, one near-ground) with your actual masks and a fixed sun, render the reference tiles identically, embed with DINOv2-small or CLIP ViT-B/32 on CPU, and compute KID renders-vs-reference-renders (shape) and renders-vs-photos (look), plus the skyline spectral slope and edge-orientation EMD; use LPIPS only to A/B two settings from identical viewpoints ([Rethinking FID](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Jayasumana_Rethinking_FID_Towards_a_Better_Evaluation_Metric_for_Image_Generation_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf); [DINOv2 and FAISS](https://medium.com/aimonks/image-similarity-with-dinov2-and-faiss-741744bc5804); [lpips](https://pypi.org/project/lpips/)). Finish with a small two-alternative test of your own in PTRM's style, even five to ten people on twenty pairs, because every published evaluation ultimately rests on one.

## Conclusion

The surprising alignment in this research is that the 2007-era AAA recipe and the 2003-era modding engine want the same data. CryEngine's painter gates layers by slope and altitude, bakes AO and soft shadow into a colour map, scatters rocks and grass by density-slope-elevation rules and matches fog to the horizon; UE2 forces alpha maps onto the heightmap grid, lights per vertex (blurry by construction), scatters DecoLayers from density textures and culls with a colour-matched DistanceFog. A generator that treats its erosion simulation's state as the source of every mask is not approximating Crysis in UE2 so much as doing what Crytek's artists did by hand, offline, from a physically motivated signal. The one thing none of the four games needed, because their large forms came from DEMs, Google Earth or sculptors, is the thing your pipeline must supply: valleys, ridges and hollows in the proportions real hills have, which stream power, droplets and banded thermal slumping produce and PTRM and Horton ratios can measure.

The practical implication is a shift in where tuning effort goes. Spectra, texture resolution and layer count are nearly irrelevant to perceived realism; landform statistics, albedo separation between a few layers, a believable palette from one real place at one hour, and the quietness of the ground under saturated placed objects are what viewers respond to. Build the scoring loop before polishing the simulation, log the realism score after each stage, and expect the biggest jump to come from the first drainage network and the second from the first baked colour map, not from any later refinement of the noise.
