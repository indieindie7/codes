# Displace volume, flag faces, sleep tiles: upgrading game shallow water

Hydrophobia's solver core (explicit HLL finite volume, hydrostatic reconstruction, CFL 0.5 sub-steps) was already the right family, but every component around that core has a documented, cheap upgrade, and the three with the highest payoff are not the solver at all. First, bodies must inject *displaced volume*, not just velocity: every real-time height-field paper from Thürey 2007 to Chentanez & Müller 2010 stamps the per-column water volume a body replaces and distributes its per-step change Δb to the neighbours, which is what produces bow waves and wakes; velocity stamping alone mostly drags water along ([Thürey et al. 2007](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/breakingWaves.pdf); [Chentanez & Müller 2010](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)). Second, walls belong on *faces* (edges between cells), not cells: a per-face reflecting flag or a per-face "conveyance porosity" ψ lets a thin wall, chair leg or door jamb block flow between two mostly-wet cells, which a per-cell flag cannot express ([Özgen et al. 2017](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/1482522)). Third, the Kurganov–Petrova 2007 package (MUSCL reconstruction of the surface w = h + B with the bed-clamp, the branch-free desingularised velocity, and `hu := h·u`) removes wet/dry front noise and thin-film racing on stairs for about twice the flux cost and no new state ([Kurganov & Petrova 2007](https://people.tamu.edu/~gpetrova//KPSV.pdf)). Shipped games since 2011 did not follow Hydrophobia: Sea of Thieves, God of War, Still Wakes the Deep and the Frostbite prototype all run a GPU or SIMD height field in a texture around the player, inject bodies from a depth capture or capsules, and read heights back at pontoon points for buoyancy, within a 1–4 ms budget ([Ang et al. 2018](https://history.siggraph.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/2018-Talks-Ang_The-Technical-Art-of-Sea-of-Thieves.pdf); [Ottosson thesis](https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/water-interaction-ottosson-bjorn.pdf)). For performance, a 128² game-quality SWE step costs 0.75 ms on a 2010 GPU and GPU throughput reaches 530 Mcells/s, but at Hydrophobia's ~1200 cells per room launch overhead dominates and the CPU remains the right place; the win there is tile sleeping with a wake-neighbours rule and SSE→AVX2 widening ([Chentanez & Müller 2010](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf); [Brodtkorb et al. 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_kp_on_gpus.pdf)). For a CPU blood-pool sim in a Direct3D-9-era mod, the same conclusions shrink to: KP07 positivity tricks, face flags rasterised by Bresenham, Thürey Δb stamping from feet and gibs, dt-normalised multiplicative damping, and one explicit step per frame, because blood millimetres deep has a wave speed under 0.3 m/s and never trips CFL on 10–20 cm cells.

A note on provenance: the description of Hydrophobia's internals (HLL, Audusse reconstruction, Toro wave speeds, SSE, Havok buoyancy, ~1200-cell sheets) comes from the reader's own reverse-engineering of the game, not from any public source; the developers deliberately withheld their method and the studio closed in 2012 ([HookedGamers interview](https://www.hookedgamers.com/features/2010/09/28/rob_hewson_about_hydrophobia.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_Energy_Digital)). All comparisons below treat that description as given.

## Shipped games converged on a texture-space height field around the player

Across every title with a public disclosure, the shipped pattern is a 2D height-field simulation (shallow water or wave equation) in a GPU texture covering only the area near the camera, with obstacles from a baked or rendered mask and bodies injected either as a depth capture or as analytic capsules. Rare's Sea of Thieves uses Tessendorf FFT for the open ocean but, for "water splashing on the deck of a ship", a **GPU surface simulation based on Mei et al. 2007's virtual-pipes model**, and for waterfalls and streams it projects **the camera's depth buffer onto the surface mesh in the simulation's texture space**, so a character standing in a stream gets foam at their feet; foam around intersecting objects uses a feedback buffer that disperses ([Ang et al., SIGGRAPH 2018](https://history.siggraph.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/2018-Talks-Ang_The-Technical-Art-of-Sea-of-Thieves.pdf)). Santa Monica's rendering lead confirms God of War (2018) and Ragnarök render "water waves and ripples ... with a similar technique as the old snow system", that is, a deformation height field written by **carving shapes attached to anything in the world** ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/santa-monica-s-senior-programmer-on-how-god-of-war-ragnar-k-s-snow-system-was-made)). The Chinese Room's Still Wakes the Deep (UE5, 2024) is the closest to Hydrophobia's gameplay goal and is the most explicit: a **2D shallow-water Niagara sim**, capsule "pumps" that inject velocity attached to the protagonist's arms, hands and feet, **wall and floor meshes tagged and baked into a heightmap texture** used as flow boundaries so water bounces off them, and **GPU readback** of flow direction, strength and height at query points to drive buoyancy and flow forces on objects ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/learn-how-still-wakes-the-deep-used-unreal-engine-5-to-create-water-mechanics)).

The one CPU-SIMD design with published numbers is Björn Ottosson's Frostbite prototype at DICE: linear wave theory on a Laplacian pyramid of sub-grids, 4-wide SIMD, jobs split into "a simulation step and a border copy step" sized for the PS3 SPU's 256 kB local store, **3 ms per step on one Xeon core**, and a measured table of 8 connected pyramids costing **6.5 ms at 128², 2.1 ms at 64², 0.9 ms at 32², 0.5 ms at 16²**, with a 52-pyramid LOD configuration at 3.1 ms sim plus 0.7 ms render ([Ottosson, EA-hosted thesis](https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/water-interaction-ottosson-bjorn.pdf)). Its interaction model is the Archimedes one: "When an object is pulled out of water the water height is decreased and when pushed down height is increased", with the intersection approximated by an ellipse and band-pass filtered so each sub-grid receives only its wavelengths; bottom height comes from vertical ray casts against static geometry, and dry cells are handled by diffusion rather than Tessendorf's zero-displacement rigid boundary because the latter "generates reflected waves ... completely unphysical" (same thesis). The simulation runs client-side only because a server-side version "would have had substantially reduced resolution": client objects both generate and respond to waves, server-mirrored objects only generate waves and float at rest height (same thesis). That split is directly relevant to any networked mod.

Naughty Dog's Uncharted 3 and 4 took a different fork: **Wave Particles** (Yuksel 2007), Lagrangian packets splatted into a height field on the GPU, "unconditionally stable", with one boat at 170 fps and 1,681 boats at 4.8 fps on a GeForce 7900 ([Yuksel](https://www.cemyuksel.com/research/waveparticles/); [attendee notes](https://blog.dsmu.me/2012/03/water-technology-of-uncharted.html)). Ubisoft's Assassin's Creed III shows how ships are coupled without any solver at all: "a bunch of giant buoyant spheres", hull sample points for drag, "depth rods/probes along gunwales" that trigger bow spray, stern wakes as "level particles", and coastal foam density baked from terrain at 512² (8 m precision on a 4 km map) ([fxguide](https://www.fxguide.com/fxfeatured/assassins-creed-iii-the-tech-behind-or-beneath-the-action/)). Unreal's own Water Buoyancy Component is the same idea productised: spheres (pontoons) as "a simplistic volumetric approximation of the object" with buoyancy coefficient, damping and drag coefficients ([Epic docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/water-buoyancy-component-in-unreal-engine)). Crest, the most documented open implementation, runs a dynamic wave sim per LOD cascade at a default **60 updates/s, damping 0.05, Courant number 0.7**, with sphere inputs that account for submersion and an ObjectWaterInteraction script that distributes force across LODs "to prevent over-application" ([Crest docs](https://crest.readthedocs.io/en/4.10/user/ocean-simulation.html)).

What this means for the Hydrophobia question is sobering and useful at once. Nobody else shipped flooding, draining rooms with a conserving finite-volume solver; everyone else shipped ripples and wakes on a fixed water plane. The 1–4 ms budgets reported (Ottosson's 3.1 ms, Elliot Gray's ~0.7 ms CPU per water body in UE, nobody above 6.5 ms) suggest the practical ceiling studios accept ([80.lv Gray](https://80.lv/articles/unified-interactive-water-system-for-ue)). Hydrophobia's solver is more capable than any of these; the shipped titles are better at the *injection and query* layer (depth capture, capsules, readback at probe points), and that is where the first upgrades should go.

## Sub-cell geometry wants face flags and porosity, not a finer grid

Hydrophobia's per-cell reflecting flag is the degenerate case of a family the flood-modelling literature has worked out in full. Guinot & Soares-Frazão's single-porosity shallow water replaces the plain equations with ∂(φq)/∂t + ∂(φf)/∂x + ∂(φg)/∂y = φ s_U + s_φ, where **φ is the fraction of the cell not occupied by structures** and s_φ = (0, g h²/2 ∂φ/∂x, g h²/2 ∂φ/∂y) is the momentum source from porosity gradients; the wave speeds stay u ± √(gh), so nothing about CFL changes ([Özgen et al. 2017](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/1482522)). The step that matters for games is Sanders et al. 2008's *integral* porosity, which adds a **conveyance porosity ψ per cell edge**, "the fraction of a cell boundary contributing to mass and momentum exchange", and whose discrete update is just the finite-volume edge sum with each flux multiplied by ψ_j: ∂(φq)/∂t = −Σ_j ψ_j (F·n)_j l_j + s ([Sanders et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhydrol.2008.08.009); [Özgen et al. 2017 eq. 18](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/1482522)). Özgen's stated reason to prefer it is precisely the game problem: the single-porosity model has an "inability to represent directionality and blocking effects", whereas an edge porosity of zero blocks flow across a wall even when both adjacent cells are mostly wet. Both porosities are computed by integrating a binary phase function over the cell (φ) and over the edge (ψ), which in practice means supersampling the room's collision geometry or SDF once at load. Building drag enters as an extra quadratic term alongside bed friction, s_f = −(c_D^f + c_D^b)‖v‖v, which is how furniture-dense cells slow the flow without being walls ([Özgen et al. 2017 eq. 6](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/1482522)). A 2026 arXiv paper generalises the idea to second order and notes that the CFL condition "is primarily determined by the coarse mesh", which is the whole point: sub-cell walls at coarse-cell cost ([arXiv 2601.01895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.01895)). No graphics paper found uses porosity SWE; it has not crossed into games.

The geometrically exact alternative is cut cells, where "solid objects are cut out of the background mesh leaving a set of irregularly shaped cells fitted to the boundary", handling "arbitrarily complex geometries and moving boundary elements" ([Causon, Ingram, Mingham](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0309170801000100)). Ryoo & Mandli model a sea wall as "a zero-width embedded boundary element that acts as a flux interface" that blocks or admits waves depending on its height, with results "virtually the same as resolving the barrier" ([arXiv 2112.09696](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.09696)). The cost is the small-cell problem: cut cells "can get arbitrarily small", which "causes strict time step restriction via CFL", and the fix (State Redistribution, merging small-cell states with a neighbour after each step) is what let them cut time steps **fivefold** versus refining at the barrier (same paper). For a game, cutting cell areas is overkill; the pragmatic middle is to rasterise walls into the two face-flag sets with Bresenham, and to reach for porosity fractions only where a cell is partially blocked by furniture.

The graphics practice that already works is Chentanez & Müller's staggered grid with velocities on faces: a face marked reflective has its velocity "always set to 0 at the end of every time step and is not updated in the advection step", and an automatic rule makes face (i+½, j) a wall whenever **h_{i,j} ≤ ε and H_{i,j} > η_{i+1,j}** (or the mirror case), ε = 10⁻⁴Δx, so "the water level in a wet cell needs to be higher than the terrain level in the neighboring dry cell before the flow starts" ([Chentanez & Müller 2010 §2.1.4](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)). That one rule is what makes stairs behave: a staircase is just a bed field B with steps smaller than a cell, and any well-balanced scheme (hydrostatic reconstruction, which Hydrophobia already has) keeps water at rest on it. Large drops are different: a face becomes a **waterfall** when (H_{i,j} − H_{i+1,j})/Δx > ∇H_cap with ∇H_cap = 3, the lower cell treats it as reflecting, and the volume that would cross is carried by splash particles seeded in the box between the upper bed and the lower surface, which the authors note "also makes the simulation more stable because steep terrain slopes cause numerical instabilities" (same paper §2.4.3). Ledges, tub rims and balcony edges in a flooded building are exactly this case. Müller's GDC 2008 slides give the two trivial boundary conditions for anything else: the clamped ghost column u[−1,j] = u[0,j] reflects, and the open-boundary update g_new = (cΔt·u + g·h)/(h + cΔt) lets waves of speed c leave ([Müller GDC 2008](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc08/slides/S6509i1.pdf)). For curved walls specifically, two further tools exist: Wave Particles reflect analytically with the dispersion angle changed by the boundary's curvature, with reflection times precomputed into an event table ([Yuksel 2007](https://www.cemyuksel.com/research/waveparticles/)), and for procedural waves Jeschke et al. bound each surface point's trajectory inside an ellipsoidal "wave cage" with a tanh soft clip (β = 0.5) so "the water surface never penetrates the sea floor" ([Jeschke et al. 2020](https://pub.ista.ac.at/~chafner/JeschkeWaveCages.pdf)). A room SDF is still worth building even with face flags, because it is the cheapest way to compute φ and ψ, to clamp foam and particle advection, and to feed the velocity-injecting capsules described next.

## Bodies must displace volume; buoyancy must be smooth at the surface

The single most consequential finding for Hydrophobia's "stamp object velocity within a radius" is that it is only half of what the papers do. Müller's GDC 2008 slides put it bluntly: "Object pushes columns beneath it down. Add the removed water in the vicinity!", and a naive push leaves "a hole ... above the body. Non-physical" for submerged bodies; the fix is a per-column state r[i,j] of water replaced by solids, with **Δr = r_t − r_{t−1} distributed as water to the neighbouring columns** ([Müller GDC 2008](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc08/slides/S6509i1.pdf)). Thürey et al. formalise the same thing for SWE: store b(x), the column length covered by rigid bodies from a top-down projection, and update h(x′, t+1) = h(x′, t) + α·Δb(x)/4 over the four neighbours; "The scheme conserves volume even for 0 < α < 1. By changing α the amplitudes of waves generated by bodies can be adjusted", and positive and negative Δb "give rise to both, the bow wave in front and the wake at the back of a body that is dragged through the water" ([Thürey et al. 2007 §7](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/breakingWaves.pdf)). This costs one float per cell and a column rasterisation per body.

Chentanez & Müller's Algorithm 2 is the full version and supplies every constant: subdivide body triangles until area < Δx²; **num_substeps = max(1, ⌊|v − v_y ŷ|Δt/Δx + 0.5⌋)** so fast bodies sweep every cell on their path instead of tunnelling; V_disp = n·v_rel A Δt; at each substep point with depth = η − p_y > 0, decay = e^{−β·depth}, **h += decay·V_disp·C_dis/(num_substeps·Δx²)**, and the face velocity is *blended* toward the body velocity, u += coeff(v_x − u), with **coeff = min(1, decay·C_adapt·sign·(depth·Δt/η)·A/Δx²)**, β = 1, C_dis = 1.0, C_adapt = 0.2 ([Chentanez & Müller 2010 §2.3.2](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)). Hydrophobia's hard stamp is the coeff = 1 case; the cap is a stability device that prevents a fast kinematic body from injecting more momentum than one step of fluid can absorb. The authors admit the coupling "do[es] not" conserve volume, which is the hidden cause of slow water-level drift in long sessions; Müller's fix is to sum V and V_new and "Distribute V − V_new evenly within connected regions" ([Müller GDC 2008](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc08/slides/S6509i1.pdf)). Cords & Staadt give the cheapest bow/stern wake for boats and, by extension, wading characters: rasterise the hull/surface intersection S_t each frame and use **bow = S_t \ S_{t−1}, body = S_t ∩ S_{t−1}, stern = S_{t−1} \ S_t** as wave sources, plus engine wakes as circular generators and a diffusing foam map f ← d·f + (1−d)/4·Σ neighbours ([Cords & Staadt 2009](http://hilkocords.de/publications/open_water.pdf)). Wave Particles handle the submerged case by routing displaced volume "to the nearest points that have a direct connection to the fluid surface" and spawning a negative wave to cancel instead of editing existing particles ([Yuksel 2007 §4.1](https://www.cemyuksel.com/research/waveparticles/)).

The water → body direction is Archimedes everywhere, and the differences are in how the surface discontinuity is smoothed. Müller: each column under the object applies f = −ρ Δu h² g (h the column width) ([GDC 2008](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc08/slides/S6509i1.pdf)). Thürey: per covered node F = ρ h b u(x) + ρ g Δx Δy b(x), the first term the fluid's momentum flux, so boxes "are dragged along with the fluid, or float on the surface ... The simulation remains stable even during quick movements" ([Thürey 2007 eq. 11](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/breakingWaves.pdf)). Chentanez & Müller per sub-triangle: f = ρ g A (η(p) − p_y) n_y ŷ with signed prisms, **drag −½ ρ C_D A_eff |v_rel| v_rel and lift −½ ρ C_L A_eff |v_rel| (v_rel × n × v_rel)/|n × v_rel|**, where v_rel subtracts the fluid velocity [u, u ∂η/∂x + w ∂η/∂z, w] and A_eff = (α n·v_rel/|v_rel| + (1−α))A with user α ([hfFluid §2.3.1](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)). Yuksel used α = 1 and notes "Smaller values can be used to dampen object motion" ([Yuksel 2007](https://www.cemyuksel.com/research/waveparticles/)). The stability lever that matters most is Cords & Staadt's: "the discontinuity of buoyancy at the free surface can lead to instantaneous force changes if a particle is passing the free surface", so **buoyancy is scaled linearly to zero over a threshold distance d_t below the surface**, making "rigid-body movement ... smooth — even for sparsely-sampled objects" ([Cords & Staadt §4.1.1](http://hilkocords.de/publications/open_water.pdf)). Game practice adds damping "proportional to submerged area ratio", F_damp ∝ v × (submerged_area/total_area), linear or quadratic, and cuts hull triangles by the surface so each produces "0, 1, or 2 fully submerged triangles" with hydrostatic pressure from centroid depth ([Game Developer boat model](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/water-interaction-model-for-boats-in-video-games-part-2)). A snippet attributed to Kellomäki 2014 states the symptom and cure in one line: buoyancy on a heavy body "causes small vibrations in most rigid body solvers", whereas "With critical damping, the forces are strong enough to prevent oscillations altogether" ([Kellomäki 2014, abstract page](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2014/580154); full text not verified).

None of these systems iterate to convergence; all are sequential water step → body step → couple, which is why they need stability levers instead of a monolithic solve. No paper formally analyses the explicit feedback loop (body pushes water down → η drops → buoyancy drops → body sinks → water rebounds → body pops). The three remedies that work empirically are bilinear sampling of η over a footprint rather than a point, the Cords d_t ramp so the force is C⁰ in height, and quadratic drag against a relative velocity that includes the fluid's own motion. Cords & Staadt also name the cost that still bites GPU designs: "the memory read back from GPU ... the local area surrounding each object has to be read back" because rigid bodies live on the CPU; in their frames rigid bodies took 1.3–12.3%, liquid and foam 23–36%, and surface extraction and rendering 63–74% ([Cords & Staadt Tables 1–2](http://hilkocords.de/publications/open_water.pdf)). Chentanez & Müller kept coupling on the CPU even in their GTX 480 build, at 2.3 ms for the boat scene ([hfFluid Table 1](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)). Hydrophobia's CPU-resident design sidesteps the readback problem entirely, which is a real argument for keeping small gameplay-coupled sheets on the CPU.

## Splash, foam and breaking criteria read straight off the height field

The shallow-water equations only deliver wakes and bow waves; everything that sells the shot is particles spawned by thresholds on h and η, and Chentanez & Müller supply the most reusable set. A cell breaks and spawns splash when all three hold: **|∇η| > α_minSplash·gΔt/Δx** (steep enough), **(h − h_prev)/Δt > v_minSplash** (rising fast, front of a wave), and **∇²η < l_minSplash** (a crest), with **α_minSplash = 0.45, v_minSplash = 4, l_minSplash = −4**, the gradient taken as the max-magnitude one-sided difference and the Laplacian the 5-point stencil; condition two "we found ... to be more robust than" Thürey's ∇η·u < 0 ([hfFluid §2.4.2](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)). The threshold scales with gΔt/Δx, so it survives a change of cell size. Particles seed in a rectangle perpendicular to the gradient with volume ∝ |∇η|, horizontal speed −(∇η/|∇η|)√(g·min(h, h_avgmax)), vertical α_y (h − h_prev)/Δt with α_y = 0.1; each particle of radius r removes and later redeposits V_eff = C_deposit·(4/3)πr³ with **C_deposit ∈ [1, 10]** controlling how many particles exist, and on return the face velocity is mass-averaged u ← (u h Δx² + v_par V_eff)/(h Δx² + V_eff) (same paper §2.4.1). Body splashes come from triangles sweeping the surface faster than v_thres, with particle velocity v_par = v_fluid + α_rn v_rn + α_p v_rp + α_n n_s|v_rel·ŷ| + α_r(v_rel − 2(ŷ·v_rel)ŷ), **α_rn = 0.86, α_p ∈ [0.10, 0.23], α_n ∈ [0, 0.01], α_r ∈ [0.17, 0.61]**, count ∝ |v_s| − v_thres (same paper §2.4.4); a cheaper proxy for debris is "cell depth rose by more than v_thres·Δt under a body footprint this step". Foam is spawned probabilistically on splash impact and "advected by the velocity field of the fluid simulation and projected onto the fluid surface" with a noisy lifetime (same paper), which is exactly the horizontal velocity the wave equation lacks: Chentanez notes wave-equation and pipe models "only work with a vertical velocity field", so "whirlpools or the flow of a river cannot be treated correctly" and vortices "are not present in the model" ([hfFluid §1](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)). That is the strongest argument for keeping Hydrophobia's full SWE rather than regressing to a ripple solver.

Thürey's breaking-wave detector is the alternative for large fronts: points where **|∇H| > p_H gΔt/Δx with p_H = ¼ and ∇H·u < 0**, broadened by 2Δx, flood-filled, fitted with a line along the tangent and extruded into a connected particle sheet with velocity u_s = (1 + p_v g (H − H_i)) u_l, which on landing dents the height field by p_m with p_m/8 pushed to the eight neighbours ([Thürey 2007 §4–5](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/breakingWaves.pdf)). Its authors are honest that it "doesn't properly handle cases with chaotic waves ... many small splashes or drops", so for a flooded interior the Chentanez per-cell criterion is the better fit. For sub-grid shimmer without refining the solver, Chentanez & Müller advect three sets of texture coordinates for an FFT ripple texture by the SWE velocity, resetting every **τ ≈ 3 s** with phase τ/3, and weight each by ω = e^{−μγ} (μ ∈ [0.5, 2], γ the largest Green-strain eigenvalue) so stretched ripples fade, and they displace a render grid at **Δx/4** with it ([hfFluid §2.5.2](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)). Ottosson's equivalent is a per-cell wave energy E and direction d "exposed in a pixel shader to generate the actual details" ([Ottosson thesis](https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/water-interaction-ottosson-bjorn.pdf)). For large open water with no global current, Wave Particles remain the right tool, with the caveat Cords & Staadt record: an object hitting the surface "would cause a radially propagating wave front with a flat surface inside" and "diffraction cannot be represented using particles" ([Cords & Staadt §6](http://hilkocords.de/publications/open_water.pdf)); Yuksel's own breaking cue is amplitude above one-seventh of wavelength ([Yuksel §7](https://www.cemyuksel.com/research/waveparticles/)). Chentanez, Müller & Kim later coupled a 3D grid/particle region to an outer SWE field "such that waves travel naturally across the border" for breaches and impacts ([TVCG 2015](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hybridsim_preprinted.pdf)).

## Second-order well-balanced reconstruction fixes the thin-film and wet/dry noise

Hydrophobia's first-order HLL with Audusse hydrostatic reconstruction is well-balanced (lake at rest on stairs) but first-order schemes are diffusive and show "thin film racing" on slopes, and all explicit schemes misbehave at wet/dry fronts. Kurganov & Petrova's 2007 scheme is the canonical answer and is simultaneously well-balanced and positivity-preserving, which earlier central-upwind versions "fail to satisfy ... simultaneously"; the trick "heavily relies on the fact that we replace the original bottom function B with its continuous piecewise linear (bilinear in 2-D) approximation" ([KP07](https://people.tamu.edu/~gpetrova//KPSV.pdf)). The recipe is compact. Reconstruct **w = h + B, not h**, with the generalised minmod limiter (U_x)_j = minmod(θ(Ū_j − Ū_{j−1})/Δx, (Ū_{j+1} − Ū_{j−1})/(2Δx), θ(Ū_{j+1} − Ū_j)/Δx), **θ ∈ [1, 2]**, larger θ "less dissipative but ... more oscillatory". Correct only w where it would dip below the bed: if w⁻_{j+½} < B_{j+½} set w⁻_{j+½} = B_{j+½} and w⁺_{j−½} = 2w̄_j − B_{j+½} (mirror case likewise), which "will remain conservative and will stay above the piecewise linear approximant of the bottom". Desingularise the velocity as **u = √2 h (hu)/√(h⁴ + max(h⁴, ε))**, ε = Δx⁴ in the paper, and then **recompute hu := h·u**, because otherwise "the scheme may produce negative values of h"; the simpler u = h(hu)/(h² + ε) "may lead to huge values of u and thus to dramatic slowdown". Take one-sided speeds a⁺ = max(u⁺ + √(gh⁺), u⁻ + √(gh⁻), 0), a⁻ = min(u⁺ − √(gh⁺), u⁻ − √(gh⁻), 0), keep **λa ≤ ½** for the positivity proof, and use an SSP Runge–Kutta, which keeps positivity because it is "a convex combination of several forward Euler steps" (same paper). Hydrophobia's HLL flux can stay; central-upwind and HLL differ only in the a± weighting. The midpoint rule it uses is not SSP; Heun (SSP-RK2) is the drop-in replacement. Brodtkorb found that first-order Euler instead of RK2 "gives minimal changes" in Malpasset results while running measurably faster ([Brodtkorb et al. 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_kp_on_gpus.pdf)), so the second-order *spatial* reconstruction is where the quality lives, not the time integrator.

The practical ε is not Δx⁴. Brodtkorb's group found KP's value "insufficient for many real-world applications" and use **ε = K0·max{1, min{Δx, Δy}} with K0 = 10⁻² in single precision**, so a 15 m cell desingularises below 15 cm of depth, with Malpasset needing 40 cm; round-off also makes tiny depths negative, so they set "water depths less than ε_m to zero, where ε_m is close to machine precision" ([Brodtkorb et al. 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_kp_on_gpus.pdf)). Two failure modes survive even KP07. The wet/dry slope correction "violates the well-balancedness of the scheme along the wet-dry interface, causing small initial waves emerging from these cells", and KP07 "suffers from incorrect high velocities along the wet/dry boundaries" so that water "climbs up on the shores" and "if a cell becomes wet, it will almost never be completely dry again", which also shrinks the CFL step; Horváth, Waser & Perdigão's 2014 reconstruction with a "draining time step technique" fixes both ([Horváth et al. 2016](http://drivenbynostalgia.com/files/keplershuffle/paper.pdf)). For a game that wants puddles to visibly dry, the lesson is to add an explicit sink and sleep tiles once depth is below ε everywhere. Brodtkorb also gives the precision rule that matters for Hydrophobia-size offsets: "it is often tempting to store the water elevation in memory. This, however, will give rise to large relative errors when the water depth is small compared to the elevation", so store h and keep grids in local coordinates ([CMWR 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_saetra_cmwr_2012.pdf)).

The graphics family's stabilisers are the safety net to use *after* adopting KP, not instead of it. Chentanez & Müller clamp **|u|, |w| < αΔx/Δt with α = 0.5**, which "places a limit on the wave speed ... but greatly improves the stability for scenes with violent water", cap the depth used in height integration by h_adj = max(0, (Σ four neighbours h̄)/4 − h_avgmax) with **h_avgmax = βΔx/(gΔt), β = 2**, use the *upwind* depth h̄ on each face (which "guarantees mass conservation" and is more stable than averaging), and damp overshoot at fronts entering shallows: if η_i − η_{i−1} > λ_edge and η_i > η_{i+1}, h_i += α_edge(max(0, ½(h_i + h_{i+1})) − h_i), **λ_edge = 2Δx, 0.1 < α_edge < 0.5** ([hfFluid §2.1.5, §2.2](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)). They also reject implicit SWE for games because it "either require[s] an iterative method which incurs a significant run time cost or need[s] to pre-factorize the matrix which means boundary conditions cannot be changed", and their honest summary is that the clamps "cannot guarantee unconditional stability. However, they allow us to explicitly integrate with a reasonable time step in all of our examples" (same paper). The exception worth keeping in mind for very viscous liquids is Kass & Miller's linearised wave equation with c² = g d solved by alternating-direction implicit tridiagonal sweeps where "A single iteration per frame suffices in most cases", with Bridson's caveat that it "can cause weird grid artifacts if dt too big" and needs care as depth goes to zero ([Kass & Miller 1990](https://ohiostate.pressbooks.pub/app/uploads/sites/45/2017/09/kass-miller.pdf); [Bridson notes](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rbridson/courses/533b-winter-2004/cs533b_slides_mar11.pdf)). The pipe model (Mei et al. 2007) is trivially positive via its outflow scaling K = min(1, d·l_x l_y/((Σf)Δt)) but its authors concede that scaling "might also introduce instability" because neighbours' fluxes are not adjusted, and it has no inertia or velocity diffusion, so it cannot streak or swirl ([Mei et al. 2007](http://www-evasion.inrialpes.fr/Publications/2007/MDH07/FastErosion_PG07.pdf); [lisyarus](https://lisyarus.github.io/blog/posts/simulating-water-over-terrain.html)). Kallin's quadtree experiments add the warning that "central differences resulted in high frequency noise and undampened oscillations" and that explicit parameters stable on a uniform grid "can produce instabilities ... in the boundaries between high and low resolution" ([Kallin 2008](https://ep.liu.se/ecp/034/010/ecp083410.pdf)).

## GPU wins above 256² cells, tile sleeping wins everywhere, and 1200-cell sheets stay on the CPU

Measured GPU numbers set the scale. A single-GPU Kurganov–Petrova implementation reaches **530 megacells per second** on a GTX 480 in single precision, with 16×12 thread blocks, shared memory padded to width 33 against bank conflicts, and flux and source terms packed to cut memory footprint by 31%; four Fermi GPUs sustain over **1.2 gigacells/s** on 235-million-cell domains ([Brodtkorb et al. 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_kp_on_gpus.pdf); [SINTEF](https://www.sintef.no/en/publications/publication/?pubid=CRIStin+888971)). Horváth's Kepler-shuffle variant cut per-block shared memory from 19,584 to 4,352 bytes by exchanging apron values through warp shuffles, taking occupancy from 25% to near 100% ([Horváth et al. 2016](http://drivenbynostalgia.com/files/keplershuffle/paper.pdf)). On the game side, Chentanez & Müller's complete height-field step (advection, integration, PML, overshoot reduction, FFT texture advection) costs **0.75 ms at 128², 1.28 ms at 256², 2.19 ms for 900×135** on a GTX 480, whereas the same boat grid on a Core i7 took 42.25 ms single-threaded and 19.06 ms on four threads, a 2.2× thread scaling and a 9–19× GPU advantage for a 121k-cell grid ([hfFluid Tables 1–2](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)). The important inference is that 0.75 ms for a 16k-cell grid is launch and synchronisation overhead, not arithmetic: roughly six kernel launches per step dominate, so small grids on the GPU want to be atlased into one dispatch, and a 1200-cell room sheet is far below the point where the GPU helps. Brodtkorb's own caution applies: the theoretical CPU/GPU gap "currently lies at around 7 times" and "speedup claims of hundreds or more require a thorough explanation" ([CMWR 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_saetra_cmwr_2012.pdf)).

On the CPU the published gains are from layout and vectorisation. hms++ reports that "a cache-blocking structure for the time marching loop, and vectorisation of flux computations, leads to a speedup of around 30x" over naive scalar C++ ([hms++](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352711023000936)); the TUM teaching code vectorises its Riemann solvers with `#pragma omp simd` and `declare simd` ([TUM-I5/SWE](https://github.com/TUM-I5/SWE)). Everything about KP07 is branch-free: the desingularised velocity is pure arithmetic, Brodtkorb's wet/dry threshold is a max/select, and Chentanez's face-wall predicate becomes a mask that zeroes the face velocity, so Hydrophobia's SSE select-by-mask style maps one-to-one onto AVX2 `blendv` or AVX-512 k-masks; a 128² sheet in three SoA planes is 192 KB in float and sits in L2 on any modern core, so cache blocking only matters above roughly 512² and a ~1200-cell sheet lives in L1. A back-of-envelope estimate (not measured) puts one AVX2 core at 50–150 Mcells/s for a first-order step, so a 128² pool at 2–4 sub-steps costs on the order of 0.3–1 ms and a 32² pool 20–60 µs. Ottosson's measured 3 ms per step on one Xeon core with 4-wide SIMD for a pyramid system is the only hard CPU-SIMD data point from a game context ([Ottosson thesis](https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/water-interaction-ottosson-bjorn.pdf)).

Sparse work is the bigger lever than raw throughput, and the rule is documented. Brodtkorb's early exit: the integration kernel reduces a per-block wet flag; the flux kernel skips a block when "the block and its four closest neighbouring blocks are dry"; "The presence of one single wet cell in a block is sufficient to consider the whole block as wet"; and the same flag marks blocks where "the source terms perfectly balance the fluxes (R = 0) as 'dry'", that is, **still water sleeps**. When the bookkeeping costs more than it saves they "swap over to using the regular kernel" and re-probe "every hundredth timestep" ([Brodtkorb et al. 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_kp_on_gpus.pdf)). Horváth's wake rule is the one to copy: "If a block is active, its neighbors could be flooded in the next time step, thus, all adjacent blocks are activated, too", blocks containing sources are force-activated, and active indices are compacted into a work list ([Horváth et al. 2016](http://drivenbynostalgia.com/files/keplershuffle/paper.pdf)). It is safe because "the compact stencil ensures that water will travel at most one cell per time step" ([CMWR 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_saetra_cmwr_2012.pdf)). Sætra's sparse-compute variant sorts a buffer of block indices so wet ones come first and launches only those ([Sætra, ENUMATH 2011](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-33134-3_71)). Kallin found "no practical performance hit of having inactive (dry) areas in the grid", so grids may cover whole environments ([Kallin 2008](https://ep.liu.se/ecp/034/010/ecp083410.pdf)). Hydrophobia's wet bounding box is the one-tile-list degenerate case; a tile list with `wet` and `active` bitmasks handles many disjoint puddles better than one box.

Adaptive resolution has a proven structure but a known trap. Sætra, Brodtkorb & Lie refine per tile when a fraction of cells meet the criterion, interpolate ghost values in space-time from the parent, step children only after parents are a step ahead, and run a flux-correction kernel that "maintains conservation of mass" by replacing coarse interface fluxes with the fine-side sum; they warn that "padding of new refined areas is necessary" so waves have room before hitting a patch border, and that maximum eigenvalues "increase with increasing resolution", shrinking fine-level steps ([Sætra et al. 2015](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/saetra_brodtkorb_lie_amr.pdf)). Local time stepping on block-uniform quadtrees adds a further 2.8× ([Dazzi et al. 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0309170817304311)). For a game the cheaper route is Müller's "Physics low-res, appearance hi-res (shader effects)" and a moving high-resolution window: Chentanez & Müller shift their grid "in whole multiple of grid spacing dx to be approximately centered around" the object of interest, and NVIDIA's 2004 DX9 sample already paired a tiling far-field texture with a "local detail" texture "free to move around to follow the character" ([hfFluid](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf); [NVIDIA WaterInteraction](https://download.nvidia.com/developer/SDK/Individual_Samples/DEMOS/Direct3D9/src/WaterInteraction/docs/WaterInteraction.pdf)).

Time stepping in a game should be bounded, not adaptive. Brodtkorb and Horváth both run **CFL = 0.25** in 2D with dt from a parallel max reduction; KP's positivity proof needs ½ in 1D ([Brodtkorb et al. 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_kp_on_gpus.pdf); [Horváth et al. 2016](http://drivenbynostalgia.com/files/keplershuffle/paper.pdf)). But a parallel reduction's order "often will be non-deterministic" ([CMWR 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_saetra_cmwr_2012.pdf)), and a dt that depends on the fastest cell lets one violent corner stall the whole sheet. The combined recipe is Hydrophobia's fixed CFL 0.5 sub-stepping with a fixed maximum sub-step count per frame, plus Chentanez's velocity clamp so the CFL inequality holds by construction; the clamp only engages in violent frames and turns a time-step-limited cost into a velocity-limited one. For damping, Müller's three options are a physical −kv force ("No stability guarantees"), multiplicative scaling v *= s ("smaller time step → stronger effect"), and slope clamping with maxOffset = maxSlope·h for resolution independence ([Müller GDC 2008](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc08/slides/S6509i1.pdf)); lisyarus gives the dt-normalised form used in practice, **frictionFactor = pow(1 − friction, dt)**, and an h-dependent viscosity factor **H²/(H² + 3·dt·ν)** ([lisyarus](https://lisyarus.github.io/blog/posts/simulating-water-over-terrain.html)). Ghost-cell expansion (an overlap of six cells lets a tile run three steps before exchanging) is the trick to cut barriers when a sheet is split across worker threads ([CMWR 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_saetra_cmwr_2012.pdf)). For determinism in lockstep networking, Gaffer on Games' collected experience is that same binary, same instruction set, `/fp:strict`, no approximate rsqrt/rcp, re-asserted FPU control word and per-frame checksums suffice on PC ("We have never had a problem with the IEEE standard across any PC cpu AMD and Intel with this approach"), while cross-platform determinism is not achievable without equivalent care on both sides ([Gaffer on Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/floating_point_determinism/)).

## Conclusion

The research reframes Hydrophobia's achievement: its finite-volume core was ahead of what the industry subsequently shipped, and what dated it was the coupling layer, not the solver. The field split into two lineages that never met: flood modellers (Kurganov–Petrova, porosity, cut cells, sparse tiles, Brodtkorb's engineering) perfected the conserving solver and its wet/dry behaviour, while graphics (Müller, Thürey, Chentanez, Yuksel, Cords) perfected displaced-volume coupling, smooth buoyancy and threshold-driven splash and foam. The two lists almost never cite each other, and porosity SWE in particular has never been used in a game despite being a trivial extension of a per-cell wall flag. A modern interactive-water system that merges them, KP07 reconstruction and face porosity in the solver, Thürey Δb and the Chentanez coefficient cap in the coupling, Chentanez's three-threshold splash rule for effects, and Horváth's wake-neighbours tile list for cost, has no published precedent and would exceed every shipped example in this survey.

The second shift is in where the cost actually sits. Launch overhead, GPU readback and the rigid-body coupling, not the stencil, dominate every system that reports a breakdown, which is why Hydrophobia-size sheets belong on the CPU and why blood pools of 32²–128² cells are almost free: millimetre-deep viscous liquid has a wave speed under 0.3 m/s, needs one explicit step per frame, reaches a sleep threshold quickly under strong damping, and never trips CFL. The engineering problem for many small pools is bookkeeping and determinism, not floating-point throughput.

## Proposal list 1: If we were upgrading Hydrophobia's solver

Ordered by payoff per effort. Δx = 20 units, Δt_sub = the existing CFL-0.5 sub-step.

1. **Inject displaced volume alongside the velocity stamp (Thürey Δb).** Add one float per cell b[i,j] = column length covered by bodies, from a top-down rasterisation of each Havok body each step. Apply h[nbr] += α·(b_t − b_{t−1})/4 to the four neighbours, α ≈ 0.5–1.0 as the wave-amplitude knob; volume is conserved for any α. This alone produces bow waves and wakes behind wading characters and dragged debris and removes the hole above submerged bodies ([Thürey 2007](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/breakingWaves.pdf); [Müller GDC 2008](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc08/slides/S6509i1.pdf)).
2. **Cap and sub-step the velocity stamp (Chentanez Algorithm 2).** Replace the hard overwrite with u += coeff·(v_body − u), coeff = min(1, e^{−depth}·0.2·(depth·Δt/η)·A/Δx²); sweep the body along its path with num_substeps = max(1, ⌊|v_horiz|Δt/Δx + 0.5⌋) so sprinting characters and thrown objects do not skip cells ([hfFluid §2.3.2](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)).
3. **Make Havok buoyancy C⁰ at the surface and dissipative.** Sample η bilinearly over the body footprint; ramp buoyancy linearly to zero over a band d_t ≈ 0.25–0.5 cells below the surface (Cords); add damping ∝ v × (submerged_area/total_area); compute drag −½ρC_D A_eff|v_rel|v_rel with v_rel = v − [u, u∂η/∂x + w∂η/∂z, w] so bodies ride the current. Run the Müller/Thürey per-column force F = ρ g Δx² b + ρ h b u for furniture and debris, triangle prisms only for hulls ([Cords & Staadt §4.1.1](http://hilkocords.de/publications/open_water.pdf); [hfFluid §2.3.1](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf); [boat model](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/water-interaction-model-for-boats-in-video-games-part-2)).
4. **Move wall flags from cells to faces, then add porosity.** Store two face bitmasks (x-faces, y-faces); rasterise thin walls with Bresenham into them; a flagged face has zero normal flux and zero velocity. Add the Chentanez automatic rule (face is a wall if h ≤ 10⁻⁴Δx on one side and that side's bed is above the other side's surface). Then, for furniture-dense cells, store φ (cell wetted fraction, clamp ≥ ~0.05) and ψ_x, ψ_y (face open fractions) sampled from the room SDF; multiply each HLL face flux by ψ, divide the cell update by φ, add s_φ = g h²/2 ∇φ to momentum and a building drag c_D^b‖v‖v ([Özgen et al. 2017](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/1482522); [hfFluid §2.1.4](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)).
5. **Adopt the Kurganov–Petrova package on top of the existing HLL flux.** MUSCL-reconstruct w = h + B (not h) with generalised minmod θ = 1.3; apply the two-case bed clamp to w; compute u = √2 h(hu)/√(h⁴ + max(h⁴, ε)) with ε = 10⁻²·Δx = 0.2 units and then set hu := h·u; snap h < 10⁻⁶ to 0 and zero its momentum; replace midpoint with Heun (SSP-RK2) or plain Euler, keep CFL 0.5. Expect ~2× flux cost, no new state, and the end of thin-film racing on stairs ([KP07](https://people.tamu.edu/~gpetrova//KPSV.pdf); [Brodtkorb 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_kp_on_gpus.pdf)).
6. **Waterfall faces for ledges and tub rims.** If (B_i − B_{i+1})/Δx > 3 and B_i > η_{i+1}, make the lower side reflecting, update the face velocity by u −= gΔt(−h_i − 3)/Δx, and emit the crossing volume as particles seeded between the upper bed and lower surface ([hfFluid §2.4.3](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)).
7. **Splash and foam from the field.** Keep h_prev; spawn splash where |∇η| > 0.45·gΔt/Δx and (h − h_prev)/Δt > 4 and ∇²η < −4, volume ∝ |∇η|, C_deposit ∈ [1, 10]; body splashes when a footprint's depth rises faster than v_thres·Δt, velocity α_rn = 0.86, α_p ≈ 0.15, α_r ≈ 0.4; foam as a decaying scalar advected by (u, w) plus a Cords diffusion step f ← d·f + (1−d)/4·Σnbrs ([hfFluid §2.4](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf); [Cords & Staadt](http://hilkocords.de/publications/open_water.pdf)).
8. **Safety clamps and volume audit.** Clamp |u|, |w| < 0.5·Δx/Δt_sub; cap integration depth with h_avgmax = 2·Δx/(gΔt_sub); overshoot damping at fronts with λ_edge = 2Δx, α_edge = 0.3; every N frames sum volume per connected region and redistribute the coupling residual evenly ([hfFluid §2.1.5, §2.2](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf); [Müller GDC 2008](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc08/slides/S6509i1.pdf)).
9. **Tile sleeping and job layout.** Split each sheet into 8×8 tiles with `wet` and `active` bits; a tile is active if any |u|, |w| or |Δh| exceeds a sleep threshold for fewer than N consecutive steps or if a stamp touched it; step active tiles plus their 8 neighbours; force-activate tiles under bodies and emitters; fall back to full-sheet stepping when >75% of tiles are active and re-probe every 100 steps. Queue all stamps from gameplay threads and apply at step start into the write buffer. Stay on the CPU: at ~1200 cells per sheet, GPU launch overhead (~0.75 ms per 128² step on a GTX 480) exceeds the whole CPU cost ([Brodtkorb 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_kp_on_gpus.pdf); [Horváth 2016](http://drivenbynostalgia.com/files/keplershuffle/paper.pdf); [hfFluid Table 1](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)).
10. **Widen SSE to AVX2.** Pad rows to a multiple of 8 plus a 2-cell halo; keep SoA planes for h, hu, hv, B, b, φ; convert select-by-mask to `_mm256_blendv_ps`; avoid approximate rsqrt in √(gh) if determinism matters; `/fp:strict` and per-frame checksums for lockstep ([CMWR 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_saetra_cmwr_2012.pdf); [Gaffer on Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/floating_point_determinism/)).
11. **Appearance above physics.** Render at Δx/4 with an FFT ripple texture advected by (u, w) through three coordinate sets reset every τ = 3 s with phase τ/3 and strain weight ω = e^{−μγ}, μ = 1; this is the Müller "physics low-res, appearance hi-res" split and costs nothing in the solver ([hfFluid §2.5.2](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)).
12. **Only then, a nested fine window.** If the player's immediate area needs finer waves, add one 2–4× finer window following the player with Dirichlet ghost cells interpolated from the coarse sheet each sub-step and flux correction of the coarse cells under it every coarse step, padded by a few cells per expected move before re-centring ([Sætra et al. 2015](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/saetra_brodtkorb_lie_amr.pdf); [hfFluid](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)).

## Proposal list 2: For our Unreal Engine 2 mod's blood-pool sim

CPU-side, 32×32 to 128×128 cells per pool, many small pools, viscous liquid, characters and gibs moving through it, Direct3D 9 era. Unreal units (UU) throughout, with the engine's default gravity of 950 UU/s² as g. Specific values are inferred from the cited rules, not shipped parameter sets.

1. **Adopt: a staggered-grid explicit SWE, first order, one step per frame.** State per pool: h, hu, hv, B (floor height from a downward trace per cell at pool creation), b (body-covered column), two face bitmasks. Cell size 4 UU for gib splats (a 32² pool covers a 128-UU, ~2.4 m square) and 8 UU for flooded floors (128² covers 1024 UU). Blood depth is 0.1–0.5 UU, so c = √(g h) ≤ √(950·0.5) ≈ 22 UU/s and CFL at 60 Hz on 4-UU cells is ≈ 0.09: no sub-stepping, and even 20 Hz stepping is safe. Use Euler, not midpoint; Brodtkorb found Euler vs RK2 "minimal changes" ([Brodtkorb 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_kp_on_gpus.pdf)).
2. **Adopt: the KP07 thin-film fixes without the MUSCL reconstruction.** Keep Hydrophobia-style hydrostatic reconstruction so blood sits still on uneven floors and stair treads; desingularise u = √2 h(hu)/√(h⁴ + max(h⁴, ε)) with ε = 10⁻²·Δx = 0.04 UU (~1 mm, about a visible smear), then hu := h·u; snap h < 10⁻⁶ to 0 and zero momentum; `h = max(h, 0)` and an `isfinite` select after each step. Skip second-order MUSCL: wave accuracy is irrelevant for a viscous dark liquid and the first-order diffusion is welcome ([KP07](https://people.tamu.edu/~gpetrova//KPSV.pdf); [Brodtkorb 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_kp_on_gpus.pdf)).
3. **Adopt: face flags via Bresenham, plus the automatic dry-wall rule.** Rasterise BSP walls, mover edges and static-mesh collision outlines that cross the pool into the x-face and y-face masks once at pool creation (re-rasterise movers when they move); face velocity is zeroed and never advected on a flagged face. Treat a face as a wall when h ≤ 10⁻⁴Δx on one side and that side's floor is above the other's surface, which keeps the pool edge creeping rather than jittering. Skip porosity: at 4–8 UU cells chair legs are one cell and a flag is enough ([hfFluid §2.1.4](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)).
4. **Adopt: Thürey Δb stamping for feet, bodies and gibs.** Each pawn foot and each gib is a top-down disc or capsule; rasterise its column coverage into b each step and distribute α·Δb/4 to the four neighbours, α = 1 for gibs landing (big splat ring), α ≈ 0.5 for footsteps. Sub-step the path when a body moves more than one cell per frame (num_substeps = ⌊|v|Δt/Δx + 0.5⌋). Blend the face velocity toward the body velocity with coeff capped at 1 (use 0.2–0.5 as the per-cell adaption rate) rather than overwriting. This gives the bow ridge in front of a dragged corpse and the trail behind it, which is the single effect that makes blood read as liquid ([Thürey 2007](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/breakingWaves.pdf); [hfFluid §2.3.2](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)).
5. **Adopt: strong, dt-normalised damping as the "viscous" model.** Per step multiply face velocities by pow(1 − friction, dt) with friction ≈ 0.9–0.98 per second for fresh blood and higher as the pool ages, add the h-dependent viscosity factor H²/(H² + 3·dt·ν) with ν tuned so a 0.2-UU film stops within ~1 s, and a quadratic drag −c_D‖v‖v for coagulated pools. Add a sink h −= k·dt (k ≈ 0.01–0.05 UU/s) so pools soak in and the tile list empties, countering the "wet cell almost never becomes dry again" effect. Skip Kass–Miller ADI unless the explicit step shows oscillation; with these damping terms it will not ([lisyarus](https://lisyarus.github.io/blog/posts/simulating-water-over-terrain.html); [Horváth 2016](http://drivenbynostalgia.com/files/keplershuffle/paper.pdf); [Müller GDC 2008](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc08/slides/S6509i1.pdf)).
6. **Adopt: tile sleeping with the wake-neighbours rule, and pool lifetime management.** 8×8 tiles with `wet`/`active` bits; a tile sleeps after 10 consecutive steps with max|u|, |v| < 0.5 UU/s and max|Δh| < 10⁻³ UU, and velocities are snapped to zero when it sleeps; a stamp wakes its tile and the 8 neighbours. A pool whose tiles are all asleep is frozen (no stepping at all) and only its mesh persists; a pool with h < ε everywhere is destroyed. Cap live pools (e.g. 16 stepping, 64 frozen) with LRU eviction into a static decal ([Brodtkorb 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_kp_on_gpus.pdf); [Horváth 2016](http://drivenbynostalgia.com/files/keplershuffle/paper.pdf)).
7. **Adopt: a surface-film scalar instead of foam.** One extra plane f advected semi-Lagrangian by (u, v) with a slow decay, seeded at impact points and under feet, with a Cords diffusion f ← 0.9f + 0.025·Σnbrs; render it as streak/gloss variation. Skip splash particles from the field entirely: a millimetre film never meets the Chentanez breaking thresholds; gib impacts spawn the usual particle splat directly from gameplay and stamp Δb ([Cords & Staadt](http://hilkocords.de/publications/open_water.pdf); [hfFluid §2.4](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)).
8. **Adopt: local coordinates, h not η, SoA planes, one job per pool.** Keep each pool's grid origin at the pool so world offsets never enter the float state; store depth, never surface height; pad rows to 4 (SSE) with a 1-cell halo; iterate pools and tiles in a fixed order with a fixed dt so results are deterministic on one machine. Run the sim client-side only, as Ottosson did for Frostbite: it is cosmetic, replicated events (gib spawn, footstep) regenerate it locally, and no server state is needed ([Brodtkorb 2012](https://brodtkorb.org/files/publications/brodtkorb_kp_on_gpus.pdf); [Ottosson thesis](https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/water-interaction-ottosson-bjorn.pdf)).
9. **Skip: the GPU.** D3D9 render-target ping-pong works (the 2004 NVIDIA sample ran on ps1.1), but gameplay queries like "is this foot in blood" need GetRenderTargetData readback stalls, and 16k-cell pools cost well under a millisecond on one CPU core; the whole budget for 16 stepping pools is on the order of 1–2 ms (estimate, not measured) ([NVIDIA WaterInteraction](https://download.nvidia.com/developer/SDK/Individual_Samples/DEMOS/Direct3D9/src/WaterInteraction/docs/WaterInteraction.pdf)).
10. **Skip: porosity, cut cells, AMR, PML open boundaries, breaking-wave sheets, Havok-style buoyancy.** Pools are disjoint, closed, and shallower than any body, so there is nothing to float; gibs settle with ordinary physics and only *stamp*. Walls at 4–8 UU resolution are face flags. Open boundaries do not occur; a pool edge is a dry front. The one thing to borrow from buoyancy work is the drag term on gibs sliding in blood, −½ρC_D A|v_rel|v_rel with v_rel against the pool velocity, if sliding bodies should visibly follow the flow ([hfFluid §2.3.1](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/hfFluid.pdf)).
11. **Rendering, briefly:** build a per-pool mesh from h on a worker as Hydrophobia did, or write h into a small A8L8/R32F texture and draw a projected decal quad with a normal map derived in the pixel shader (the NVIDIA 2004 pattern); fade pool alpha with h/ε_visible so edges feather instead of stepping ([NVIDIA WaterInteraction](https://download.nvidia.com/developer/SDK/Individual_Samples/DEMOS/Direct3D9/src/WaterInteraction/docs/WaterInteraction.pdf)).
