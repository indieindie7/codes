# Spring bones and light-warp ramps win first

The best soft-body and skin upgrades for Advent Rising and Unreal II are also the cheapest ones. For flesh, that means **script-driven spring ("jiggle") bones**. For skin, it means a **per-texture light-warp ramp inside the existing charlight pixel shader**. A **Jimenez separable screen-space SSS pass** in the post chain is the optional second step for skin, and a **wrapper vertex shader** for wobble and impact dents is the optional deep step for flesh. Everything heavier is out of reach on Direct3D 9, or in an engine whose bone matrices never reach the GPU: texture-space diffusion, Burley/Disney diffusion, XPBD tet cages inside the renderer, Delta Mush and ML deformers. The deciding fact is local and verified. **Both games skin characters on the CPU and draw them as fixed-function draws with vertex blending off.** That means a D3D wrapper only ever sees final vertex positions and can never swap the skinning method itself, as Dual Quaternion Skinning would require. The good news is that **our fork's charlight path already passes camera-space normals and positions to the pixel shader and lights per pixel from captured D3D lights.** That makes Valve-style Half-Lambert, pre-integrated skin and DICE-style translucency more reachable than the generic SSS research assumed. The open-source base is clean. **iryoku/separable-sss is BSD-2-Clause with an attribution line.** Ten Minute Physics, PositionBasedDynamics, Kawaii Physics and velocity skinning are MIT, and Bullet is zlib. The code to avoid is NVIDIA FleX, Unreal Engine and Unity HDRP source, GPU Pro book code, and every pre-integrated-skin LUT repository found, because none of those LUT repos declares a licence.

## Both engines hand the wrapper already-skinned vertices, which sets every limit

Unreal II's `Engine.dll` contains the string `SSESkeletalSkinning`. It has no hardware-skinning option string, and it exports `USkeletalMeshInstance::GetFrame`, which writes skinned vertex positions into a caller buffer. That is the classic UE1/UE2 CPU skinning entry point. These are local string and export scans of the Steam install ([local evidence](file:///C:/Program%20Files%20(x86)/Steam/steamapps/common/Unreal%20II%20The%20Awakening/System/)). Our own character probe confirms it from the D3D side. **Every logged character and weapon draw in Advent and U2 was fixed-function with `vblend 0/0`** (vertex blending and indexed blending off), lit by up to 4–8 D3D point lights. That held across 51 and 717 distinct draw keys ([Advent probe](file:///C:/Users/john/Documents/AdventRising_research/pbr_probe/chars.txt); [U2 probe](file:///C:/Users/john/Documents/github/codes/test-results/2026-10-01-pc/02-char-probe/chars.txt)). Advent's own Engine.dll was not string-scanned because its install path was not found, but the probe shows the same pattern empirically.

The consequences are direct. **Bone matrices never reach the GPU**, so any per-bone effect inside the wrapper has to be reconstructed from vertex motion between frames. **Geometry hashes are useless for identification** because positions change every frame. RTX Remix documents the same problem, and its roadmap states that replacing CPU-skinned or shader-skinned objects "requires investigation" ([RTX Remix roadmap](https://github.com/NVIDIAGameWorks/rtx-remix/wiki/Roadmap); [Remix asset docs](https://docs.omniverse.nvidia.com/kit/docs/rtx_remix/latest/docs/howto/learning-assets.html)). The texture hash our fork already computes is the right key. To tell several copies of the same enemy apart, combine it with vertex count, index count and the World-matrix translation. No public precedent was found of a D3D8/9 wrapper adding per-vertex deformation to CPU-skinned characters, so this part would be novel work.

The skinning-quality fixes from the literature split cleanly along this line. **Dual-quaternion skinning costs only about 7 extra vertex-shader instructions and 8 floats per bone** ([Kavan et al.](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~ladislav/dq/)). Disney's **optimized centres of rotation** fix both candy-wrapper twist and bulging with an offline per-vertex precompute ([Le & Hodgins 2016](https://studios.disneyresearch.com/?p=39548)). Both need bone transforms at draw time, though, so in UE2 they would mean patching the engine's CPU skinning through the Ghidra work, not shader work. **Direct Delta Mush** made Delta Mush real-time capable but carries heavy per-vertex data ([EA SEED](https://www.ea.com/seed/news/siggraph2019-direct-delta-mush)). **Ziva-style ML deformers**, which shipped in Hellblade II, are out of reach, and Unity discontinued the product in April 2024 ([Unity](https://unity.com/blog/news/update-about-ziva)). At the content level, the reachable fixes are **helper and twist bones plus corrective shapes** baked into meshes we import ourselves.

## Shipped games fake flesh with bones, zones and layer reveals, not volumetric simulation

The research found no shipped game that simulates character flesh volumetrically in real time. The closest is Hellblade II's learned Ziva deformation ([CG Channel](https://www.cgchannel.com/2023/07/unity-releases-ziva-real-time-2-0/)). What games actually ship falls into four families.

**Jiggle bones** are the main source of "soft" secondary motion. Source's `$jigglebone`, in every Source game since Source 2007, marks bones for runtime spring simulation of "bouncy flesh, floppy ears". It has rigid, flexible, base-spring and "boing" modes, and stiffness runs from 1 to 1000 ([VDC $jigglebone](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/$jigglebone)). The page was fetched only as a search snippet, so treat exact parameter semantics as unverified. Modern open equivalents are UE's AnimDynamics ([Epic](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/animation-blueprint-animdynamics-in-unreal-engine)) and Kawaii Physics. Kawaii Physics keeps bone lengths fixed, so it "cannot stretch or collapse even if simulation breaks down" ([GitHub, MIT](https://github.com/pafuhana1213/KawaiiPhysics)). The Source Filmmaker "soft body" look the user associates with this style is mostly jiggle bones plus corrective flex shapes. The truly volumetric parts are offline Blender bakes.

**Zone-based gore** is the oldest damage family. **Soldier of Fortune (2000) split each character into 26 independently damaged "gore zones" on Raven's GHOUL engine**, and GHOUL 2.0 in SoF II raised that to 36 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soldier_of_Fortune_(video_game))). This is CPU bookkeeping plus content: stumps, hidden sections and attached gibs. It needs no shaders, so it is the most directly portable precedent for a 2004 engine.

**Layered anatomy reveal** is the current high end. **Dead Island 2's FLESH** (2023) models skin, muscle, bone and organs. Blades cut layer by layer and blunt weapons crush ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/dead-island-2-flesh-system-and-procedural-gore-showcased-in-new-gameplay)). **The Dead Space remake builds each Necromorph from the skeleton outward and peels flesh off in chunks**, and the peeling doubles as a health readout ([EA/Epic](https://store.epicgames.com/news/inside-dead-space-2-the-new-necromorph-nightmare)). The RE2 remake's "wet gore" focused on visible per-hit damage ([SYFY](https://www.syfy.com/syfy-wire/resident-evil-2-remake-horror-gore-zombie-evolution)). Naughty Dog's The Last of Us Part II gore tools exist only as a GDC Vault abstract ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1027376/Character-Technology-of-The-Last)). **None of these sources spells out the rendering method.** The likely pattern is a per-region damage mask that reveals an inner material or inner skinned meshes. That is an inference from the descriptions, not a citation.

**Active ragdolls** sell mass and pain rather than tissue. Euphoria in GTA IV combines physics, AI and authored animation so that a body reacts to where and how hard it was hit ([Oxford University Innovation](https://innovation.ox.ac.uk/?p=8954)). In UE2 this maps onto the existing Advent physics plan (fix the .ka limits and solver, then powered ragdolls, then bone lifters), not onto renderer work.

For wounds that stay attached to an animated body, production uses two approaches. The first paints hits into a **per-character UV-space render target**, as in UE3's HitMask and Ryan Bruck's GDC demos. It needs unique UVs and costs a render-to-texture draw per hit, and Tom Looman measured **1.6–4.5 ms spikes** from it. Looman's fix is the second approach: store **3–5 hit points in reference-pose space and evaluate sphere masks in the pixel shader** at constant cost ([Tom Looman](https://tomlooman.com/unreal-engine-render-character-wounds/)). The UDK HitMask page itself returned 403, so its existence rests on the search listing. On the decal-parallax versus vertex-deformation question raised earlier, the evidence favours using both for different jobs. **Parallax wound decals are pixel-only and fit the wrapper today** (single-step offset parallax on ps_2_0, steep parallax on ps_3_0). **Vertex dents need the hit location in mesh space**, which the wrapper cannot see without a channel from script.

## Soft-body solvers rank by stability, and only the stateless ones fit inside a shader

Of the simulation families, two suit a CPU modding budget. **Shape matching** (Müller et al. 2005) is "unconditionally stable", because particles are always pulled toward well-defined goal positions. It costs a constant 3×3 matrix assembly and rotation extraction per cluster. In 2005, on a Pentium 4 at 3.2 GHz, a game-like head of **8 clusters and 66 simulated points drove a 6,460-face mesh**, and 100 such objects ran at 50 fps ([Müller 2005](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/MeshlessDeformations_SIG05.pdf)). **XPBD with substeps** makes stiffness independent of iteration count ([XPBD](https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/XPBD.pdf)), and "Small Steps" shows that many single-iteration substeps beat one large step ([SCA 2019](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1145/3309486-3340247)). The closest published precedent for "skin with LBS, then run a flesh pass" is Position Based Skinning. It **cut LBS's ~14% volume loss to within 0.5%** with PBD edge and tet constraints, and it gave passive jiggle in zones far from bones ([Abu Rumman & Fratarcangeli 2014](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/21211/1/FullText.pdf)). Its timing table did not extract cleanly from the PDF, so read the original before quoting numbers. Projective dynamics and fast mass-spring are stable but need a prefactored sparse solve. Plain explicit mass-spring blows up when stiff. Vivace, Vertex Block Descent and FleX are GPU-compute methods and do not fit D3D9.

**The decisive split is between stateless and stateful methods.** Anything that "rings out" after motion stops needs persistent per-particle state: jiggle, shape matching, PBD and FEM. In this engine generation, that state has to live on the CPU. **Velocity skinning** is the exception. It adds drag and squash-and-stretch from bone velocities "as a single pass vertex shader" on existing skinning weights ([velocityskinning.com](https://velocityskinning.com/)), and the reference GLSL shader and C++ code are MIT ([drohmer/velocity_skinning_gpu](https://github.com/drohmer/velocity_skinning_gpu/blob/master/scenes/shared_assets/shaders/velocity_skinning/shader.vert.glsl)). It stops dead when the bone stops, but a damped spring per bone on the CPU restores settle and overshoot for a few floats each. Because the wrapper has no bones, its version is **per-vertex lag from previous-frame positions** (`offset = -k·(pos − pos_prev_smoothed)·mask`). That is stateless in the shader and needs one extra vertex stream plus an offline per-vertex jiggle mask exported from our .psk/.gem pipeline.

The 2026 CPU cost estimates in the notes are extrapolations, not measurements. They put shape matching with 10–30 clusters at well under 0.1 ms and XPBD over a 500–2,000 particle tet cage at about 0.2–2 ms per character.

## The charlight shader already supplies normals, so most skin tricks are one pass

The SSS literature ranks techniques by what they need, and the generic assumption that UE2 characters are "vertex-lit with no per-pixel normals" is **out of date for our fork**. Advent's skins were drawn by a game ps_1_1 shader over fixed-function vertices. **Our charlight/pbr path already replaces that pass, carries camera-space normal and position in TEXCOORD2/3, and lights per pixel from the captured D3D lights** ([local: u2shaders.hpp ~1835–1880](file:///C:/Users/john/Documents/github/d3d8to9-gi/source/u2shaders.hpp)). This is verified for Advent. For U2 it is implied by the shared charlight path, so check it per game. With normals and light vectors available, the "needs custom vertex shader" column in the generic matrix collapses for most techniques.

| Technique | What it needs | Shader model | Fit in our fork |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light-warp ramp (Valve `$lightwarptexture` style) | Half-Lambert N·L, 1D ramp per texture rule | ps_2_0 | **Do first**: one dependent fetch in charlight |
| Half-Lambert + Fresnel rim (TF2) | N, L, V | ps_2_0 for 1–2 lights | Feasible now with charlight's normal and position |
| Pre-integrated skin LUT (Penner) | N·L, curvature from `ddx`/`ddy` or baked | ps_2_a / ps_3_0 (gradients) | Feasible; generate our own LUT |
| DICE thickness translucency | N, L, V, baked thickness map | ps_2_0 | Feasible; bake thickness in Blender |
| Separable screen-space SSS (Jimenez) | lit colour, linear depth, skin mask | 11-tap ps_2_0; 17–25 tap + follow-surface ps_2_a/3_0 | Good fit with the post chain |
| Jimenez shadow-map transmittance | light-space depth of characters | ps_2_a / 3_0 | Poor: U2 shadows are projected silhouettes, not depth maps |
| Texture-space diffusion (GPU Gems 3) | irradiance texture per mesh, ~13 passes | ps_3_0 | Not feasible |
| Burley / Disney normalized diffusion | compute shader, 21–55 samples, TAA | CS 5.0 | Not feasible |

The Valve recipe is the cheapest convincing skin. Half-Lambert `(0.5·N·L + 0.5)²` keeps shape on the dark side. A warp texture painted with "a small reddish terminator region" and allowed to go "up to two times overbright" fakes the light bleed of SSS at the terminator ([Mitchell et al., NPAR 2007](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall07/cos597B/papers/mitchell-team-fortress.pdf)). In Source, `$lightwarptexture` requires `$phong`, with `$phongboost 0` used to get the warp without visible highlights ([VDC $phongboost](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/$phongboost)). The pages for `$lightwarptexture` and `$rimlight` themselves were blocked, so their exact wording is unverified. **Pre-integrated skin reaches the same red-shifted terminator** from a 2D LUT indexed by N·L and curvature, with no blur passes ([Penner, SIGGRAPH 2011](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/penner-preintegrated-skin-rendering-siggraph-2011-advances-in-realtime-rendering-course/13966747)). It is more physical but needs gradient instructions or a baked curvature map ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/in-depth-skin-shading-in-unity3d)). The LUT ring integral in the notes is recalled from the book, not checked, so verify it before use. Per-texture ramps also cover the non-skin materials. **Wax gets a strong warm band at low contrast, and gore gets a deep red band with high specular**, each chosen by texture hash.

For translucency, DICE's GDC 2011 method uses a baked **local thickness map**, made by inverted-normal ambient occlusion, plus a distorted back-light vector. It ran on DX9-era consoles without shadow maps ([Barré-Brisebois](https://colinbarrebrisebois.com/2011/03/07/gdc-2011-approximating-translucency-for-a-fast-cheap-and-convincing-subsurface-scattering-look/)). **The exact formula in the notes is recalled, not read from the slides**, so check it before implementing. Jimenez's 2010 alternative takes thickness from shadow-map depth ([iryoku](https://www.iryoku.com/translucency/)), but U2's projected silhouette shadows provide no character depth maps. Unity HDRP itself takes the maximum of shadow-map and baked thickness, because shadow-map precision fails on fine features ([Golubev 2018](https://www.advances.realtimerendering.com/s2018/Efficient%20screen%20space%20subsurface%20scattering%20Siggraph%202018.pdf)). Baked thickness is the right choice here.

For true diffusion blur, **Jimenez's separable SSS needs only colour, linear depth, a mask and the FOV**: two 1D passes of 11, 17 or 25 taps, with optional depth-aware "follow surface" ([iryoku separable-sss](https://www.iryoku.com/separable-sss/)). The header ships a ready `SSSS_HLSL_3` path that targets ps_3_0 ([SeparableSSS.h](https://github.com/iryoku/separable-sss/blob/master/SeparableSSS.h)). The port has four requirements. The post chain's depth must be converted to linear depth. Mixed-up `sssWidth` units must be converted to Unreal units (1 UU ≈ 1.9 cm is an assumption to verify per game). Render targets must be non-multisampled. And skin pixels must be marked, preferably with a **stencil reference set by the wrapper during skin-hash draws**, because UE2 may already use framebuffer alpha. ENB's Skyrim SSS shows the same principle, a skin flag the injector can see, and it also shows the limit: no direct light means no visible effect ([STEP ENB reference](https://stepmodifications.org/wiki/SkyrimSE:ENBSeries_INI_Reference/Subsurfacescattering)). The only cost figure available is "under 0.5 ms" for the 2015 importance-sampled version on modern hardware. Nobody has measured the 2012 17-tap code on D3D9-class GPUs.

## Script springs come first; bone access needs one test before anything else

The user's notes conflict on bone access, and the local evidence settles part of it. **Both games' exported Actor.uc declare SetBoneRotation, SetBoneLocation, SetBoneDirection and SetBoneScale.** Unreal II's Engine.dll exports the native `USkeletalMeshInstance::SetBone*` functions, and stock U2 code already uses bone controls on the Araknid ([local: U2 Actor.uc 1301–1317](file:///C:/Users/john/Documents/Tools/u2_export/full_Engine/Classes/Actor.uc); [local: Advent Actor.uc 1059–1083](file:///C:/Users/john/Documents/AdventRising_src/Engine/Classes/Actor.uc)). The earlier "script bone posing is blocked in U2" finding therefore probably reflects a narrower failure, perhaps on specific meshes, in rotation spaces, or when combined with playing animations, not a missing API. **That is unverified and should be the first test.** One more caution: UE2 bone controllers **replace** the animated rotation rather than add to it ([UDN SkelAnim2, snippet only](https://docs.unrealengine.com/udk/Two/SkelAnim2.html)). A spring therefore has to read the animated pose (GetBoneRotation or GetBoneCoords) and add its own offset. The `Space` parameter in Advent's API and its `bRubbery` Karma flag still need tracing in Ghidra.

UnrealScript cannot add bones. **Jiggle bones only exist where the mesh already has them**: on stock meshes that means coarse sway of spine, head and clavicles, and on our own Golem/.psk imports it means real belly, cheek, sac, tail and flap bones with skin weights. Springy KHinge and KConeLimit apply to Karma actors, not to bones of an animated pawn. The community "Karma Ragdoll Injury System" shows how painful live partial ragdolls are in UE2: about 3 per zone, network issues, and animation lock-out ([UnrealWiki mirror](https://unrealarchive.org/wikis/unreal-wiki/Legacy:Karma_Ragdoll_Injury_System.html)). **Keep Karma for death and knock-down, and use script springs for living flesh.** That matches the existing physics plan.

The recommended path runs in this order, cheapest and most visible first, in line with the user's stated preference for animation over texture sharpness.

| Step | Work | Where | Gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Verify SetBoneRotation/Location on a playing animated pawn in each game | UnrealScript test | Decides whether step 2 is script or native |
| 2 | Spring bones: per-bone damped spring driven by bone acceleration, plus "boing" squash on hits | Pawn Tick | Bones must exist; add them to our imports |
| 3 | Skin ramp: Half-Lambert + per-texture warp ramp (skin, wax, gore variants) + Fresnel rim in charlight; the same draw sets the skin stencil | Wrapper pixel shader | None; ps_2_0 |
| 4 | Baked thickness translucency from captured lights | Wrapper pixel shader + Blender bake | Verify the DICE formula against slides |
| 5 | Separable SSS, 11 or 17 taps, stencil-masked | Post chain | Linear depth, unit scale |
| 6 | GHOUL-style zone stumps plus parallax wound decals | Content + script + pixel shader | Builds on U2Gore/AdventMod gibs |
| 7 | Wrapper vertex shader: fixed-function transform plus velocity-lag wobble and 4–8 dent spheres | Wrapper | Script-to-wrapper hit channel (undesigned); must also run in U2's shadow silhouette pass |
| 8 | Shape-matching or XPBD flesh clusters | Native CPU pass | Only if 2 and 7 fall short |

Licences are favourable. **Port `SeparableSSS.h` and keep its BSD header and the required credit line**, "Uses Separable SSS. Copyright (C) 2012 by Jorge Jimenez and Diego Gutierrez." BSD-2 is GPL-compatible ([LICENSE](https://github.com/iryoku/separable-sss/blob/master/LICENSE.txt)). For simulation, lift algorithms rather than libraries, because a self-contained C port builds best with Zig against a 2004 ABI. **Ten Minute Physics** (MIT) has XPBD tet soft bodies, soft-body skinning and a Blender tetrahedralizer ([GitHub](https://github.com/matthias-research/pages/tree/master/tenMinutePhysics)). Other permissive sources are **PositionBasedDynamics** (MIT, [GitHub](https://github.com/InteractiveComputerGraphics/PositionBasedDynamics)), **Bullet** (zlib, [LICENSE](https://github.com/bulletphysics/bullet3/blob/master/LICENSE.txt)), Kawaii Physics and UniVRM (MIT), admm-elastic (MIT) and Gaia (Apache-2.0). Three need care. ShapeOp is MPL-2.0, which is file-level copyleft and acceptable. libigl mixes MPL-2.0 with GPL-3 modules, which is acceptable for a GPL mod. **NVIDIA FleX's "1-Way Commercial" licence is non-standard and the library is GPU-only, so avoid it.** Do not copy code from **Unreal Engine source (Epic EULA), Unity HDRP (Unity Companion License) or GPU Pro book code**; those licence terms were not fetched and rest on general knowledge. The GPU Gems 3 code licence is also unverified, so re-derive its maths instead. **All five-plus pre-integrated LUT repositories checked declare no licence.** Instead, generate the LUT and ramps with our own script from the published d'Eon Gaussian constants and release them under the mod's GPL.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. The wrapper's big advantage is not the post chain. It is that charlight already does per-pixel lighting with real normals, which turns most "needs a custom vertex shader" skin techniques into single-pass edits to a shader we own. On the deformation side, CPU skinning blocks the elegant fixes (DQS, centres of rotation, GPU velocity skinning), but it makes the cheapest fix, bone springs, structurally correct. Shadows, Karma and LOD all follow automatically because the engine skins the result. Per-vertex wrapper deformation would be novel work that even RTX Remix has not solved.

Two unknowns gate most of the plan and cost little to resolve. The first is a one-hour script test of SetBone* on an animating pawn in each game. The second is a design for a script-to-wrapper data channel, which every wrapper-side dent, wound mask or per-character jiggle intensity depends on. Until both are answered, steps 3–5 are the safe, independent wins: they can ship with no answer to either.
