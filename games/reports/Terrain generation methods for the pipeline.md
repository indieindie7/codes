# Paint uplift, not height: base shapes for the pipeline

The base-shape stage should be a **sketch-to-uplift authoring layer feeding the stream-power solver the pipeline already owns**, with noise, real DEM tiles and machine-learned detail demoted to style inputs rather than shape sources. The designer draws ridge lines, river corridors, plateau blocks and peak heights; a Hnaidi-style diffusion rasteriser turns those curves into a smooth uplift field `u₀` and a sparse set of elevation constraints; `terrain_erode.stream_power(h, uplift=u₀)` runs a few hundred steps from a flat start and self-organises dendritic valleys and ridges exactly where the uplift gradient puts them; PID-controlled dynamic uplift pins named peaks to designed heights; and the existing droplet, thermal and scoring stages run unchanged on top. This is the Cordonnier 2016 / Schott 2023 architecture, which is the only family in the literature that both honours designer intent and guarantees a pit-free, outlet-draining network ([Cordonnier 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Cordonnier-Computer_Graphics_Forum-2016-Large_Scale_Terrain_Generation_from_Tectonic_Uplift_and_Fluvial_.pdf); [Schott 2023](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)). It is also the cheapest route for this codebase: the solver, priority-flood fill, D8 routing and drainage accumulation exist in `codes/tools/python/terrain/terrain_erode.py`, so the new work is roughly 300 lines of uplift primitives, a sparse Laplace/gradient solve and a PID loop **[inference from the tool's API]**. Real public-domain DEM tiles (USGS 3DEP, SRTM, Copernicus GLO-30) serve three roles at once: direct FUEL-style bases when a real place is wanted, exemplar dictionaries for Guérin-style amplification of the coarse 128–256 grid, and the reference set the geomorphon scorer compares against. Machine-learned generation is worth exactly two downloads (the Guérin 2017 sketch-conditioned cGAN with its pre-trained model, and xandergos's MIT-licensed terrain-diffusion weights that run in about 2.2 GB of VRAM), and no training: every documented training run is days to weeks on an A100 or 3090 Ti class card, and the published failure modes (grid artifacts, flicker, failed altitude constraints) land on precisely the control the designer needs ([Lochner 2023](https://people.cs.uct.ac.za/~jgain/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/lochner2023.pdf); [InfiniteDiffusion](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08309)). Gameplay shaping (walkable slopes under 30°, A* roads that re-flow terrain, cut-and-fill pads, waist-high berms, an "approach" vista before each arena) is a post-pass whose hard constraints are frozen through erosion with the tool's existing `fixed` mask, then verified by reachability and viewshed metrics.

## The erosion stage is already the generator; it only lacks a steering wheel

The pipeline's current shape problem is a control problem, not a realism problem. The earlier report established that noise of every flavour fails perceptual realism because it lacks connected valleys, ridges and hollows, and that stream power plus droplets plus thermal slumping supplies them ([PTRM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04610)). What the earlier report did not ask is where the stream-power solver's *inputs* come from. Cordonnier et al. answered that in 2016 by inverting the usual order: instead of eroding a noise heightfield, they start from **h = 0 and a painted uplift map `u`, and iterate `dh/dt = u − k A^m s^n` (m = 0.5, n = 1) until steady state**, so ridges emerge where uplift is high and dendritic valleys self-organise between them ([Cordonnier 2016, Sec. 3–5](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Cordonnier-Computer_Graphics_Forum-2016-Large_Scale_Terrain_Generation_from_Tectonic_Uplift_and_Fluvial_.pdf)). Their key design finding is that **"the main impact is in the gradient of the uplift values. The actual shape of the uplift does not have a strong influence, except on the boundaries"**; a slowly decreasing gradient gives straight parallel valleys in the gradient direction, a staircase of constant-value steps gives randomly placed valleys, and lower uplift gives smaller valleys ([Cordonnier 2016, Sec. 6.4](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Cordonnier-Computer_Graphics_Forum-2016-Large_Scale_Terrain_Generation_from_Tectonic_Uplift_and_Fluvial_.pdf)). Mountains are "fully shaped after 50 iterations" and converge in 100–300, with the empirical rule **h_max [km] ≈ 2.244·u/k** giving a starting k for a target relief, and only the ratio u/k matters because both multiply the time step ([Cordonnier 2016, Sec. 6](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Cordonnier-Computer_Graphics_Forum-2016-Large_Scale_Terrain_Generation_from_Tectonic_Uplift_and_Fluvial_.pdf)). Their thermal correction caps slopes at 30° and, when the cap is modulated by 3-D Perlin noise between 6° and 54°, randomises the valley pattern; a height-dependent cap puts cliffs on crests ([Cordonnier 2016, Sec. 5, 6.4](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Cordonnier-Computer_Graphics_Forum-2016-Large_Scale_Terrain_Generation_from_Tectonic_Uplift_and_Fluvial_.pdf)).

Schott et al. 2023 turned this into the authoring tool the pipeline needs. Their equation adds a hillslope term, `∂h/∂t = u − s^n a^m + Δh`, solved explicitly on a regular grid, and splits uplift into **`u = u₀ + ũ`: a static part from an "Uplift Tree" of vector primitives and a dynamic part from elevation constraints** ([Schott 2023, Sec. 3–4](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)). The primitives are the designer vocabulary: a disc primitive `u_P(p) = u_S (1 − d²/r²)³`; a **segment "mountain range" primitive with varying radius r(x) and crest value u_S(x)** for "ridge along this line"; a **valley primitive, a segment whose profile is low inside and high outside, with width w ∝ a^0.4 from drainage area**, for "river from here to there" ("By limiting the amount of tectonic uplift within river and valley areas, we force the flow routing to those specific zones"); and asymmetric star primitives whose boundary curve controls flank steepness ([Schott 2023, Sec. 5.1, 6.2](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)). Primitives combine by max for ridge networks and blend for soft transitions, and crucially **discontinuities are fine in the uplift domain ("approximation of faults") whereas blending or warping elevation directly "yields unrealistic artificial-looking terrains with inconsistent drainage network featuring numerous pits"** ([Schott 2023, Sec. 5.2, 7.1](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)). Exact heights come from a PID controller on `ũ`: `ũ(k+1) = K_p e + K_i Σe + K_d Δe` with e = h₀ − h, **K_p = 0.01, K_d = 0.01, K_i = 0.001, updated every 10 iterations**, where a point constraint is a compactly supported smooth step of radius r and curves are sampled into points; controllers are frozen into u₀ at steady state, with the stated limitation that overlapping constraints at incompatible heights "may lead to instabilities" ([Schott 2023, Sec. 6.4, 7.5](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)). Flow routing is multiple-direction with L_p weights, **p = 4**, and drainage area is approximated iteratively inside the erosion loop, reaching the exact value on a 256² grid at about 130 iterations ([Schott 2023, Sec. 4.1–4.2](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)). Performance is not a concern at this scale: **256² runs 5,000 steps in 0.5 s on a GTX 1080 Ti**, and "the shape of the final terrain is observable after only a few hundred iterations" ([Schott 2023, Table 1, Sec. 7.2](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)). The release code is MIT C++ ([H-Schott/StreamPowerErosion](https://github.com/H-Schott/StreamPowerErosion)). Validation is the strongest of any base-shape method: Hack's law fits across nine terrains gave **c = 1.00–1.38 and n = 0.542–0.584 against literature ranges c ∈ [1, 6], n ∈ [0.45, 0.7]** ([Schott 2023, Sec. 7.3](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)). Its known weaknesses are no lakes (flow is forced to the border), no sediment deposition so "valley floors lack variety", and cell sizes of 80–600 m with amplification needed afterwards ([Schott 2023, Sec. 7.5, Table 2](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)).

What this means for the codebase is direct **[inference]**. `terrain_erode.stream_power(h, steps, dt, k, m_exp, uplift, diffusion, fixed)` is the Cordonnier loop with Braun–Willett's implicit update and a diffusion term; called on `h = zeros` with a designed `uplift` array for 300–1,000 steps it *is* the base-shape generator, and its existing `fixed` argument is the mechanism for freezing building pads and road corridors. The new code is the Uplift Tree: segment, disc and valley primitives rasterised onto the grid (a distance transform from a polyline, `scipy.ndimage.distance_transform_edt` on a rasterised curve, is enough for r(x)-varying segments), combined with `np.maximum` for ridges and multiplied by a low-uplift corridor for rivers; a PID loop wrapping `stream_power` in chunks of 10 steps for peak-height constraints; and Cordonnier's noise-modulated slope cap, which the tool's `thermal(hardness=...)` already approximates with its banded hardness field. At 10–30 m cells the result is, as Schott warns, too uniformly dendritic and V-shaped at the fine end; that is why the uplift output should be treated as the *base shape* and the existing droplet and thermal stages kept as the detail and variety stage **[inference]**. Lakes, which Schott drops, are available two ways: Cordonnier's lake-pass graph (the hard part of his paper) or, far simpler at 256², letting `fill_depressions` run each step and then re-lowering designated basin cells below their spill height after the loop so water can be placed there **[inference]**.

Génevaux's hydrology-first method is the alternative when river *topology* must be designed rather than emergent. It grows a river graph inward from mouths on the coast with a three-rule grammar gated by Horton–Strahler order, assigns rising elevations from a user "river slope map", builds ridges on the Voronoi diagram of river nodes with elevation `q_z = max(a_z, b_z, c_z) + λ(q)·d`, **λ ∈ [0, 0.25] from a "terrain slope map"** that "describes which parts of the terrain will become plains, plateaus, valleys, or mountains", and fills the space with blended disc primitives ([Génevaux 2013, Sec. 3–7](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf)). The control knobs are concrete: candidate selection window ζ (ζ = 0 gives networks of similar size, ζ = 20 one large network and several small ones), rule probabilities P_c/P_s/P_a (0.2/0.7/0.1 gives curved watersheds with >75 % first-order streams; 0.2/0.1/0.7 gives long main rivers), a constant edge length e with separation η = σ = ¾e, and a Lipschitz bound κ that "prevents the creation of huge cliffs" ([Génevaux 2013, Sec. 4.2, Fig. 8](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf)). Sketched partial rivers are seeded as high-priority candidates, which is "river from here to there" literally. But the authors list the failure modes themselves: greedy construction "can lead to meandering crests and outcroppings of unnatural-looking hills", plain-to-mountain transitions are abrupt, and "If the slope maps have strong variations flat rivers can cut through high mountains"; Schott adds that its ranges "may not be hydrologically correct, and may not exhibit characteristic dendritic shapes" ([Génevaux 2013, Sec. 8](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf); [Schott 2023, Sec. 7.4](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)). There is no author code; the only public implementation is a third-party Python repo that does not support inland seas or lakes ([bmelaiths/terrainHydrology](https://github.com/bmelaiths/terrainHydrology)). The productive reading **[inference]** is to borrow only the network-growth stage: grow a Génevaux graph at e ≈ 3–6 cells, rasterise its edges as Schott valley primitives (low-uplift corridors of width ∝ A^0.4), and let stream power do the ridges. That gives designed branching where uplift alone would not produce it, with none of the Voronoi-crest artefacts. mapgen4 confirms the same lesson from the hobby side: Patel paints ocean, valley and mountain with brushes and derives elevation from Dijkstra distance fields, but his rivers are a downslope assignment that sometimes "flow along coastlines" and over mountain slopes, and he states that local noise "cannot support global constraints like ... a river flowing from the top of the highest peak down to the ocean" ([mapgen4 river post](https://simblob.blogspot.com/2018/09/mapgen4-river-appearance.html); [Red Blob terrain-from-noise](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/terrain-from-noise/)). Only the physics-based loop satisfies global constraints.

## Diffusion curves supply what uplift cannot: exact plateaus, cliffs and pads

Uplift control is indirect: it says where relief will be, not what height a cell will have. Three designer requests need exact geometry: "plateau here at this height", "cliff along this line", and "flat pad for this building". Hnaidi et al. 2010 is the cleanest method for those. Feature curves are piecewise cubic Béziers carrying per-point constraints **((h, r), (a, b, θ, φ), (A, R))**: elevation h with plateau radius r, slope angles θ and φ with extents a and b on each side, and noise amplitude A and roughness R; the recipes are a **ridge** (elevation plus slope angles each side, small r), a **riverbed** (small elevation range, r = bed width, slight upward slopes), a **cliff** (opposite slope angles on each side), a **hill** (horizontal slope constraints, which force the neighbourhood to the same elevation) and a **crack** ([Hnaidi 2010, Sec. 3.1–3.2](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2010-terrain.pdf)). The solver is a guided diffusion: Laplace `ΔF = 0` where there is no information, a gradient constraint `∇F = G` in slope bands, and `F = F̄` on strict elevation cells, discretised as a weighted Jacobi relaxation **`F^{k+1} = α F_L + β F_G + (1−α−β) F_I` with α = β = ½ in gradient areas, α = β = 0 on strict elevation, α = 1 elsewhere**, run multigrid-style with 5(l − i) iterations per level; the authors specifically reject a Poisson formulation because it "forbids the creation of horizontal angle constraints" ([Hnaidi 2010, Sec. 4.2, 5.2](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2010-terrain.pdf)). Detail is a four-octave noise whose amplitude A and roughness R are themselves diffused fields, with R damping high frequencies rather than changing feature size because the latter "breaks the continuity and gives unnatural ring effects" ([Hnaidi 2010, Sec. 4.3](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2010-terrain.pdf)). It ran at 0.25–0.34 s for 1024² on a 2008 GPU and a canyon sketch took under three minutes, with most of the 45 minutes of tuning spent on noise parameters ([Hnaidi 2010, Sec. 6](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2010-terrain.pdf)). Its weakness is the one Génevaux names: "it does not address the geomorphological properties of a river-network creation", so rivers are just low curves and nothing guarantees drainage ([Génevaux 2013, Sec. 2](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf)). No open-source implementation was found.

In NumPy at 256² this is a one-shot sparse solve rather than a multigrid loop **[inference]**: rasterise curves with `skimage.draw` or a distance transform into Dirichlet cells, gradient-band cells and free cells; assemble a sparse matrix with identity rows on strict cells, one-sided-difference rows equal to the target gradient in slope bands, and five-point Laplacian rows elsewhere; `scipy.sparse.linalg.spsolve` on 65,536 unknowns returns in well under a second. The important architectural move is to run this solver **twice with different outputs**. First, as Schott's rasteriser: ridge curves become high-uplift bands, river curves low-uplift corridors, plateau polygons uniform uplift blocks, and the diffusion fills smooth gradients between them, giving `u₀` with exactly the controlled gradient structure Cordonnier says matters. Second, after stream power and erosion, as an exact-geometry pass: plateau tops, cliff lines and building pads are strict elevation or gradient constraints solved against the eroded terrain as the free field, which yields seamless flats and cliffs without the pit problem because the drainage network already exists and the constraints are local **[inference]**. Guérin's 2022 gradient-domain authoring is the same idea with a Poisson reconstruction and both point and curve constraints on elevation and gradient; Schott's verdict on it, "great control to the user but they do not create hydrologically-correct terrains", is exactly why it belongs after stream power, not instead of it ([Guérin 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cgf.14460?af=R); [eric-guerin/gradient-terrains](https://github.com/eric-guerin/gradient-terrains); [Schott 2023, Sec. 7.4](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)).

## Noise sets the landform family and the roughness, not the layout

Noise remains useful in two places: as the texture of the uplift field (so steady-state ridges are not perfectly smooth) and as the detail octaves added after the solver. The published mechanisms all reduce to one trick, making each octave's amplitude depend on something already computed. Musgrave's **hybrid multifractal (`weight *= signal`, recommended H = 0.25, offset = 0.7)** and **ridged multifractal (`signal = (offset − |noise|)²`, H = 1.0, offset = 1.0, gain = 2.0)** gate detail by altitude ([musgrave.c](https://engineering.purdue.edu/~ebertd/texture/1stEdition/musgrave/musgrave.c)); Quilez's derivative-damped fBm **`a += b·n/(1 + dot(d, d))` with f = 1.98, s = 0.49, b₀ = 0.5** gates detail by accumulated slope and gives "flat areas as well as more rough areas" ([iquilezles.org/morenoise](https://iquilezles.org/articles/morenoise/)); de Carpentier's **swiss turbulence (lacunarity 2.0, gain 0.5, warp 0.15, `amp *= gain·saturate(sum)`)** adds derivative-driven warping that pulls ridges together into connected glacial-looking crests, and **jordan turbulence (gain1 0.8, gain 0.5, warp0 0.4, warp 0.35, damp0 1.0, damp 0.8)** separates gradient damping (thermal, rounded) from gradient warping (fluvial gullies) ([decarpentier.nl](https://www.decarpentier.nl/scape-procedural-extensions)); Red Blob's three-line version multiplies ridged octaves by the lower octaves and adds `pow(e, 3–5)` redistribution and `round(e·n)/n` terraces with n = 2–15 ([Red Blob](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/terrain-from-noise/)). Parberry's exponentially distributed noise is the one change justified by DEM statistics: gradient magnitudes in a 160,000 km² Utah DEM "appear to be exponentially distributed" at every octave from 5 m to 1.28 km, and replacing Perlin's unit gradients with a magnitude table `m[i] = μ^i` yields "mostly flat and gently sloping terrain, with occasional areas of higher gradient that create ... cliffs and escarpments" instead of "large gradients almost everywhere" ([Parberry, JCGT 2015](https://jcgt.org/published/0004/02/01/paper.pdf); code [Ian-Parberry/Tobler](https://github.com/Ian-Parberry/Tobler)). For exact spectral control, Saupe's synthesis fills the frequency domain with Gaussian magnitudes scaled by `f^(−β/2)` and uniform phases and inverse-FFTs; Bourke's landscape examples use **β = 1.8 and 2.4** ([paulbourke.net](https://paulbourke.net/fractals/noise/)). Runevision's 2026 gully filter is the cheapest analytic approximation of fluvial texture: gradient-aligned stripe patterns in randomly pivoted Worley-like cells with lacunarity, detail, gully-weight and edge-rounding knobs, evaluated per point with no simulation ([blog.runevision.com](https://blog.runevision.com/2026/03/fast-and-gorgeous-erosion-filter.html)).

Two measurements decide how to tune these. Natural transects have **S(k) ∝ k^−2 (β ≈ 2, H ≈ 0.5, D ≈ 2.5) regardless of age**: "young, rough mountain ranges, such as the Rocky Mountains, exhibit the same power spectral exponent or fractal dimension as smooth mountain ranges such as the Appalachians"; rougher terrain has larger spectral density at the same exponent ([Pelletier](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/9705033)). Standard fBm with gain 0.5 has H = 1 and therefore β = 2H + 1 = 3, too smooth, which is why pipelines measuring β ≈ 3–4 on their bases should move to gain ≈ 2^−0.5 ≈ 0.707 or synthesise at β = 2.0–2.2 directly **[inference from the fBm relation G = 2^−H, β = 2H + 1 in [iquilezles.org/fbm](https://iquilezles.org/articles/fbm/)]**. A convention trap applies to `terrain_score.psd_slope`: a radially averaged 2-D spectrum of an isotropic surface has exponent β₂D = β₁D + 1, so "natural ≈ 2" in 1-D is ≈ 3 in 2-D; check which the function computes before retuning gain **[inference]**. The second measurement is the geomorphon regression. PTRM's per-class weights are **Valley 0.66, Ridge 0.64, Summit 0.44, Depression 0.42, Spur 0.33, Hollow 0.22, Flat −0.10, Footslope −0.15, Shoulder −0.17, Slope −0.65**, synthetic ridged noise scored lowest of all (0.18) and real terrain's class fractions "distribute normally with a wide span" where synthetic ones are "multi-modal and low-variability" ([PTRM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04610)). Reading the class definitions against those weights **[inference]**: ridged noise raises ridge and spur but produces almost no linear valleys; billow does the opposite; derivative damping and multifractal gating raise flat and footslope on low ground; exponential-gradient noise concentrates slope into escarpment bands; none of them creates the connected dendritic valley network that carries the top weight. So the right job for noise in this pipeline is to supply roughness texture inside the uplift field (break the smoothness of primitives with a low-amplitude swiss or hybrid term) and the fine octaves after the solver, scaled by the slope and altitude gates, while stream power supplies the layout. Since `pow` redistribution and smoothing push β upward, measure after each tweak rather than assume.

## One real DEM tile is a base, an exemplar and a reference at once

For many maps the best base shape is a real one, resampled. The licensing is clean for a non-commercial mod. **USGS 3DEP (1 m lidar, 1/3 arc-second ≈ 10 m, 1 arc-second ≈ 30 m; CONUS only) is US Government public domain** with no attribution required; SRTM GL1 (30 m, 60°N–56°S) is "available for public distribution at no charge and contain no restrictions on data reuse"; ASTER GDEM v3 (30 m) has "no restrictions on reuse, sale, or redistribution"; **Copernicus GLO-30 (30 m, global DSM) is free but requires attribution to ESA/Copernicus**; ALOS AW3D30 needs a "© JAXA" credit; MERIT DEM (90 m) is CC BY-NC or ODbL ([AWS registry: USGS 3DEP](https://registry.opendata.aws/usgs-lidar/); [OpenTopography SRTM GL1](https://portal.opentopography.org/raster?opentopoID=OTSRTM.082015.4326.1); [gee-community ASTER](https://gee-community-catalog.org/projects/aster/); [Copernicus licence PDF](https://docs.sentinel-hub.com/api/latest/static/files/data/dem/resources/license/License-COPDEM-30.pdf); [JAXA AW3D30](https://www.eorc.jaxa.jp/ALOS/en/dataset/aw3d30/aw3d30_e.htm); [GEE MERIT](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/MERIT_DEM_v1_0_3)). Access routes are mature. **py3dep** (`pip install py3dep`) pulls 3DEP for a bbox or polygon at any resolution and returns xarray, with `check_3dep_availability` to see which resolutions cover an area ([py3dep docs](https://docs.hyriver.io/readme/py3dep.html)). The **OpenTopography Global DEM API** is a REST call with a bounding box, `demtype` (SRTMGL1, COP30, COP90, AW3D30, NASADEM, EU_DTM and others), output format and an API key; free non-academic keys allow **50 calls per 24 h**, and the 3DEP raster API serves 1 arc-second and 1/3 arc-second to all registered users while 1 m is academic-only ([OpenTopography developers](https://opentopography.org/developers); [3DEP API news](https://opentopography.org/news/api-access-usgs-3dep-rasters-now-available)). Copernicus GLO-30 sits on AWS as Cloud Optimized GeoTIFFs with 1024² internal tiles under `s3://copernicus-dem-30m`, which rasterio can window-read over HTTPS with no quota ([AWS registry: Copernicus DEM](https://registry.opendata.aws/copernicus-dem/)). The cell arithmetic **[inference]** is unforgiving: a 30 m DEM gives 67 cells over 2 km and 267 over 8 km, so SRTM and GLO-30 natively fit a 128–256 grid only for 4–8 km maps, while 3DEP 10 m fits 1.3–2.6 km; everything else needs amplification. The 30 m optical and radar products are surface models, so forested valleys read 20–30 m "filled in"; 3DEP is bare earth ([Wikipedia: DEM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_elevation_model)), which with the public-domain licence makes a US-first reference set the convenient default.

Picking the right tile is a landform-classification problem that is already solved at 90 m. **Geomorpho90m** (CC BY 4.0) provides 26 global derivatives of MERIT DEM at 90, 100 and 250 m, including slope, TRI, TPI, curvature and a **geomorphon layer with the ten classes flat, peak, ridge, shoulder, spur, slope, hollow, footslope, valley and pit** ([Amatulli et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7256046/); [Jasiewicz & Stepinski 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169555X12005028)). Hammond's scheme (slope + local relief + profile type, computed in a 9.65 km window, yielding plains, tablelands, hills, low mountains, high mountains) gives the vocabulary for tagging ([Hammond, OSU](https://ir.library.oregonstate.edu/downloads/4x51hk80p)). The region picker **[inference]** is a sliding window the size of the target map over the Geomorpho90m layers, tagging windows by class histogram and relief (fluvial hills: high valley+spur+slope, relief 100–400 m; mesa: bimodal elevation with flat at two levels and a shoulder/footslope ring; alpine: high peak/ridge, relief > 800 m), then fetching the fine DEM only for the winners. The same per-window statistics are the scoring vector `terrain_score.score` already computes, so the reference set and the picker share one code path. Shipped precedent is strong: FUEL's 14,000 km² "blends satellite data and procedurally generated data" with regions "modelled from satellite data", MotorStorm's Monument Valley was built from footage, Google Earth elevation and photographs, and Wildlands studied 11 Bolivian ecosystems by "heat, drought, wetness, erosion, rocks, vegetation, colors" ([VideoGamer FUEL interview](https://www.videogamer.com/previews/fuel-interview/); [Definition Magazine](https://definitionmagazine.com/features/making-of-motorstorm/); [80.lv Wildlands](https://80.lv/articles/landscape-and-material-pipeline-of-ghost-recon-wildlands)).

Adapting a tile is a short procedure **[inference, standard GIS practice]**: reproject to the patch's UTM zone with `rasterio.warp.reproject` into a square metric grid; use `Resampling.average` when shrinking and `cubic` or `lanczos` when enlarging; normalise by the 0.5–99.5 percentiles to kill lidar spikes; scale relief to the design range while clamping slopes that exceed the engine's limits; apply a morphological opening (minimum then maximum filter, radius 2–3 cells) as a cheap DSM-to-DTM fix on 30 m products; blend edges into generated terrain with a smoothstep over 10–15 % of the map width after matching mean and slope in the seam ring; run a few erosion iterations across the seam. Amplification is the one non-trivial step, and Guérin et al. 2016 is the method built for it: terrain as a sparse combination of dictionary atoms learned from exemplar DEMs, with "terrain amplification and synthesis from a coarse sketch" as the demonstrated applications; the official code is Matlab (`terrain_super_resolution(factor, exemplarhr, inputterrain, ...)`) and a current open-source C++ port in HighMap builds a dictionary of block-mean-downsampled exemplar patches paired with their high-res counterparts, codes input patches by orthogonal matching pursuit, applies the coefficients to the paired atoms and blends, completing **512² "in well under a second" with 225 atoms** and falling back to bicubic where the mask cannot reach ([Guérin blog](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eguerin/new/blog/sparse-representation-of-terrains-for-procedural-modeling/); [eric-guerin/terrain-amplification](https://github.com/eric-guerin/terrain-amplification); [HighMap PR 439](https://github.com/ottolink-dev/HighMap/pull/439)). Schott uses it as a ×8 amplifier after stream power ([Schott 2023, Sec. 7.5](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)). In Python this is `sklearn.linear_model.OrthogonalMatchingPursuit` over 8×8 patch pairs, about 150 lines **[inference]**, and the dictionary is literally the reference set of the chosen landform class, so the amplifier's "style" is whatever class the designer picked. Quality at factor 4–8 in one step is not documented in the fetched sources; chaining two ×2 passes with a short erosion pass between them is the safe assumption **[inference]**.

Biome and material layout at 1–8 km is a *local* climate field, because the whole map is one point on Whittaker's precipitation-versus-temperature diagram ([Wikipedia: Biome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biome)). Temperature falls with the lapse rate, **5.5 °C per 1000 m** in the alpine-climate reference, so a kilometre of relief spans only one or two Whittaker rows, enough for forest to subalpine to alpine on a mountain map and nothing on a hills map ([Wikipedia: Alpine climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_climate)). Moisture is distance to water and flow accumulation, Patel's rule for "distributing vegetation" in his polygon maps ([Polygon map FAQ](https://simblob.blogspot.com/2012/02/polygon-map-generation-faq.html)). The rule set to copy **[inference]** is `T = T₀ − L·(h − h₀)`, `M = a·log(flow accumulation) + b·(1 − dist_to_water/d_max) − c·insolation(aspect) + d·noise`, wetlands where accumulation is high and slope flat, a riparian strip within a few cells of channels, and a tree line as a T cutoff lowered on exposed ridges (high TPI) and raised in hollows. Wildlands placed materials "by slope, height, flow map, density map, deposition map, curvature" in per-ecosystem rule sets, and Horizon Zero Dawn had artists author placement rules in a graph editor driving GPU placement ([80.lv Wildlands](https://80.lv/articles/landscape-and-material-pipeline-of-ghost-recon-wildlands); [Guerrilla GDC 2017](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024700/GPU-Based-Run-Time-Procedural)). That is the vocabulary `terrain_erode.layer_masks` already uses (deposit, flow, debris, wear, slope); the biome layer's only addition is the T/M fields choosing *which* textures fill the cliff, deposit, wet, dry and snow slots, with four of the five rules kept per biome so the UE2 layer stack stays short **[inference]**.

## Machine learning: download two models, train nothing

The honest summary of the example-based and learned literature is that it offers realistic *texture* at a resolution the pipeline does not need, with *control* that is weaker than the physics route. Patch synthesis (Zhou 2007: real DEM patches placed under a sketched ridge/valley map, joined by graph cuts and Poisson editing; Tasse 2012: GPU patch merging under curvilinear features) is the most honest reuse of real relief because drainage is real by construction, but neither has public code, Zhou's promised World Machine plug-in never shipped, and Lochner's 2023 survey of the field says such methods do not "always respect global properties, such as realistic drainage patterns" ([howardzzh.com](http://www.howardzzh.com/research/terrain/); [Tasse 2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2012.03076.x); [Lochner 2023](https://people.cs.uct.ac.za/~jgain/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/lochner2023.pdf)). At 128–256 cells a single 3DEP tile holds thousands of 32–64 px candidate patches, so there is no data shortage, but a single patch would nearly cover the map; the Guérin amplifier above captures the useful part with far less code **[inference]**.

Among GANs, exactly one is both sketch-conditioned and downloadable. **Guérin et al. 2017's cGAN** ships a TensorFlow pix2pix fork with 16-bit PNG support, a training set (`deepterrains-dataset.tgz`) and a pre-trained model (`multi_train.tgz`) offered "for those who don't have a Titan X", trained 500 epochs; the artist sketches rivers, valleys, ridges and elevation cues ([Deep Terrains code and data](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eguerin/new/blog/deep-terrains-code-and-data/); [pix2pix fork](https://github.com/eric-guerin/pix2pix-tensorflow/tree/png16bits-support)). Lochner's 41-person study on 256² terrains found the cGAN "lacks any discernible notion of style" and "has a tendency to produce small grid artifacts", though these "can pass for plausible detail at medium viewing distances", and users could not reliably tell its plains, cliffs or mountains from real ones ([Lochner 2023](https://people.cs.uct.ac.za/~jgain/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/lochner2023.pdf)). dandrino's progressive GAN has weights but is unconditional and cost "around a week even with a beefy Nvidia Tesla GPU" to train on ~1,000 USGS NED tiles ([dandrino](https://github.com/dandrino/terrain-erosion-3-ways)). Spatial GANs show "noticeable noise in elevation values", "repeated small-scale undulations" and "struggle to generate globally coherent terrain structures, such as continuous ridgelines" ([IEEE CoG 2019](https://ieee-cog.org/2019/papers/paper_57.pdf)). A 4070's 12 GB equals the Titan X's, so retraining the Guérin cGAN would take hours to a day, but the pre-trained model makes that unnecessary **[inference]**.

Diffusion has displaced GANs since 2023, and three systems have downloadable weights. **xandergos's terrain-diffusion / InfiniteDiffusion (MIT)** refines coarse maps, procedural or hand-drawn, to 30 m/px heightmaps, upscaling 256× per axis; the two Hugging Face models are `terrain-diffusion-30m` (30 m/px, 7.7 km coarse cells, "best for playable worlds") and `terrain-diffusion-90m`; inference on a 3090 Ti takes 1.72 s to first tile and 0.66 s per subsequent tile at **peak VRAM ≈ 2,214 MB (1,846 MB in half precision)**, so it fits a 4070 trivially; training the pipeline took "approximately two weeks on an RTX 3090 Ti"; FID 14.78 versus 186.70 for Perlin blending; limitations are artefacts at low step counts and "Coarse-layer training data acquisition remains challenging" ([GitHub](https://github.com/xandergos/terrain-diffusion); [HF 30m](https://huggingface.co/xandergos/terrain-diffusion-30m); [arXiv 2512.08309](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08309)). **TerraFusion** (Stable-Diffusion-based LDM plus a ControlNet taking valley/ridge/cliff sketches, 512² heightmap plus texture, NASADEM-trained, weights on GitHub under PolyForm Noncommercial) infers in ~2 s at 20 steps but "Some heightmaps exhibit unnatural structures" and it lacks rivers and lakes; training took 2 + 7 days on 48 GB cards ([arXiv 2505.04050](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.04050v2); [millennium-nova/terra-fusion](https://github.com/millennium-nova/terra-fusion)). **MESA** (text to paired optical + elevation, SD 2.1, MIT, "Minimum 8GB VRAM") is text-conditioned and therefore the wrong control modality for this pipeline ([PaulBorneP/MESA](https://github.com/PaulBorneP/MESA)). Lochner et al.'s sketch-plus-style model, the closest match to a designer's ridge/drainage/cliff drawing, hit a hard **256² cap from GPU memory**, trained 72 h per model on a 20 GB A100 slice, and reports that elevation constraints at peaks and junctions were "impossible to achieve alignment", text conditioning "deeply disappointing", and small canvas edits cause "flickering and inconsistent outputs"; its code and Blender add-on are claimed available but no repository URL was found ([Lochner 2023](https://people.cs.uct.ac.za/~jgain/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/lochner2023.pdf)). The pattern across all of these **[inference]** is that altitude control is the weakest channel of sketch-conditioned generation and the strongest channel of the uplift/PID route, so ML belongs where it is good: as a *style amplifier* for a base the physics has already laid out. The practical use is to feed xandergos's model a 64² downsample of the stream-power result as its coarse map and A/B the output against the Guérin dictionary amplifier on the geomorphon EMD; if the diffusion output wins at the 30 m/px scale, it becomes the detail stage for 4–8 km maps, and nothing is trained on the 4070. Fine-tuning SD with ControlLoRA (~7 M parameters) is the fallback if style control is still lacking, and it is the only training that fits 12 GB comfortably ([ControlLoRA](https://github.com/HighCWu/ControlLoRA)) **[inference]**.

## Gameplay shaping is a post-pass with constraints frozen through erosion

The documented industry architecture is the same everywhere: terrain first, then linear features that locally override it, then point features scored against both, then regenerated masks. Far Cry 2's Guay described designers moving a road and "the terrain will actually smooth in according to how the rules were designed, and the vegetation will move and regrow", with a level designer and artist building 1 km² of jungle in about four hours ([Game Developer: MIGS Far Cry 2](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/migs-i-far-cry-2-i-s-guay-on-the-importance-of-procedural-content)). Wildlands' road builder "is basically a customized pathfinding algorithm that follows the terrain features" where "steep slopes or making lots of tight turns in the road costs more than shallow roads with less turns" and following an existing road is "dirt cheap", so networks branch; its settlements evaluate 70+ rules per building (slope, distance to centre and water, spacing between like buildings, churches not within ~150 m of each other), landmarks placed first ([80.lv Wildlands world building](https://80.lv/articles/procedural-world-building-in-ghost-recon-wildlands)). Andereck's thesis makes designer paths hard constraints that "terrain shape should not manipulate", solving the surrounding terrain to interpolate them ([Andereck 2014](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/etd/r/1501/10?clear=10&p10_accession_num=osu1388357258)). The slope budget comes from engine defaults: Unreal's walkable floor angle defaults to about 45°, UE3's `WalkableFloorZ = 0.7` (acos 0.7 ≈ 45.6°) with `MaxStepHeight = 35`, and academic generators keep slopes in "0–60 degrees in game units" ([Epic: Walkable Slope](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/walkable-slope-in-unreal-engine); [UE3 Pawn variables](https://wiki.beyondunreal.com/UE3:Pawn_internal_variables_(UDK)); [Doran & Parberry](https://ianparberry.com/pubs/terrain.pdf)). Unreal II's native constant was not found in a UE2-specific source; verify it in the Pawn code, and in the meantime keep designed routes under ~30°, optional shortcuts under ~40°, and treat >45° as a wall **[inference]**.

The recipes **[inference, consistent with the cited pipelines]** are short. Roads: A* over the heightmap with cost `length × (1 + k_s·slope²) + k_turn·|Δheading|`, existing roads at near-zero cost, then set the centreline to a smoothed profile with grade clamped (15–20 % for vehicles), blend terrain to it with a cosine falloff 1–3 cells either side, and mark the corridor in the `fixed` mask so droplets and thermal slumping do not wash it out; roads are computed *after* stream power (so they follow real valleys) and *before* droplet erosion (so the flattening survives). Building pads: cut-and-fill to the footprint's mean height, extend the flat 1–2 cells, blend over 2–4 cells, score sites by slope < 10°, road distance and like-building spacing, landmark first, and freeze them. Both are exactly Hnaidi strict-elevation and horizontal-gradient constraints, so the second diffusion-solver pass does them in one call. Terrain cover follows the Level Design Book's Uncharted-derived classes: high cover 1.75 m+, **low cover 1.0–1.25 m**, nothing below 0.5 m counts, with "less is more" because low cover "drastically increased options for the player" and dense grids read as an artificial "cover box"; Pears works with a **2 m cover spacing** and exteriors give "bigger spaces ... flanking opportunities, as well as ... a larger line of sight" ([Level Design Book: Cover](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/combat/cover); [Max Pears](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/engaging-level-design-for-combat---pt-2)). Stamped berms of 1.0–1.25 m inside arenas and 1.75 m+ at edges, 8–15 per 40×40 m arena, mostly waist-high, is a tuning start, not a published number **[inference]**.

Composition rules translate into terrain operations. A landmark reads only by local contrast ("A tall thing only seems special if it is surrounded by short things"), an **approach is "a path with a vista"** where a vista "offers an overview of the next area", and players won't use a sightline irrelevant to their goal ([Level Design Book: Composition](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/massing/composition)). Nintendo's Breath of the Wild "triangle rule" uses terrain masses at three scales so the player chooses to go over or around while the mass "obscures the player's view, so designers can utilize them to surprise players", chaining reveals "hill → bridge structure → tower" ([CEDEC 2017 translation](https://gist.github.com/idbrii/e39fe96279aa1670319bfa521d907399)). For the generator **[inference]**: enforce landmark contrast by lowering a ring around the landmark rather than raising it; place a medium ridge (2–4× player height) between approach and arena so the reveal is staged; require the approach point's viewshed to include both the landmark and the arena; and provide three arena templates as uplift-plus-constraint macros: a bowl (enemies on the rim, ambush beat), a saddle between two knolls (two flank routes), and a ridge approach (player high, vista over the camp). Halo 3's encounter model of declarative "territories" that squads fill in priority order suggests emitting named territories (a defensible high spot, a fallback, a flank) per arena for the UE2 AI scripting ([Game Developer: Halo 3 encounters](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/combat-evolved-the-encounter-design-of-halo-3)).

Playability is then measurable. Isovists (the set of points visible from a vantage) with area, perimeter and occlusivity metrics are the standard vocabulary ([Turner et al. 2001](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/160/1/turner-doxa-osullivan-penn-2001.pdf)); PCG evaluation suites use route redundancy, line-of-sight variety and landmark visibility ([arXiv 2404.18657](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.18657)); PathOS drives agents with player-type settings over a navmesh to show where players get stuck ([PathOS](https://github.com/samanthastahlke/pathos)). The metric set for this pipeline **[inference]**: route slope histogram along the beat path with a hard fail above the walkable angle; flood-fill reachability of every beat anchor from spawn (hard fail); isovist area sampled along the route with ~360 rays at 1.7 m eye height, expecting low values in the funnel and a spike at the approach; landmark-visible fraction of route samples; cover count and spacing per arena; fraction of the arena from which the spawn is visible; and edge-disjoint walkable routes between approach and arena (≥ 2 flanks). The project's existing U2Pilot driver can play PathOS's role by logging slope, stuck events and visible-landmark flags along the path.

## Recommended architecture and ordered build plan

The base-shape stage sits between the designer's sketch and `terrain_erode.erode`, and it is organised as five passes over the same 128–256 grid **[inference throughout this section unless cited]**: (1) **Sketch**, a small JSON/Python description of polylines (ridges, rivers, roads), polygons (plateaus, basins, arenas), points (peaks with heights, landmarks, buildings) and an optional real-DEM or noise "texture" source; (2) **Rasterise**, the Hnaidi diffusion solver turning curves into the uplift field `u₀` (ridge bands high, river corridors low, plateau blocks uniform, Génevaux-grown tributaries as extra corridors, swiss/hybrid noise at 10–20 % amplitude for roughness) plus a constraint list; (3) **Form**, `stream_power(zeros, uplift=u₀, ...)` run for 300–1,000 steps with the PID wrapper for peak heights and Cordonnier's noise-modulated slope cap; (4) **Detail**, the chosen amplifier (Guérin dictionary from the reference class, xandergos diffusion for 4–8 km maps, or gated noise octaves) followed by the existing droplets and thermal pass; (5) **Shape for play**, A* roads, pads and berms as strict constraints solved by the diffusion solver against the eroded field and frozen in `fixed`, then `layer_masks`, the biome T/M fields, and `score` plus the playability metrics. Real DEM tiles enter at pass (2) as an alternative source of `u₀` (a tile's smoothed elevation works as uplift and reproduces its drainage layout) or at pass (3) as an initial `h` with a short run, and at pass (4) as the amplifier dictionary. Everything stays NumPy/SciPy; PyTorch is needed only if the xandergos option is exercised.

The ordered build plan, cheapest and highest-payoff first:

1. **Uplift primitives and the flat-start run (1 day, ~120 lines).** Segment, disc and valley primitives on the grid using `distance_transform_edt` from rasterised curves; `np.maximum` for ridges, multiplicative low-uplift corridors for rivers with width ∝ A^0.4 (Schott), a border of zero uplift as the outlet; call the existing `stream_power` on zeros with `uplift`, k from `h_max ≈ 2.244·u/k` in normalised units, `diffusion` 0.03–0.05, 300–600 steps ([Cordonnier 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Cordonnier-Computer_Graphics_Forum-2016-Large_Scale_Terrain_Generation_from_Tectonic_Uplift_and_Fluvial_.pdf); [Schott 2023](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)). Expected result: dendritic relief with ridges and rivers where drawn, Horton Rb in 3–5. Score it with `terrain_score.score` immediately so the baseline is recorded.
2. **PID elevation constraints (half a day, ~50 lines).** Wrap `stream_power` in 10-step chunks, apply Schott's `K_p = 0.01, K_i = 0.001, K_d = 0.01` on smooth-step point supports of radius 3–6 cells, freeze the resulting ũ into u₀ at convergence; keep constraints non-overlapping or equal-height where they overlap ([Schott 2023, Sec. 6.4](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1)).
3. **Hnaidi diffusion solver (1–2 days, ~150 lines).** Sparse system with Dirichlet, one-sided-gradient and Laplacian rows, `spsolve`; recipes for ridge, riverbed, cliff, hill and plateau from the paper's constraint tuples ([Hnaidi 2010](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2010-terrain.pdf)). Use it first to smooth the uplift field between primitives (replacing hand-tuned blends), then as the post-erosion exact-geometry pass for plateaus, cliffs and pads.
4. **Reference set and DEM base path (1–2 days, ~150 lines).** `py3dep.get_dem` for US tiles and a rasterio COG window reader for Copernicus GLO-30; UTM reprojection, percentile normalisation, average/cubic resampling, morphological-opening DSM fix, seam blending; store 20–50 tiles per landform class as `.npy` with metres per cell; a Geomorpho90m window picker tagging fluvial-hills, mesa, alpine and coastal candidates ([py3dep](https://docs.hyriver.io/readme/py3dep.html); [AWS Copernicus DEM](https://registry.opendata.aws/copernicus-dem/); [Geomorpho90m](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7256046/)). This unblocks the scorer's "no reference DEM set yet" caveat at the same time.
5. **Noise library with measurement (1 day, ~200 lines).** One derivative-capable gradient-noise primitive; hybrid (H 0.25, offset 0.7), ridged (H 1, offset 1, gain 2), derivative-damped (f 1.98, s 0.49), swiss (gain 0.5, warp 0.15), jordan (gain1 0.8, warp0 0.4, damp0 1.0), Parberry exponential gradients (μ ≈ 0.9), FFT synthesis at a chosen β, terraces and `pow` redistribution; 7 octaves for 256 cells, domain rotated ~30° per octave ([musgrave.c](https://engineering.purdue.edu/~ebertd/texture/1stEdition/musgrave/musgrave.c); [iquilezles.org/morenoise](https://iquilezles.org/articles/morenoise/); [decarpentier.nl](https://www.decarpentier.nl/scape-procedural-extensions); [Parberry](https://jcgt.org/published/0004/02/01/paper.pdf); [Bourke](https://paulbourke.net/fractals/noise/)). Fix the `psd_slope` convention and target β₁D ≈ 2.0–2.2. Use the library for uplift roughness and post-solver detail only.
6. **Gameplay post-pass (2 days, ~300 lines).** A* roads with slope² and turn costs on the formed terrain, profile smoothing, cosine re-flow, corridor into `fixed`; pad siting and cut-and-fill via the diffusion solver; berm stamps; the three arena macros; reachability, route-slope, isovist and landmark-visibility metrics ([80.lv Wildlands](https://80.lv/articles/procedural-world-building-in-ghost-recon-wildlands); [Level Design Book: Cover](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/combat/cover)).
7. **Guérin dictionary amplifier (1–2 days, ~150 lines).** 8×8 patch pairs from the class reference set, `OrthogonalMatchingPursuit` with sparsity 1–3, mask-weighted blending, two ×2 passes with a short erosion pass between, bicubic fallback ([HighMap PR 439](https://github.com/ottolink-dev/HighMap/pull/439)). Compare against gated noise on geomorphon EMD.
8. **Génevaux network growth (2–3 days, ~250 lines), only if emergent rivers disappoint.** Grammar with ζ, P_c/P_s/P_a, e = 3–6 cells, η = σ = ¾e, Lipschitz κ from the terrain-slope map; output edges as valley primitives for step 1 rather than building its Voronoi crests ([Génevaux 2013](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf)).
9. **Learned detail trial (half a day, no training).** Install xandergos terrain-diffusion, feed a 64² downsample of the formed terrain as the coarse map, compare the 30 m/px output against step 7 on the scorer; optionally run the Guérin 2017 cGAN's `multi_train.tgz` on the same sketch for a second A/B ([xandergos](https://github.com/xandergos/terrain-diffusion); [Deep Terrains](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eguerin/new/blog/deep-terrains-code-and-data/)). Proceed to ControlLoRA fine-tuning only if both lose on style and the physics route cannot be tuned to the reference class.
10. **Biome T/M fields and material slot assignment (1 day, ~100 lines).** Lapse-rate temperature, flow/distance moisture, aspect insolation, tree line by TPI; per-biome choice of four of the five mask rules ([Wikipedia: Alpine climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_climate); [80.lv Wildlands](https://80.lv/articles/landscape-and-material-pipeline-of-ghost-recon-wildlands)).

## Conclusion

The research reframes the base-shape question. The pipeline does not need a new generator next to its erosion stage; it needs to run its erosion stage from a flat start under a designed uplift field, which converts the solver it already has into the most controllable and most hydrologically honest base-shape method in the literature. Every other family then finds its proper place: diffusion curves are the sketch rasteriser and the exact-geometry finisher, noise is roughness inside the uplift and detail after it, real DEMs are the reference set and the amplifier dictionary, and learned models are a weekend A/B rather than a training project. The control the designer actually wants, "ridge here, river there, plateau at this height, peak this tall, road from A to B", decomposes cleanly into uplift primitives, PID constraints, strict-elevation solves and frozen corridors, and each of those is a day or less of NumPy against existing functions.

The implication for effort is that the first two days (uplift primitives and PID on top of `stream_power`) should produce maps that beat anything the noise bases have scored so far on the landform-share and Horton metrics, because the dendritic network is what those metrics reward and what uplift-driven stream power produces by construction. Measure after each build step, keep the geomorphon EMD against the reference class as the objective, and let the scorer, not the literature, decide whether the dictionary amplifier or the diffusion weights win the detail stage.
