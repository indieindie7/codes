# Fix the feet, then swap the costs

Agents in Advent Rising can move far more convincingly without new animation tech. Two cheap layers give most of the gain. The first is a **post-animation bone layer** written in UnrealScript: speed-matched playback, lean, foot IK with a pelvis drop, and spring hit reactions. Players read "proper walking" mainly from the feet, which is why motion matching matches foot positions and velocities and little else ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/most-inspiring-game-animation-tech-talks-of-2016)). The second is **tactical routing**: a change of strategy becomes a change of path-cost profile plus a position query, not a new algorithm, which is how Killzone, Crytek's TPS and Unreal's EQS all work ([Killzone GDC05](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/gdce05_killzone_ai.pdf); [Jack, Game AI Pro ch. 26](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter26_Tactical_Position_Selection.pdf)). Build 2226 already exposes the levers both layers need. On the movement side there are `SetBoneRotation`, `GetBoneCoords` and `Trace`. On the routing side there are per-node `ExtraCost`, `TransientCost`, `FearCost` and `bBlocked` and per-edge `ReachSpec.Distance`, all writable before the native `FindPathTo`. Stock `SquadAI` and `Bot` code already writes these costs. The heavier options rank much lower. Powered ragdolls exist only as an impulse approximation (prototype built, untuned, crash-prone). Procedural hound stepping is hard. SIMBICON-style balance walking, neural motion models and RL policies are out of reach in Karma and a 2005 frame budget. The recommended order is to validate the native cost hooks first, then cost profiles and position queries wired into ModMinds' existing tasks, then lean, speed matching and standing foot IK, and only then powered ragdolls and creature stepping. Every slice has a hidden pilot test built from `ModPilot` steps, `MINDLIST`, `SHOTP` and `AdventNative.log`.

## Feet and weight sell the walk, and a script layer can supply both

The research converges on one cheap architecture: let the authored clip play, then correct the result with a post-process pass. It has four parts. A ground probe, analytic two-bone leg IK and pelvis lowering make feet touch the ground. Foot locking stops skating during contact. Stride or speed warping makes foot spacing match the capsule's speed. A damper keeps mesh and capsule together. Daniel Holden's foot-locking recipe is the clearest description. Lock the foot at its ground-projected position when contact begins. Hold it with an inertializer while locked, unlock when contact ends or the lock drifts past an `unlock_radius`, and pin the heel with two-joint IK. He also warns that foot locking "is hard to make look good" and that a little sliding often beats a distorted pose ([Holden](https://theorangeduck.com/page/code-vs-data-driven-displacement)). His capsule-sync rule matters for Advent's native movement. Pull the mesh toward the simulation with a damper `1 − 2^(−dt/halflife)`, rotate more slowly than you translate, and **cap each correction at half the current speed times dt**, so a standing or turning character barely slides ([Holden](https://theorangeduck.com/page/code-vs-data-driven-displacement)). Epic's AnimationWarping plugin shows these layers are standard production practice. Its stride warping uses stride scale = locomotion speed / root-motion speed. Its orientation warping turns the legs toward the travel direction and counter-twists the spine, with a 90° guard, so strafing needs fewer clips ([Epic: Pose Warping](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/pose-warping-in-unreal-engine)). UE5 also ships foot-placement lock types ([Epic API](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/API/Plugins/AnimationWarpingRuntime/EFootPlacementLockType)). David Rosen's Overgrowth talk argues that a few keyframes plus simple procedural layers can carry a whole game ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/video-an-indie-approach-to-procedural-animation)). Its technical details sit behind GDC Vault and were not verified.

Advent's engine fits this pattern closely, with one structural limit. Locomotion selection is native. It drives a 4-way blend on channels 4–7, and script only picks clip names and speeds. Native code calls `InputSpeedChange(speed)` (`EonPawn.uc:284`), which reaches `AdventPawn.animateMovement` (`AdventPawn.uc:4267`). That in turn calls `AnimateRunning/Jogging/Walking/Crouching`, which set `MovementAnims[]`, `GroundSpeed_F/B`, `BaseAnimSpeed_*` and the `SpeedChangeMin/Max` thresholds that re-fire the event (local source `AdventRising_src`, see `advent_hooks.md`). **There is no foot IK, slope adaptation or lean in the game.** `PlayerController.ikTest` only zeroes limb rotations, and only `lastFloorNormal` is stored. Additive bone work already runs on top of the clips: head/neck look-at, the Aurelian cape spring chain, the Seeker's native spine flinch, and ModReact's spring flinch (`SetBoneRotation(..., 0, alpha)`, `ModReact.uc:499–510`). That proves the mechanism. The limit is that every animation, Karma and navigation native is `final`, the mod cannot replace `AdventPawn` (Mutator replacement never runs in this build), and there is **no two-bone IK solver and no per-bone physics blend**. So every correction must be computed in script from a ticking `Info` and written through bone controllers that run after the animation.

That shapes the feasibility verdicts. **Lean is a clear yes**: roll and pitch the spine and hips by a damped function of lateral acceleration and yaw rate, the same mechanism as the flinch. **Speed matching is a likely yes**, because playback rate and per-gait speed are script variables. But `animateMovement` rewrites them whenever `InputSpeedChange` fires, so the mod must re-apply after it or use `SetAnimRate` on channels 4–7, and which works needs a test. **Foot IK is partial.** Per tick it means `GetBoneCoords` on hip, knee and ankle, one `Trace` per foot, a law-of-cosines solve, and `SetBoneRotation` on UpLeg/Leg/Foot, plus a `PrePivot` drop for the pelvis. The risks are a frame of lag (bone coordinates come from the last pose) and unknown `Space` conventions per call. So start with standing and idle poses on slopes and stairs, not running. Foot locking needs contact timing. Here the notes conflict. The hook recon found `AnimNotify_FootStep` calling `AdventPawn.FootStepping(boneName)` (`animNotify_FootStep.uc:13`, `AdventPawn.uc:740`). ModGore's footprint code, however, says "the game has no footstep notify" and counts steps by distance (`ModGore.uc:734`). Either the notify is missing from the clips enemies use, or ModGore missed it. Since the mod cannot override `FootStepping` anyway, detect contact the way Holden annotates clips automatically: foot height and velocity below thresholds ([Holden](https://theorangeduck.com/page/code-vs-data-driven-displacement)). Treat the notify, if a pilot run proves it fires, as a cross-check.

## Physics should flavour reactions, not carry the walk

The physics literature splits sharply between what ships and what stays in papers. The practical tier is "physical animation": per-joint springs drive a ragdoll toward the animated pose, as in UE5's PhysicsControl, where damping ratio 1 is critical and a velocity multiplier tracks the clip ([Epic API](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/API/Plugins/PhysicsControl/FPhysicsControlData?lang=en-US)). Full balance control is another league. SIMBICON's walker needed **a 5 ms ODE step, PD torques τ = kp(θd − θ) − kd·θ̇ on every joint, at least 90 Nm or it "becomes weak-kneed and falls"**, plus world-frame torso and swing-hip control with balance feedback θd = θd0 + cd·d + cv·v ([SIMBICON](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2007-siggraph-simbicon.pdf)). Euphoria's reputation in GTA IV rests on stumbles, wound-grabbing and recovery, not on the ordinary walk ([NaturalMotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NaturalMotion)). The lesson is that physical motion reads as real through weight transfer, recovery steps and limbs that collide. All of those show up in reactions and deaths, not in locomotion.

Karma narrows the options further. The script API offers `KAddImpulse(impulse, pos, BoneName)`, `KSetSkelVel`, `KAddBoneLifter` and `KScaleJointLimits`, but **no per-bone motor target and no anim/physics blend weight** (`Actor.uc:1163–1193`). Epic's own docs warn that the largest-to-smallest mass ratio must stay at or below 100 ([UDN KAT](https://udn.epicgames.com/Two/KarmaAuthoringTool.html)). Creating a ragdoll when the queue is full is unpredictable, and destroying an actor inside `KImpact` crashes ([Unreal Wiki](https://unrealarchive.org/wikis/unreal-wiki/Legacy:Karma_Functions_And_Events.html)). AdventMod has its own traps on top of those. `KIsAwake` always returns false, `KWake` does nothing, `KFreezeRagdoll` drops bodies through the world, hound ragdolls crash, and the clip-to-ragdoll handoff crashed twice. ModReact already contains the only viable script approach: `bPoweredRagdoll`, per-bone impulse springs toward `GetBoneLocationAtFrame` in `Steer` (`ModReact.uc:961`), tuned by `PowerSpring`, `PowerDamp`, `PowerMaxAccel` and `PowerTime`. It is off by default because the gains are untuned. A gain of 1e7 crashed and 2e5 launched the body. The research gives the tuning rule: keep the gain near critical damping, kd ≈ 2·√(kp·I), against Karma's real substep. In practice, cap acceleration rather than raising the spring, and fade the push over about 0.5 s (my synthesis from the SIMBICON and PhysicsControl parameters above).

Data-driven locomotion is mostly out. Motion matching itself is cheap, a nearest-neighbour search over foot positions and velocities, hip velocity and future trajectory. But it needs 5–10 minute unstructured mocap takes and a "jump to frame N of clip S and blend" playback path ([Clavet, GDC 2016 report](https://cgworld.jp/feature/1604-gdc2016-03-2.html)). Advent has authored `.psa` clips, not long takes, and `SetAnimFrame` plus `TweenAnim` would be a crude inertializer at best. PFNN costs 0.8–1.8 ms per character on modern hardware ([PFNN slides](https://www.theorangeduck.com/media/uploads/other_stuff/pfnn_slides.pdf)). DeepMimic-class policies train for about 60 M samples in a different simulator ([arXiv 1804.02717](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.02717)) and would not transfer to Karma. The one data-driven idea worth keeping is Holden's MIT-licensed `spring.h` damper and inertialization maths ([orangeduck/Motion-Matching](https://github.com/orangeduck/Motion-Matching)). It is compatible with the mod's GPL licence and useful for every layer above. Hounds are the hard middle case. Procedural walkers converge on one recipe: plant each foot until it drifts from a predicted rest target (rest offset + velocity × lookahead, raycast down), step along an arc, alternate leg groups, and let body height and tilt follow the foot plane through a spring ([Spider Walker](https://digitalproduction.com/2026/08/28/spider-walker-automates-multi-leg-animation/)). In Advent that means either disabling native movement anims (`Controller.bControlAnimations`, `Pawn.bPhysicsAnimUpdate`) and posing about 14 bones per tick in script, or, far more realistic, a correction layer of per-leg IK plus spine pitch on slopes over the native gait.

| Technique | Verdict for Advent | Hooks | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lean / bank, look-at | Yes | `SetBoneRotation` on spine, hips, head | Bone axis conventions |
| Speed/stride matching | Likely | `InputSpeedChange` → `animateMovement`, `BaseAnimSpeed_*`, `SetAnimRate` | Native code re-sets the values |
| Foot IK + pelvis drop (standing first) | Partial | `GetBoneCoords`, `Trace`, `SetBoneRotation`, `PrePivot` | One-frame lag, `Space` semantics |

| Powered ragdoll via impulses | Yes, fragile | `KAddImpulse`, `GetBoneLocationAtFrame`, `Steer` | Crashes, launch at high gain |
| Hound step correction layer | Hard | Per-leg IK over native gait, `SetBoneRotationOnly` spine | Leg crossing, native spine bend |
| Native PD ragdoll / Jolt via DLL | Later, big | Ghidra on Karma exports | Unknown motor API |
| SIMBICON, PFNN/LMM, DeepMimic | No | — | Sim step, data, CPU |

## Strategy changes are cost profiles plus position queries

The pathfinding half of the question has a firmer answer, because shipped games agree on it. Killzone's team put the problem bluntly: "Shortest paths often aren't tactically sound." Their fix was plain A* with extra cost on links that cross a friendly line of fire or are under fire. A **64 KB polar line-of-fire table for 4000 waypoints** made those costs cheap: per waypoint and radial sector, the largest distance from which an attacker in that sector can fire at it. The slides call it "inaccurate, but consistent", and it drove up to 14 AIs on a PS2 ([Killzone GDC05](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/gdce05_killzone_ai.pdf)). Brewer formalises the cost as `exposedLength × coverBias + (segmentLength − exposedLength)`, plus a designer "unsafe" penalty. He keeps biases at or above 1, so the distance heuristic stays admissible, and warns that over-inflating costs makes A* explore far more nodes ([Brewer, Game AI Pro ch. 27](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter27_Tactical_Pathfinding_on_a_NavMesh.pdf)). Van der Sterren's budget for a 2001-era waypoint shooter is the right scale for Advent. He measured **about 3,000 A* searches/s and 50,000 traces/s, or roughly 15 searches and 250 traces per AI tick at 10 Hz**. He stored sparse per-waypoint visibility lists rather than an N² matrix, and lookups ran about 1000× faster than pathfinding ([van der Sterren](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/terrain-reasoning-for-3d-action-games)).

Choosing *where* to go is the other half, and it is a weighted sum over candidate points. Killzone's attack-position function scores proximity 20, line of fire to the primary threat 40 with partial cover (20 without), cover from secondary threats 20, and preferred range 10, with weights set per action and per personality ([Killzone GDC05](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/gdce05_killzone_ai.pdf)). Crytek's TPS splits a query into generation, conditions and normalised weights, each multiplied by a signed user weight. A query is a list of options, where option 2 is the fallback when option 1 returns nothing, and a failed "flank left" query is fed back to abort the squad action. Jack's performance rules carry straight over to UnrealScript. Run cheap filters first, then weights and a sort, then raycasts from the best score down, stopping at the first pass. Time-slice the work, and keep a chosen spot until a cheap re-validation fails, so agents do not twitch between spots ([Jack ch. 26](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter26_Tactical_Position_Selection.pdf)). EQS is the same pattern ([Epic EQS](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/environment-query-system-overview-in-unreal-engine)). Dave Mark's influence maps supply the shared layer that both scoring and path cost read. There is a proximity map and a threat map per faction, stamped from templates with falloff `Max − Max·(d/D)` or `(d/D)^4`, refreshed about once a second, and combined with add, multiply and invert. The zero crossing between ally and enemy influence is a front line that agents can push to, avoid or flank ([Mark, Game AI Pro 2 ch. 30](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter30_Modular_Tactical_Influence_Maps.pdf)).

Path diversity and flanking need no new algorithm either. Killzone 2's squads each ran a strategic pathfinder whose costs included the paths of squads planned earlier. These "squad corridors" mean squads "actively pick paths to avoid other squads", and sub-objectives carried multiple approach routes for flanking ([Killzone 2 MP bots](https://www.slideshare.net/guerrillagames/killzone-2-multiplayer-bots)). A common practitioner recipe for flanks is A* in "flank mode", with high cost on nodes in an arc in front of the target ([GameDev.net](https://humor.gamedev.net/forums/topic/673823-shooter-game-flanking-algorithm-or-way-of-implementation), informal source). No primary source describes an explicit pincer algorithm. It follows from two opposite-side flank queries where the second is planned after the first's corridor is penalised. The ordered vocabulary is therefore the deliverable, and the profiles below are illustrative starting weights, not sourced values. Push uses `L·(1 + 0.5·exposure)` toward the best attack position. Hidden uses `L·(1 + 8·exposure)` and filters out nodes visible to any enemy. Fall back uses `L·(1 + 4·exposure)` plus a penalty for closing on threats, toward a safe-withdraw spot. Flank uses `L·(1 + exposure)` plus a large penalty for the enemy's front arc and the squad's main corridor. Pincer is two flanks on opposite sides of the enemy-to-squad line. Grid techniques (JPS, flow fields) do not apply to a sparse PathNode graph. The graph analogue of a flow field is a single-source Dijkstra tree, which Killzone 2 cached per squad ([Harabor & Grastien](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/7994); [Killzone 2 MP bots](https://www.slideshare.net/guerrillagames/killzone-2-multiplayer-bots)). The recent LLM-commander work, such as StarCraft II planners over deterministic executors, confirms the layering rather than replacing it. A slow planner issues symbolic orders, and the fast cost-and-query layer executes them, never in the per-tick loop ([arXiv 2508.10428](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.10428v1)).

## Advent's native A* already has the knobs, and ModMinds already has the tasks

The decisive local finding is that strategy-switchable routing may cost almost nothing in Advent. `NavigationPoint` declares `ExtraCost`, transient `TransientCost` and `FearCost`, `bBlocked`, and an event `SpecialCost(Pawn, ReachSpec)`. `ReachSpec.Distance` is non-const, so edge weights are writable. Stock code uses these fields as tactical costs. `SquadAI.AddTransientCosts` sets `TransientCost = 1000·f` on squad mates' move targets, which is path diversity (`SquadAI.uc:3156`). `Bot.uc:11179` raises `FearCost = 2·FearCost + 600` where a bot dies, and `GameInfo.Timer` decays it by 0.95. `ExtraCost` is 100 on doors, 400 on lifts and 2500 on claimed action points (local source, `AdventRising_src`). That is strong circumstantial evidence that the native A* adds these fields, but it is **unverified for build 2226**. `TransientCost` is probably cleared per search, as in UT2004, so it must be set immediately before `FindPathTo`. `SpecialCost` is only called for special specs such as `JumpSpot`/`JumpDest`, not ordinary PathNodes, and `PathList` is const, so no edges can be added. The fallback is also available. The full graph is readable through `Level.NavigationPointList`, `PathList`, `ReachSpec.Start/End/Distance/reachFlags`, so a time-sliced script A* with per-agent, per-link costs can feed `MoveToward` node by node. Its throughput in UnrealScript is unmeasured, and the runaway-loop limit is about 1M iterations.

ModMinds is the natural consumer, because its weak points are exactly what this research fixes. Its cover finder already filters path nodes and cover points: hidden at crouch height, shot available one step aside, reachable in a straight run, not much nearer the enemy, unclaimed. That is a TPS query with hard-coded weights. Its flanker goes about 75° round the enemy to the nearest node that sees the enemy, leg by leg via `NextLeg`. `NextLeg` calls `M.B.FindPathTo(Dest)` and takes `RouteCache[1]` when the first node is underfoot (`ModMinds.uc:619–632`). Its own design notes list the gaps: "moves are straight `MoveTo` legs", cover must be in a straight line, and flanks re-path every leg (`AI-MINDS-DESIGN.md` §6). The cost-profile approach drops in at that single function. `NextLeg(M, Dest)` becomes `NextLeg(M, Dest, Profile)`. It writes the profile's costs onto the nodes, calls `FindPathTo`, restores the costs, and logs the route. Each existing task then gets a profile. Pinned and cover use *hidden*, fall back uses *fallback*, the flanker uses *flank* with the squad corridor penalised, and charge uses *push*. Hounds circle with an inverted, *exposed* query, matching DOOM's "exposed cover" for creatures that should never hide (earlier AI report). This also answers "change strategy on request". An order is a profile token plus a query, and any source can issue it: the director, a squad territory switch, or a pilot console command.

The order follows a simple rule: cheapest proof first, and visible payoff before fragile tech. R0 is a one-session experiment that decides the whole routing architecture. If the native search honours `TransientCost` and `ExtraCost`, R2–R6 run at native speed and R8 never gets built. R2 and R3 are what make "change strategy on request" real, and they plug into the AI roadmap at three points. The **director (AI slice 7)** issues profile tokens instead of only adjusting budgets: Build-Up allows push and flank, Peak Fade forces fallback and hidden, and Relax holds squads on hidden spots. **Squad territories (AI slice 8)** become a hard filter in the R4 query, so the forward, fallback and last-stand node sets bound where profiles may send a squad. The **flanker role** gets its wide, consistent swing from the R2 flank profile plus the R6 corridor penalty, instead of TLOU's fixed combat vector. The **search slice (AI slice 6)** reuses R5's node arrays for its diffusing probability. The **damage bucket (AI slice 4)** is where L2's lean and L5's powered ragdoll meet the minds: a falter adds a lean impulse, and a stagger death hands off to the powered ragdoll. On the locomotion side, L0–L2 come first because they cost little and show on every enemy. L3–L4 follow once bone `Space` conventions are pinned down by the `BONETEST` logs. L5 waits for the Karma fixes in the existing physics plan (per-character joint limits, `KSetSimParams`). L6 comes last, because the native hound spine bend and the hound ragdoll crash make it the riskiest slice.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of "agents that move dynamically" for this mod. The impressive-sounding tier of motion matching, neural controllers and balance-walking ragdolls needs data, simulation steps or native access Advent does not have. What it does have is a writable cost on every path node and a writable rotation on every bone. Both levers are post-hoc corrections on top of systems the mod cannot replace. That turns out to be how the shipped games did it too: Killzone bent plain A* with costs, and UE5 bends clips with warping. The practical consequence is that one unknown dominates the plan, whether build 2226's native search reads the cost fields. A single `ROUTETEST` run answers it before any architecture is committed.

The deeper implication is that movement and strategy should share one vocabulary. A profile token such as *hidden*, *fallback* or *flank* can set path costs, choose the position query, pick the gait (walk or crouch for hidden, run for push) and select the bark, all from one director or squad decision. That makes a strategy change legible in where creatures go, how they move and what they say, the three channels the earlier AI research found players actually read. Building that shared token early in R3 costs nothing extra and keeps every later slice, from lean to the director, wired to the same switch.
