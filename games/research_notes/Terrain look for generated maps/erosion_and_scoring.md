# Terrain formation models (noise, erosion, strata) and realism scoring for 256x256 heightmap generators

Scope: (A) cheap, documented procedural models of how real terrain forms, concrete enough for a few hundred lines of NumPy; (B) how to score a generated heightmap (and renders of it) against copyright-free DEMs and photos so parameters can be tuned by measurement. Everything marked **[own suggestion]** is my inference, not a published result. All equations below were read from the cited papers/code unless flagged.

Notation used throughout: `h` = terrain height grid, `w`/`d` = water depth, `s` = suspended sediment, `N` = grid side (256 here), `dt` = timestep.

---

## A1. Noise bases: fBm, ridged multifractal, hybrid multifractal, domain warping

### Takeaway
fBm with gain 0.5 per octave (H = 1, spectral falloff ~ -9 dB/octave) is the graphics default and matches measured mountain profiles; ridged noise (`1 - |noise|`) makes sharp crests; Musgrave's hybrid/ridged multifractals make smooth valleys with rough peaks; domain warping (`f(p + fbm(p))`) bends the straight-ish structure of plain noise into folded, tectonic-looking shapes. None of these produce drainage networks; that is why erosion is needed afterwards (PTRM study, A-B below).

### Cited Findings
- fBm = sum of noise octaves with frequency x lacunarity and amplitude x gain each octave; gain `G = 2^-H`, H in [0,1]; H = 0.5 is Brownian; spectrum decays as `f^-B` with `B = 2H+1`; "G=0.5 (H=1) dominates computer graphics" and "measured mountain profiles confirm -9dB/octave", i.e. isotropic self-similarity. — [Inigo Quilez, fBm](https://iquilezles.org/articles/fbm/)
- Olsen's spectral synthesis: amplitude per octave `A = p^i` with persistence p = 0.5 approximating 1/f noise; cubic interpolation best, linear 5x faster; diamond-square with uniform (not Gaussian) offsets is 4x faster and acceptable; base terrain = 2/3 smoothed diamond-square + 1/3 Voronoi with coefficients c1 = -1, c2 = 1 (gives ridge lines and connected riverbeds), then a perturbation (domain-warp) filter of magnitude 0.25 (max displacement N/4 cells), which maximised his "erosion score". — [Olsen 2004, Realtime Procedural Terrain Generation](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf)
- Red Blob Games recipe: octave amplitudes `[1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125...]` (or `[1, 0.5, 0.33, 0.25, 0.2]` for more fine detail), normalise by the amplitude sum; redistribute elevation with `pow(e * 1.2, exponent)` with exponent 1.0-3.0+ (higher pushes mid-elevations down into valleys); ridged noise `2*(0.5 - abs(0.5 - noise))`; add ridged detail only on high ground by multiplying higher octaves by the accumulated elevation; terraces `round(e*n)/n`, n = 2-15; island shaping `d = 1-(1-nx^2)(1-ny^2)` and `e = lerp(e, 1-d, mix)`; wavelength = map_size/frequency. — [Red Blob Games, Making maps with noise functions](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/terrain-from-noise/)
- Musgrave's fBm code: `exponent_array[i] = pow(frequency, -H)`, `frequency *= lacunarity`; "lacunarity is a non-issue, as we almost always leave it set at a value very close to 2.0"; an ordinary fBm patch has "fractal dimension ~2.1"; uniform fractal dimension everywhere is unrealistic ("Real mountains ... rise out of flatter terrain and have rolling foothills"), motivating heterogeneous (multifractal) terrain where higher frequencies are scaled by the lower-frequency value. HybridMultifractal "good parameter values to start with: H: 0.25, offset: 0.7"; the Bryce "ridges" model is a hybrid multifractal with basis `1 - |Perlin|`, "Mordor" uses Worley's Voronoi distance-squared basis. — [Musgrave, Procedural Fractal Terrains (course notes)](https://www.classes.cs.uchicago.edu/archive/2015/fall/23700-1/final-project/MusgraveTerrain00.pdf)
- RidgedMultifractal recommended parameters in Musgrave's original C: H = 1.0, offset = 1.0, gain = 2.0 (lacunarity ~2). — [musgrave.c, Texturing & Modeling 1st ed. source](https://engineering.purdue.edu/~ebertd/texture/1stEdition/musgrave/musgrave.c) (parameter values per search summary; the file itself is the authoritative source)
- Domain warping (dandrino): "fBm offset by another fBm noise map" gives a warped, tectonically deformed look; ridge noise = take `abs()` of each noise layer and invert the result. — [terrain-erosion-3-ways README](https://github.com/dandrino/terrain-erosion-3-ways)
- Inigo Quilez's domain-warping article (`f(p + fbm(p))`, nested `f(p + fbm(p + fbm(p)))` with scale factors ~4.0) could not be fetched (404 at the time); the technique is referenced by dandrino above. — [iquilezles.org/articles/warp](https://iquilezles.org/articles/warp/) (unverified this session)

### Inferences
- **[own suggestion]** Mapping to landform types, for a 256x256 grid at (say) 2-4 km across: rolling hills = plain fBm, H ~ 0.8-1.0, 5-6 octaves, then `pow(e,1.5-2)`; alpine ridges = ridged multifractal or `1-|noise|` with amplitude gated by elevation (Red Blob trick); mesas/plateaus = low-octave fBm terraced (`round(e*n)/n`) then thermal erosion to put talus on the step faces (see A3/A4); folded/tectonic look = domain warp with warp amplitude 0.1-0.25 of the map (Olsen's 0.25 maximum).
- **[own suggestion]** Because all of these are ~1/f^2 random fields, they will already pass a power-spectrum test (B2) while failing drainage tests — so do not tune noise against spectra alone.

### Gaps
- Quilez's warp article could not be fetched; the exact nested-warp constants (4.0 scale, specific offsets) are from memory and unverified.
- Musgrave's RidgedMultifractal code text was not in the fetched course-notes PDF; values come from the cited `musgrave.c` via a search summary.

## A2. Hydraulic erosion: pipe/shallow-water grid model (Mei 2007, Št'ava 2008, Jákó 2011), droplet/particle model (Beyer 2015, Lague, McDonald), and uplift + stream power (Cordonnier 2016, Schott 2023)

### Takeaway
Three implementable families: (1) the virtual-pipe grid model — 5 steps per iteration, fully vectorisable in NumPy, with a complete published parameter table (Jákó & Tóth 2011, dt = 0.02, Kc = 1, Ks = 0.5, Kd = 1, Ke = 0.015, Kr = 0.012, A = 20, g = 9.81, ~1000 iterations); (2) the droplet model — tens of thousands of independent droplets, each a 30-step walk with inertia, capacity = f(slope, speed, water), erosion brush radius 2-3 (Beyer/Lague defaults listed below), giving the best-looking gullies cheaply; (3) stream-power + uplift — `dh/dt = U - K A^m S^n` on a D8 drainage graph with m/n ~ 0.5, which produces real dendritic valley networks and a steady-state mountain range, the thing noise never gives.

### Cited Findings

**Pipe model (Mei, Decaudin, Hu 2007; as restated with values by Jákó & Tóth 2011)**
- Per-cell state: terrain height b, water height d, suspended sediment s, outflow flux `f = (fL, fR, fT, fB)`, velocity v. Iteration = (1) rain, (2) shallow-water flow via virtual pipes (flux + water height + velocity), (3) erosion/deposition, (4) sediment advection, (5) evaporation. — [Jákó & Tóth, Fast Hydraulic and Thermal Erosion on the GPU (CESCG 2011)](https://old.cescg.org/CESCG-2011/papers/TUBudapest-Jako-Balazs.pdf)
- Rain: `d1 = d + dt * r(x,y) * Kr` (constant rain per cell rather than random drops, "more balanced and finer grained results").
- Flux: `fL(t+dt) = max(0, fL + dt * A * g * dhL / l)`, with `dhL = b(x,y)+d1(x,y) - b(x-1,y) - d1(x-1,y)`; scale all four fluxes by `K = min(1, d1*lx*ly / ((fL+fR+fT+fB)*dt))` so outflow cannot exceed the water present; `dV = dt*(sum f_in - sum f_out)`; `d2 = d1 + dV/(lx*ly)`; velocity `vx = 0.5*(fR(x-1,y) - fL(x,y) + fR(x,y) - fL(x+1,y))` (y likewise). — same source
- Capacity: `C = Kc * sin(alpha) * |v|` with a lower clamp on sin(alpha) "to allow some erosion at nearly flat areas"; if `s < C` erode `Ks*(C - s)` from b into s (times local hardness R), else deposit `Kd*(s - C)`; Jákó adds a water-depth factor to C and a "softening" rule `R(t+dt) = max(Rmin, R - dt*Kh*Ks*(s - C))`; sediment advected semi-Lagrangian `s(t+dt)(x,y) = s1(x - u*dt, y - v*dt)` with bilinear interpolation; evaporation `d(t+dt) = d3*(1 - Ke*dt)`. — same source
- Published parameter table (range; typical value): dt [0;0.05] 0.02; Kr rain [0;0.05] 0.012; Ke evaporation [0;0.05] 0.015; A pipe area [0.1;60] 20; g [0.1;20] 9.81; Kc capacity [0.1;3] 1; Ks suspension [0.1;2] 0.5; Kd deposition [0.1;3] 1; Kh softening [0;10] 5; Kdmax max erosion depth [0;40] 10; Ka talus tangent coeff [0;1] 0.8; Ki talus tangent bias [0;1] 0.1; Kt thermal rate [0;3] 0.15. Figures show results "after 1000 iterations" on a diamond-square base. CPU timings: 256x256 = 102-325 ms/iteration single-thread C++ (so ~1000 iterations is minutes on CPU; NumPy vectorised should be similar or faster). — same source, Table 1 & 2
- Mei et al. 2007 original: "based on the velocity field of the running water, which is created with an efficient shallow-water fluid model", using O'Brien's virtual pipes for parallelism. The original PDF/HAL pages were blocked (Anubis/403) this session; equations above are from Jákó's restatement, which states its hydraulic part "is the method introduced by Mei, Decaudin and Hu". — [Mei 2007 listing](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Fast-Hydraulic-Erosion-Simulation-and-Visualization-Mei-Decaudin/6bd6dc1ff1259029422934480b76a61e53f86174); [UnityTerrainErosionGPU (open-source pipe model + thermal, parameters Kc, Ks, Kd, Ke, Kr, Kt, talus scale & bias in `Shaders/Erosion.compute`)](https://github.com/bshishov/UnityTerrainErosionGPU)
- Št'ava et al. 2008: layered terrain (up to 4 material layers), two coupled hydraulic models — "Areas where the motion is slow become more eroded by the dissolution erosion, whereas in the areas with faster motion, the force-based erosion prevails" — plus slippage: `dd = dt*(dh - l*tan(alpha))` if `dh > l*tan(alpha)` else 0, with "for dry sand we take the talus angle of 30°"; runs >= 20 fps at 2048x1024 with 4 layers on GPU; 256x256 costs ~1.2-1.7 ms/iteration total on their 2008 GPU. They note "user studies are required to assess its actual usefulness". — [Št'ava 2008, Interactive Terrain Modeling Using Hydraulic Erosion](https://cgg.mff.cuni.cz/~jaroslav/papers/2008-sca-erosim/2008-sca-erosiom-fin.pdf)
- Olsen's simpler cellular hydraulic model (Beneš & Forsbach style): rain `w += Kr`; dissolve `h -= Ks*w; m += Ks*w`; move water to lower neighbours to level total altitude `a = h + w` (`dw_i = min(w, da) * d_i/d_total`), sediment follows water `dm_i = m*dw_i/w`; evaporate `w *= (1-Ke)`; capacity `m_max = Kc*w`, deposit excess. Constants Kr = 0.01, Ks = 0.01, Ke = 0.5, Kc = 0.01 on a [0,1] height range, N = 512. — [Olsen 2004](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf)

**Droplet / particle model**
- Sebastian Lague's C# port of Beyer's algorithm, defaults: erosionRadius 3, inertia 0.05, sedimentCapacityFactor 4, minSedimentCapacity 0.01, erodeSpeed 0.3, depositSpeed 0.3, evaporateSpeed 0.01, gravity 4, maxDropletLifetime 30, initialWaterVolume 1, initialSpeed 1. Per step: direction = lerp(old dir, -gradient, by inertia) and normalise; move 1 cell; deltaHeight = newH - oldH; capacity = max(-deltaHeight, minSlope) * speed * water * capacityFactor; if sediment > capacity or moving uphill, deposit `(sediment - capacity)*depositSpeed` (or fill the hole if uphill); else erode `min((capacity - sediment)*erodeSpeed, -deltaHeight)` spread over a weighted brush of radius 3; `speed = sqrt(speed^2 + deltaHeight*gravity)`; `water *= (1 - evaporateSpeed)`. — [SebLague/Hydraulic-Erosion Erosion.cs](https://github.com/SebLague/Hydraulic-Erosion/blob/master/Assets/Scripts/Erosion.cs)
- erodr (C implementation of Beyer 2015) defaults: 70,000 particles, ttl 32, radius 2, initial velocity 0.9, initial water 1, gravity 1, inertia 0.3, capacity 8, evaporation 0.02, erosion 0.7, deposition 0.3, min slope 0.0001. — [henrikglass/erodr](https://github.com/henrikglass/erodr)
- Beyer's thesis PDF (firespark.de) could not be fetched (expired certificate); the two implementations above are faithful ports and give the parameter ranges. — [Beyer 2015 thesis link](https://www.firespark.de/resources/downloads/implementation%20of%20a%20methode%20for%20hydraulic%20erosion.pdf) (unverified this session)
- Nick McDonald's "simple particle-based hydraulic erosion": per particle `speed += dt * normal.xy / mass (mass = volume*density)`, `pos += dt*speed`, `speed *= (1 - dt*friction)`; equilibrium sediment `c_eq = volume * |speed| * (h_old - h_new)` (clamped >= 0); `cdiff = c_eq - sediment`; `sediment += dt*depositionRate*cdiff`; `h -= dt*volume*depositionRate*cdiff`; `volume *= (1 - dt*evapRate)`; ~200,000 particles for very good results; it "is not capable of simulating actual fluid flow and rivers". — [Simple Particle-Based Hydraulic Erosion](https://nickmcd.me/2020/04/10/simple-particle-based-hydraulic-erosion/)
- McDonald's "Procedural Hydrology" extension: stream map (time-averaged particle positions, decaying at lrate = 0.01) and pool map; particles that stop flood (pool fill with approach = 0.5, drainage = 0.001, spill = 5 cycles); parameters coupled to hydrology: `effD = depositionRate*(1 - treedensity)`, `effF = friction*(1 - 0.5*stream)`, `effR = evapRate*(1 - 0.2*stream)`; volumeFactor 100; 256x256 grid in examples. The 2023 update "removed flooding due to reliability issues" and added explicit momentum and discharge maps producing "river meandering". — [Procedural Hydrology](https://nickmcd.me/2020/04/15/procedural-hydrology/); [SimpleHydrology repo](https://github.com/weigert/SimpleHydrology)
- dandrino grid simulation (NumPy): per iteration add water (uniform random), compute gradient, capacity from slope/velocity/volume, erode or deposit, displace water and sediment downhill, evaporate a fraction; README gives no coefficients ("vectorized implementation of the code found on [this page]"). — [terrain-erosion-3-ways](https://github.com/dandrino/terrain-erosion-3-ways)

**Uplift + stream power**
- Stream power incision: `E = K A^m S^n` (A = upstream drainage area, S = slope); "the most common choice of exponents satisfies m/n = 0.5"; K values used in LEM runs with m/n = 0.5 were 1.00e-12 s^-1 and 2.83e-11 s^-1 for different uplift scenarios; with hillslope diffusion neglected, m/n = 0.5 makes the landscape "invariant to horizontal stretching" (a 1 km and a 100 km domain have the same relief pattern) — so add diffusion for realism. — [Kwang & Parker 2017, ESurf](https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/5/807/2017/); [Wikipedia, Stream power law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_power_law)
- Cordonnier et al. 2016: "the first method in computer graphics that combines uplift and hydraulic erosion"; given a user-painted uplift map it builds a stream graph "embedding elevation information and stream flow", applies the stream power equation, and converts the graph to a DEM by "blending landform feature kernels"; gives "high-level control over large scale dendritic structures of river networks, watersheds, and mountain ridges". The PDF (HAL/Purdue) could not be fetched (blocked / >10 MB), so k, m, n and node counts are not quoted here. — [Cordonnier 2016 project page](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/publications/cordonnier2016large/); [HAL record](https://inria.hal.science/hal-01262376)
- Schott et al. 2023 (ToG): models terrain "in the uplift domain", simulates stream power erosion interactively with "a fast yet accurate approximation of drainage area and flow routing", supports inverse modelling (recover uplift from a real DEM) and "hydrologically consistent blending between terrain patches"; open-source code (C++/CMake, paint uplift with Ctrl+click). — [project page](https://aparis69.github.io/public_html/projects/schott2023_Uplift.html); [H-Schott/StreamPowerErosion](https://github.com/H-Schott/StreamPowerErosion)
- Braun & Willett 2013: O(N), fully implicit, parallel solver for the stream power equation (implicit Euler + Newton-Raphson allows long timesteps); FastScape implementations exist in Fortran/C++/Python. — [Braun & Willett 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169555X12004618); [FastScapeLib](https://fastscape.org/fastscapelib-fortran/)
- Priority-Flood depression filling (needed before D8 routing so every cell drains): O(N log N), "pseudocode is only 20 lines", works on 4-/8-connected grids. — [Barnes, Lehman, Mulla 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0098300413001337)
- Pelletier 1997: a model with diffusion on hillslopes and diffusivity proportional to sqrt(discharge) in channels reproduces both `S(k) ∝ k^-2` topography and the observed river-network scaling laws (Tokunaga side-branching); random-phase synthetic k^-2 topography "resemble[s] natural topography" but "the lack of any river networks ... indicates that the power spectrum is not a complete representation". — [Pelletier, Why is topography fractal?](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/9705033)

### Inferences
- **[own suggestion]** Minimal stream-power loop for 256x256 in NumPy: (i) start from fBm scaled to ~0-1 plus a smooth uplift map U (e.g. Gaussian blob or painted); (ii) priority-flood fill; (iii) D8 receivers and drainage area A by accumulating in descending height order (one `argsort`); (iv) explicit update `h += dt*(U - K*A^m*S^n) + D*laplacian(h)` with m = 0.5, n = 1, D small (hillslope diffusion); (v) repeat 200-1000 steps, choosing dt so that `K*A_max^0.5*S_max*dt < ~0.5 cell` for stability (or implement Braun-Willett's implicit per-node update `h_i = (h_i + dt*U + dt*K*A^m*h_rcv/dx) / (1 + dt*K*A^m/dx)` for n = 1, which is unconditionally stable and only a few lines once nodes are processed from outlets upward). K in the normalised unit system is a free knob — tune it by the B2 metrics, not by geological units.
- **[own suggestion]** For "looks eroded now" on small grids, the droplet model is the best cost/benefit: 256x256 with 50k-100k droplets x 30 steps is ~10^6-10^7 cell updates, seconds in pure NumPy if droplets are batched (vectorise across droplets per step). Use stream power for the valley layout, droplets for gullies, thermal (A3) to clean up over-steep cells and make scree.
- **[own suggestion]** Pipe model is the one to use if you want water/flow accumulation maps that respect ponding (lakes), since the other two do not model standing water well; use Jákó's table as the starting point and reduce Kc/Ks if 256x256 over-channelises.

### Gaps
- Mei 2007 and Cordonnier 2016 primary PDFs were inaccessible (bot-blocked / size limit); Cordonnier's actual k, m, n, dt, node counts and lake handling are not quoted. Schott 2023's README does not list numeric defaults; they live in the source.
- Beyer's thesis parameter table was not read directly (certificate error); the Lague/erodr defaults are faithful ports but differ from each other (inertia 0.05 vs 0.3, capacity 4 vs 8), so treat them as ranges.
- No source gave a tested "iterations vs grid size" rule for 256x256 beyond Jákó's 1000-iteration figures and Olsen's 50-iteration thermal result.

## A3. Thermal erosion / talus (Musgrave 1989; Olsen 2004; Jákó 2011; Houdini) and sediment deposition producing flat valley floors, alluvial fans, beaches

### Takeaway
Thermal erosion is a 10-line cellular rule: if the height difference to a lower neighbour exceeds the talus threshold, move a fraction c of the excess downhill (c = 0.5 stable; Olsen's fast variant moves to the lowest neighbour only and stabilises in ~50 iterations). Material-specific angles of repose are published (dry sand 34°, soil 30-45°, talus 33-37°). Flat valley floors, fans and beaches come from deposition rules: capacity dropping with slope/velocity (pipe/droplet models), a "base level" below which downcutting stops and deposits begin (Gaea), and inhibited transport producing fans (Gaea "Inhibition").

### Cited Findings
- Musgrave, Kolb & Mace 1989 introduced a "physical erosion model which simulates hydraulic and thermal erosion processes to create global stream/valley networks and talus slopes" with local control of fractal dimension; the PDF is paywalled at ACM. — [SIGGRAPH history entry](https://history.siggraph.org/learning/the-synthesis-and-rendering-of-eroded-fractal-terrains-by-musgrave-kolb-and-mace/); [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/74334.74337)
- Olsen's reference thermal erosion (Moore 8-neighbourhood): for each neighbour `d_i = h - h_i`; if `d_i > T` then `h_i += c*(d_max - T) * d_i/d_total` (d_total = sum of d_i > T); "A reasonable value for c is 0.5; higher values may cause oscillation"; `T = 4/N`. Fast variant: Von Neumann 4-neighbourhood, move only to the lowest neighbour, move more per iteration, update in place; 6x faster, "most of the change is done during the first 50 iterations ... no more than 50 iterations of any of the versions are needed". — [Olsen 2004](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf)
- Jákó's parallel thermal: `H = max(b - b_i)` over 8 neighbours; volume moved `dS = a*dt*Kt*R(x,y)*H/2` ("This is the maximum, otherwise the algorithm will oscillate"); recipients = lower neighbours with `tan(alpha) > R*Ka + Ki` (hardness-dependent talus angle; Ka = 0.8, Ki = 0.1, Kt = 0.15); share proportional to height difference; moved through 8 "virtual pipes" so the update is write-independent. — [Jákó & Tóth 2011](https://old.cescg.org/CESCG-2011/papers/TUBudapest-Jako-Balazs.pdf)
- Št'ava slippage rule: `dd = dt*(dh - l*tan(alpha))` when `dh > l*tan(alpha)`; dry sand talus 30°; in multi-layer setups slippage is computed per layer. — [Št'ava 2008](https://cgg.mff.cuni.cz/~jaroslav/papers/2008-sca-erosim/2008-sca-erosiom-fin.pdf)
- Angle of repose table: dry sand 34°, wet sand 45°, water-filled sand 15-30°, crushed gravel 45°, natural gravel with sand 25-30°, soil 30-45°. — [Wikipedia, Angle of repose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_repose)
- Talus/scree slopes: the "steep upper rectilinear segment of talus slopes most commonly rests at approximately 33-37°", upper part 32-37° (up to 40°), medial ~33°, lower part low angles with basal concavity. — [Talus slopes (ResearchGate chapter, via search)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/371031125_Talus_slopes)
- Alluvial fan slopes average ~5°, range <1° to 25° but rarely >10°; streamflow fans generally <3-4°, debris-flow fans 6-8°+. — [Wikipedia, Alluvial fan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alluvial_fan); [DTIC, Physical characteristics of alluvial fans](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/AD0627707.pdf)
- Beach-face slope rises with grain size: 0.01 (0.6°) for very fine sand to 0.83 (40°) for boulders; for sand (0.0625-2 mm) coarser = steeper is well established. — [Bujan et al. 2019, Marine Geology](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0025322718304365)
- Houdini HeightField Erode Thermal: computed "by comparing the height field (including debris) against a blurred version"; where height exceeds the blurred height the cell is "exposed" and erodes; parameters Cut Angle (stop cutting beyond it), Repose Angle ("0° for water, 30-45° for realistic dirt or rock"), Global Erosion Rate (m per iteration), Removal Rate (debris deleted, default 0), Spread Iterations (slumping steps), Max Debris Depth. Erode 3.0 outputs layers height, sediment (hydro-deposited), debris (thermal-deposited), flow, flowdir; hydro parameters include Bank Angle, Rainfall Coverage, Erosion/Deposition/Removal/Evaporation rates. — [HeightField Erode Thermal](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/nodes/sop/heightfield_erode_thermal.html); [HeightField Erode 3.0](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/nodes/sop/heightfield_erode.html)
- Gaea Erosion node: Duration default 0.04; Rock Softness; Strength (sediment transport capacity); Downcutting (vertical depth and transport distance); Inhibition "restrains downcutting by slowing undissolved sediments, enabling both deep channels and alluvial fans"; Base Level "sets the lowest elevation threshold where downcutting ceases and deposits begin"; Feature Scale in metres (width of valleys/ridges); Volume mode makes wider channels with more deposits. — [Gaea Erosion reference](https://docs.quadspinner.com/Reference/Erosion/Erosion.html)
- World Machine Erosion: Rock Hardness (higher = less erosion but "deeper, steeper gullies"), Sediment Carry Amount (more = "greater sediment deposition in valleys"), Channel Depth, Erosion-time Intensifier (exponential strengthening), Uplift during simulation, filter types "Simple Filter: rounded, soil-mantled hillslopes" vs "Inverse Filter: steepened features suited to ice-carved terrain". — [World Machine Erosion (Legacy)](https://help.world-machine.com/topic/device-erosion/)

### Inferences
- **[own suggestion]** Translate angles to grid thresholds: with cell size `dx` metres and height in metres, `T = dx * tan(angle)`; use per-material T from the table (sand 34°, scree 35°, soil 30-45°, hard rock: effectively no thermal erosion, or 60-80° "cut angle" to leave cliffs). Olsen's `T = 4/N` on a unit-height map is equivalent to about 45° on a square unit map of side 1 (4/N cells per cell step at slope 1), i.e. a generic rock/soil value.
- **[own suggestion]** Flat valley floors with the droplet model: force deposition when slope < s_floor (e.g. 2-3°, matching stream-flow fan slopes) by using `capacity = max(-dh, minSlope)*...` with a slightly larger minSlope and higher depositSpeed near low flow-accumulation thresholds; alternatively run a final "base level" pass (Gaea idea): any cell below a chosen elevation receives deposits until slope <= 1-3°. Beaches: same base-level pass at sea level with target slope 1-5° depending on intended grain size.
- **[own suggestion]** Alluvial fan: where a channel (high flow accumulation) exits onto low slope, deposit a cone with slope 3-8° and radius growing with upstream area; cheap to do as a post-process on the flow-accumulation map.

### Gaps
- Musgrave 1989's actual talus-angle constants and iteration counts were not read (paywalled).
- No published source was found giving a quantitative recipe for alluvial-fan or beach deposition in a heightmap simulator; the fan/beach rules above are my suggestions constrained by the measured slope ranges.

## A4. Rock strata, hardness layers, mesas/buttes, and deriving texture masks from the simulation

### Takeaway
Layered-material terrains (Št'ava 2008's 4 layers, Peytavie's Arches with stacked material/water/air layers, Cordonnier 2017's bedrock + granular + vegetation layers) are the published way to get hard caprock, cliffs and scree: erosion rate and talus angle are read from the top layer at each cell, so a hard layer protects what is under it (mesa) and soft layers retreat and feed talus. Texture masks are the standard outputs of commercial erosion nodes: Flow (where water ran), Wear (bedrock removed), Deposition/Sediment (where it settled), Debris (thermal talus).

### Cited Findings
- Št'ava 2008: terrain "composed of layers of materials", up to four, each with its own erosion behaviour and talus angle; slippage computed per layer; "forming rivers, drying flooded areas, rain ... adding obstacles" as editing operations. — [Št'ava 2008](https://cgg.mff.cuni.cz/~jaroslav/papers/2008-sca-erosim/2008-sca-erosiom-fin.pdf)
- Peytavie et al. 2009 (Arches): a hybrid model combining "a volumetric discrete data structure that stores the different materials and an implicit representation"; expands "stacked layers by incorporating water and air layers, thereby enabling caves and overhangs"; includes a rock-pile generation technique that avoids physically based simulation. — [Arches (Galin's page PDF)](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2009-arches.pdf); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2009.01385.x)
- Cordonnier et al. 2017: "four types of layers: bedrock, granular material, vegetation and dead vegetation, with elevation determined by the sum of the first two layers"; vegetation controlled by moisture and sun exposure; bidirectional feedback between erosion and vegetation (rock, sand, humus, grass, shrubs, trees). — [Purdue publication page](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/publications/cordonnier2017authoring/); [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3072959.3073667)
- Jákó 2011 uses a per-cell hardness `R(x,y)` multiplying both hydraulic suspension and thermal volume, and a hardness-dependent talus angle `tan(alpha_min) = R*Ka + Ki` (Ka 0.8, Ki 0.1) — so hard cells both erode slower and hold steeper slopes. — [Jákó & Tóth 2011](https://old.cescg.org/CESCG-2011/papers/TUBudapest-Jako-Balazs.pdf)
- Paris et al. 2019 (Terrain Amplification with Implicit 3D Features) amplifies heightfields with "slot canyons, sea arches, stratified cliffs, fields of hoodoos, and complex karst cave networks" via an implicit model (3D, beyond heightmaps). — [ACM full text](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3342765); [code](https://github.com/aparis69/Implicit-Volumetric-Terrains)
- Gaea Erosion outputs Wear ("portions where erosion removes sediments"), Deposits ("resting position of those sediments"), Flow ("path of the sediments from their original location to the final resting position"); "By using the masks indicating areas of wear, deposition, and flow, you can create much more interesting terrain textures than a typical height and slope distribution." — [Gaea Erosion](https://docs.quadspinner.com/Reference/Erosion/Erosion.html); [Gaea Deposits node](https://docs.quadspinner.com/Reference/Erosion/Deposits.html)
- World Machine masks: Flow ("areas where water flow has occurred"), Wear ("bedrock has been eroded"), Deposition ("sediment has been deposited"); Mask Output Structure controls grain (grainy/small-scale to averaged/large-scale). — [World Machine Erosion](https://help.world-machine.com/topic/device-erosion/)
- Houdini: separate sediment (hydraulic deposits) and debris (thermal deposits) layers plus flow and flowdir. — [HeightField Erode 3.0](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/nodes/sop/heightfield_erode.html)
- Geomorphon landform classes computable from any DEM (flat, peak, ridge, shoulder, spur, slope, hollow, footslope, valley, pit) with GRASS defaults search = 3 cells, skip = 0, flat = 1°. — [r.geomorphon](https://grass.osgeo.org/grass-stable/manuals/r.geomorphon.html)

### Inferences
- **[own suggestion]** Strata for a heightmap: define hardness as a function of height (and optionally a tilted/warped coordinate for dipping beds): `R(h) = 1 - 0.7*softness_profile(h)` where the profile is a periodic band pattern (e.g. 3-6 bands across the height range, hard bands thin). Feed R into Jákó's rules (erosion x R, talus tan = R*Ka + Ki). Soft bands retreat into slopes, hard bands remain as ledges/cliffs — stratified cliffs for free. Mesa/butte: a thick hard caprock band near the top plateau plus terraced base noise.
- **[own suggestion]** Mask derivation, all cheap from the simulation state: rock = cumulative erosion (wear) high AND slope > ~35°; scree = thermal debris layer thickness > threshold (slope ~30-37°); soil/grass = hydraulic deposit layer thickness > threshold AND slope < ~25°; wet/dark = flow accumulation (or droplet visit count / stream map) above a percentile, dilated 1-2 cells; snow/bare = elevation band. Blend masks with `smoothstep` and small noise to avoid hard edges. Geomorphon classes give an alternative purely geometric mask set (valley/hollow -> moist; ridge/spur/peak -> bare).

### Gaps
- Arches and Cordonnier 2017 PDFs were not read in full; the exact hardness/erodibility numbers per material were not extracted.
- No source gives a validated "hardness profile" for a mesa; the band design is my suggestion.

## B2. DEM-side metrics that correlate with "looks like real terrain" (spectrum/Hurst, slope and curvature statistics, drainage statistics, hypsometry, geomorphons/PTRM)

### Takeaway
Cheap NumPy metrics with published reference ranges: power-spectrum exponent beta ~ 2 (measured 1.88-2.1 for Earth; equivalently H ~ 0.45-0.55, fractal dimension D = 3 - H ~ 2.45-2.55 for the surface, or 2.2-2.4 by other estimators — estimators differ, so compare generated vs reference with the SAME estimator); slope histograms unimodal with right skew (normal-ish in uplifting ranges, exponential in depositional areas); Horton bifurcation ratio 3-5, length ratio 1.6-2.4; hypsometric integral 0.3-0.6 for mature landscapes; and PTRM, a published perceptual-realism regression on the 10 geomorphon class fractions (R^2 = 0.72 against a 70-person study) that penalises noise terrains lacking valleys/ridges/hollows.

### Cited Findings
- Earth topography transects: `S(k) ∝ k^-2` ("exponent close to -2"); Turcotte: "self-affine fractal with a fractal dimension close to 2.5"; synthetic random-phase k^-2 surfaces lack river networks, so "the power spectrum is not a complete representation". — [Pelletier 1997](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/9705033)
- Global ETOPO2v2 fit gives beta = 1.88(10); roughness exponent H ~ 0.55 implies beta = 2H+1 = 2.1. — [Nature Sci. Rep. 2016, Earth-atmosphere scaling](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep34005)
- Relations: `D = 2 - H = (5 - beta)/2` for a profile (1-D transect); H from PSD `H = (beta - 1)/2`; smaller H = rougher. "Real landscapes are said to have Hurst exponents of about 0.7", but may show "considerably flatter structure functions than 0.7" (lower H). — [arXiv 1611.06190, Fractal dimension of self-affine signals](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.06190); [arXiv cond-mat/0009383](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0009383)
- Slope frequency distributions of landforms are "mostly unimodal ... with a long tail and right skewness"; in the Olympic Mountains 100 km^2 slope histograms are "approximately normal or exponential" in uplifting vs depositional areas; Oregon Coast Range local slope distributions normal, left-skewed where gentler. — [Montgomery 2001, Slope distributions, threshold hillslopes](https://earth.geology.yale.edu/~ajs/2001/Apr_May/qn10t100432.pdf); [Global Land Slope Frequency Dataset](https://www.geodoi.ac.cn/WebEn/HTML_INFO.aspx?Id=e52f0330-c40c-4486-b835-2fea2801fc9e)
- DEM resolution changes slope statistics (100 m DEM: 26.9-39.9°, 10 m DEM: 33-40.7° in the same area) — compare at matched resolution. — [ESRI knowledge base via search](https://support.esri.com/en-us/knowledge-base/how-to-calculate-the-average-slope-angle-of-an-area-usi-000029992)
- Horton ratios in natural basins: bifurcation ratio typically 3.0-5.0 (literature 3.3-4.7; "between 2 and 5"); length ratio 1.6-2.4. — [Horton laws for hydraulic-geometric variables, NPG 2014](https://npg.copernicus.org/articles/21/1007/2014/npg-21-1007-2014.pdf); [Geba basin morphometry, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9792793/)
- Hypsometric integral classes: young/inequilibrium HI >= 0.6 (convex curve); mature 0.3-0.6 (S-shaped); old/monadnock HI < 0.3 (some use 0.35). — [opengeology.in](https://opengeology.in/hypsometric-curve-and-integral/); [Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2022.827836/full)
- Olsen's "erosion score" `eps = sigma_s / mean_s` on the slope map (std/mean of slope) — higher for eroded terrain (steep walls + flat floors); base terrains scored 0.40-0.67; midpoint-displacement noise ~0.40-0.48, noise+perturbed Voronoi 0.657 (N = 512). Intended as a game-suitability proxy, not validated against real DEMs. — [Olsen 2004](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf)
- Geomorphons: line-of-sight in 8 directions, zenith/nadir angles -> ternary 8-tuple; 498 unique patterns -> 10 landforms (flat, peak, ridge, shoulder, spur, slope, hollow, footslope, valley, pit); GRASS defaults search = 3 cells, skip = 0, flat = 1°, dist = 0. — [r.geomorphon](https://grass.osgeo.org/grass-stable/manuals/r.geomorphon.html)
- PTRM (Rajasekaran, Kang, Benes, Čadík, Galin, Guérin, Peytavie, Slavík): 2AFC study on Mechanical Turk ("Which terrain looks more realistic (left or right)?"), 70 "Masters" participants, |R| = |S| = 150 DEMs (real: SRTM aeolian/coastal/glacial/fluvial/slope; synthetic: Perlin, ridged Perlin, fBm, thermal erosion, fluvial erosion via Št'ava's implementation, coastal erosion), all rendered identically from ~45°. Linear regression of perception score on the 10 normalised geomorphon fractions: `PTRM = (-38.02 + 3.55*G_depression + 1.75*G_summit + 25.12*G_flat + 9.61*G_valley + 7.59*G_ridge + 6.71*G_hollow + 9.02*G_spur + 7.31*G_shoulder + 28.95*G_slope + 7.63*G_footslope) / 69.96`, R^2 = 0.72, standard error 0.13, all 10 predictors significant (F = 153.5, p < 0.01); 5-fold 80/20 validation gave near-identical coefficients. Example values: real Arizona terrain PTRM = 0.76 (ranked top 78%), thermal-erosion synthetic 0.51 (49%), synthetic fluvial 0.23, synthetic coastal 0.24; real terrains have "strong presence of valleys, ridges, and hollows" that synthetic ones lack; CycleGAN-transferring real features raised a synthetic terrain to 0.69. — [PTRM arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04610); [ACM ToAP](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3514244)
- Argudo, Guérin, Schott, Galin 2025 (EG STAR): a review of terrain metrics from geomorphology for synthesis/analysis, with all metrics implemented, performance comparisons, "a correlation matrix to help identify instances where simpler, faster metrics can serve as proxies for more computationally intensive ones", and an artist study. — [CGF 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cgf.70080); [code](https://github.com/oargudo/terrain-descriptors)
- Galin et al. 2019 (EG STAR "A Review of Digital Terrain Modeling") organises methods into procedural, simulation and example-based; it is the standard survey. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cgf.13657); [UCT open copy](https://pubs.cs.uct.ac.za/id/eprint/1337/)

### Inferences
- **[own suggestion]** Scoring recipe, DEM side, all NumPy/SciPy, < 1 s per 256x256 map:
  1. Normalise height to the reference's relief (or compare relief-independent stats only).
  2. Spectrum: `P(k)` by 2-D FFT, radially binned, fit log-log slope over k = 4..64 cycles/map; target beta_2D = beta_1D + 1 ~ 3 (i.e. radial-average of the 2-D PSD falls ~k^-3) — but simplest is to fit the same estimator on reference DEMs and take the distance `|beta_gen - beta_ref|`.
  3. Slope histogram (degrees, 36 bins) and plan/profile curvature histograms: Earth mover's distance (`scipy.stats.wasserstein_distance`) to the pooled reference histogram; also check skewness sign (should be right-skewed, unimodal).
  4. Drainage: priority-flood fill, D8, flow accumulation; threshold at accumulation > ~0.5-1% of cells to get channels; Strahler order; Horton Rb and Rl via log-linear fit; target Rb 3-5, Rl 1.6-2.4. Also drainage density (channel cells / area) vs reference.
  5. Hypsometric integral `(mean - min)/(max - min)`; target the class you want (0.3-0.6 mature).
  6. Geomorphons (search 3 cells, flat 1°) -> 10 fractions -> PTRM via the published coefficients; plus EMD between the generated and reference 10-bin geomorphon histograms (this is the discriminating feature the PTRM authors found).
  7. Ridge/valley sharpness **[own]**: histogram of profile curvature at ridge and valley cells (from geomorphon classes); real eroded terrain has sharp V valleys (large negative curvature concentrated in channel cells) and relatively rounded ridges unless alpine.
  Combine as a weighted sum of normalised distances; weight geomorphon/PTRM and Horton terms highest, spectrum lowest (noise passes it trivially, per Pelletier).
- **[own suggestion]** Report the PTRM number alongside the EMD-to-reference; PTRM alone saturates (it rewards "slope" and "flat" fractions heavily) and was fit on 45°-view renders, not on DEMs of your scale.

### Gaps
- No single published paper gives "fractal dimension 2.2-2.4" with an estimator for DEMs at the 10-30 m scale; the values 2.5 (Turcotte, from beta ~ 2) vs 2.2-2.4 (box-counting/variogram estimators in geomorphology) differ by estimator, so the note recommends same-estimator comparisons instead of an absolute target.
- Argudo 2025's full metric list and correlation matrix were not extracted (paywalled HTML; README does not enumerate).
- PTRM geomorphon parameters (search radius, flatness threshold) used in the paper were not found in the extracted text.

## B3. Photo-side metrics: comparing renders of the generated terrain with copyright-free photos (LPIPS, DISTS, CLIP/DINO embeddings, FID/KID, skyline statistics, simple histograms) and CPU cost

### Takeaway
Photo comparison is distributional, not pairwise: render the generated map from many 45° or eye-level viewpoints with a fixed neutral shading, embed renders and reference photos with a small pretrained network (DINOv2-small or CLIP ViT-B/32 on CPU is ~tens of ms per image), and compute KID (unbiased, fine for a few hundred samples) or a simpler mean-embedding cosine distance; LPIPS/DISTS are pairwise metrics and only make sense against a matched reference render (e.g. a real-DEM render of the same class). Cheapest useful photo statistics need no network: skyline (silhouette) roughness spectrum and edge-orientation histograms.

### Cited Findings
- LPIPS: `pip install lpips`, backbones 'alex' (best forward scores) or 'vgg'; PyTorch, runs on CPU if you do not call `.cuda()`; lower = more similar. — [lpips on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/lpips/); [richzhang/PerceptualSimilarity](https://github.com/richzhang/PerceptualSimilarity/blob/master/lpips_loss.py)
- DISTS is available in the `piq` (PyTorch Image Quality) library. — [piq on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/piq/0.5.1/)
- FID assumes Gaussian embeddings and computes a Fréchet distance; KID uses squared MMD with a polynomial kernel, is unbiased and "more reliable for smaller sample sizes". — [Rethinking FID, CVPR 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Jayasumana_Rethinking_FID_Towards_a_Better_Evaluation_Metric_for_Image_Generation_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf); [REGEN (uses FID/KID)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.17061)
- DINOv2-small feature extraction: ~1000 images in ~12 s (~12 ms/image) in a FAISS similarity tutorial; DINOv2 "is best for image <-> image similarity"; normalise embeddings before cosine similarity. — [Image similarity with DINOv2 and FAISS](https://medium.com/aimonks/image-similarity-with-dinov2-and-faiss-741744bc5804); [CLIP vs DINOv2 in image similarity](https://medium.com/aimonks/clip-vs-dinov2-in-image-similarity-6fa5aa7ed8c6); [dinov2.cpp (CPU/ggml)](https://github.com/lavaman131/dinov2.cpp)
- PTRM rendered every DEM "by using the same settings to avoid bias", camera at "about 45° angle", and found perceived realism is driven by landform presence (valleys, ridges, hollows), which is exactly what a photo-embedding comparison will also pick up. — [PTRM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04610)
- Zhao et al. 2019 (multi-theme GAN terrain amplification) evaluated realism via "drainage simulations and a user study testing the perceived realism for different classes of terrain". — [ACM ToG 2019](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3355089.3356553)
- StyleDEM (Perche et al. 2023) was "compared to other standard methods and real digital elevation models in the training dataset" (metric details not retrieved). — [StyleDEM arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.09626)

### Inferences
- **[own suggestion]** Render protocol: fixed sun (e.g. 35° elevation, 135° azimuth), Lambert shading, your actual texture masks applied, sky colour constant, 8-16 views per map (4 azimuths x 2 elevations incl. one near-ground view that produces a skyline). Downscale to 224x224. Do the same for 20-50 reference DEM tiles of the target class (so you get "real-shape-rendered-with-your-shader" references, which removes the shader/photo domain gap) AND collect 200+ real photos of that landform class (Commons CC0/NPS).
- **[own suggestion]** Three-tier photo score: (i) no-network: skyline curve from the near-ground renders vs skylines auto-extracted from photos (sky segmentation by brightness/blue ratio), compare the PSD slope and the histogram of local skyline angles; edge-orientation histogram (Sobel, 18 bins) EMD; per-altitude-band luminance/hue histograms vs photos (requires rough altitude labels, so use only where masks are applied); (ii) CPU network: DINOv2-small or CLIP ViT-B/32 embeddings, KID between {renders} and {reference-DEM renders} (shape fidelity) and separately between {renders} and {photos} (look fidelity); (iii) pairwise LPIPS/DISTS only for A/B of two parameter settings rendered from identical viewpoints of the same seed, to measure how much a parameter changed perception. All of this runs on CPU in minutes for a few hundred images.
- **[own suggestion]** Because embedding distances to photos are dominated by texture/colour, use the DEM-side metrics (B2) as the primary optimiser objective and the photo-side KID as a sanity check / tie-breaker when tuning texture-mask thresholds.

### Gaps
- No published terrain paper was found that uses LPIPS/DISTS/CLIP/DINO/KID to score heightmap realism against photographs; the protocol above is my suggestion assembled from general image-generation evaluation practice.
- No measured CPU timings for LPIPS/DISTS specifically on terrain renders were found.

## B4. How existing work evaluated terrain realism

### Takeaway
Published evaluation is mostly (a) visual comparison and user studies (PTRM's two 70-participant 2AFC studies; Zhao 2019's class-wise realism study; Št'ava explicitly deferring "user studies"), (b) drainage/hydrology checks, and (c) in the survey literature, a push toward borrowing quantitative geomorphology descriptors (Argudo 2025). PTRM is the only published numeric "realism" function fit to human judgement, and it shows that noise and thermal-only terrains are rated well below real DEMs.

### Cited Findings
- PTRM: two large 2AFC studies; synthetic terrains "are perceived as lacking realism as compared to the real ones"; regression on geomorphons R^2 = 0.72; feature transfer with CycleGAN confirmed causality of landform presence. — [PTRM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.04610)
- Olsen 2004 evaluated with a slope-statistics "erosion score" and a unit-accessibility "playability" score, not with real-DEM comparison. — [Olsen 2004](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf)
- Št'ava 2008: "user studies are required to assess its actual usefulness." — [Št'ava 2008](https://cgg.mff.cuni.cz/~jaroslav/papers/2008-sca-erosim/2008-sca-erosiom-fin.pdf)
- Zhao et al. 2019: drainage simulations + user study of perceived realism per terrain class. — [ACM ToG](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3355089.3356553)
- Pelletier 1997 compared his model to real DEMs via power spectra and five river-network scaling relations across seven networks (Tokunaga statistics). — [Pelletier 1997](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/9705033)
- Argudo et al. 2025 provide the metric survey, implementations, timing and a proxy-correlation matrix. — [CGF 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cgf.70080)
- Galin et al. 2019 STAR: the organising survey (procedural / simulation / example-based). — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cgf.13657)
- Kwang & Parker 2017 show a modelling pitfall relevant to scoring: pure stream power with m/n = 0.5 is scale-invariant and ultra-sensitive to initial conditions, so realism of relief vs extent requires hillslope diffusion. — [ESurf 2017](https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/5/807/2017/)

### Inferences
- **[own suggestion]** For parameter tuning, treat PTRM + geomorphon-histogram EMD + Horton ratios + slope-histogram EMD as the objective; sweep erosion parameters (droplet count, capacity, erode/deposit speed, thermal T, stream-power K/U) on a handful of seeds, and keep settings that move the generated distributions toward the reference-DEM distributions of the chosen landform class. Then confirm with a small 2AFC of your own (even 5-10 people, 20 pairs) in the PTRM style.
