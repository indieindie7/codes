Scope: research and shipped-game practice that goes beyond shortest-path A*, so agents choose routes and positions tactically and can switch strategy on request (flank, fall back, push, stay hidden). ## 1. Tactical pathfinding: cost functions with exposure, cover, danger and ally distance

### Takeaway
The standard, shipped technique is plain A* (or Dijkstra) with extra per-link or per-node cost for being exposed to threats and for crossing friendly lines of fire. Killzone (PS2, 2004) did this on a waypoint graph using a 64 KB precomputed line-of-fire table for 4000 waypoints. It maps directly onto a UE2 PathNode graph. Advent's own engine already has per-node cost hooks (ExtraCost, TransientCost, FearCost, SpecialCost), so some tactical costs may reach the native A* without a script A*.

### Cited Findings
- Killzone's tactical pathfinding: "Shortest paths often aren't tactically sound". Listed failure cases are paths that cross friendly lines of fire, ignore cover from hostile fire, or leave the AI exposed unnecessarily. Fix: "In A*, add to the costs of traveling a link: traversing a friendly line-of-fire; being under fire from one or more threats." — [Beij & Straatman, "Killzone's AI: Dynamic Procedural Tactics", GDC Europe 2005 slides](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/gdce05_killzone_ai.pdf)
- Killzone world representation: (1) navigation info fine enough to represent cover locations ("waypoints, nav meshes, cells: any will do"), and (2) fast line-of-sight/line-of-fire checks. Because tactical decisions involve hundreds of LoF checks and raycasts are expensive, they use a small precomputed lookup table. It covers multiple stances and minor movement, is "pessimistic about cover", and uses a polar representation. **Killzone's LoF table for 4000 waypoints: 64 KB.** Compression: for every waypoint and every radial sector, store the largest distance from which an attacker in that sector can fire at the waypoint. The slides call this "Inaccurate, but consistent." — [Killzone GDC05 slides](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/gdce05_killzone_ai.pdf)
- Killzone results: tactical position picking, tactical pathfinding, suppression by infantry and mounted MGs, and indirect fire for grenades and tank shells, with up to 14 AI fighting simultaneously on PS2. Cons: "Harder to control in detail, harder to test." — [Killzone GDC05 slides](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/gdce05_killzone_ai.pdf). A forum summary of the full paper (Straatman, van der Sterren, Beij) adds that level designers initially had trouble controlling the AI, which might pick a "smarter" path than the one the design called for — [Quarter to Three forum summary](https://forum.quartertothree.com/t/the-ai-behind-killzone-explained/64142) (secondary source).
- Core idea of tactical A* (Brewer, citing van der Sterren 2002): raise the cost of nodes visible or exposed to the enemy so A* favours concealed nodes. Exhaustive visibility can be precomputed offline into lookup tables (Lidén 2002), but that is O(n²) memory. Approximations are a per-node radial distance field (Straatman 05), extended to 3D with depth-buffer cube maps in Killzone 2 (van der Leeuw 09), or rasterising cover into a grid with the enemy's view cone (Company of Heroes, Jurney 07). Brewer argues these approximations are more robust than exact raycasts, which can leak through tiny gaps or flip with tiny origin changes. — [Brewer, "Tactical Pathfinding on a NavMesh", Game AI Pro ch. 27 (free PDF)](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter27_Tactical_Pathfinding_on_a_NavMesh.pdf)
- Brewer's cost formula (Listing 27.1): `cost = exposedLength * agentCoverBias + (segmentLength - exposedLength)`, plus `segmentLength * polyFlags.isUnsafe * agentSafetyBias`. The exposed length comes from clipping the path segment against frustums built from each cover segment and the enemy position. Advice: keep biases at or above 1.0, so costs only grow and the distance heuristic stays admissible. Don't inflate costs too much either, or A* explores far more nodes than necessary. Designer flags such as "unsafe" (an open courtyard under balconies) and "provides concealment" also feed the cost. — [Brewer, Game AI Pro ch. 27](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter27_Tactical_Pathfinding_on_a_NavMesh.pdf)
- Van der Sterren, "Terrain Reasoning for 3D Action Games" (Game Programming Gems 2, 2001), works on the waypoint graph of a 3D action game. It precomputes waypoint-to-waypoint line-of-sight/line-of-fire and shortest-path travel-time tables, which "easily consume a few MB" and take "a few tens of seconds" to build for the example level (~480 waypoints). A full N×N matrix is O(N²). Store per waypoint only the waypoints it sees and those that see it, use a hierarchy plus a path cache, and limit queries to a 1–3 s time horizon. Measured costs: about 3,000 A* searches/s and 50,000 traces/s. At a 10% AI budget and 10 ticks/s, that is roughly 15 path searches and 250 traces per tick for all actors. Lookups are about 1000× faster than pathfinding and about 100× faster than traces. — [Game Developer reprint of van der Sterren's article](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/terrain-reasoning-for-3d-action-games)
- Van der Sterren's "Tactical Path-Finding with A*" is in Game Programming Gems 3 (2002), pp. 294–306. I found no free full text, only the citation. — [Game Engine Gems database](https://gameenginegems.com/gemsdb/article.php?id=227)
- Learned danger: the terrain-reasoning article records, per waypoint and per direction, time spent, damage dealt and damage taken (for players and AI). It folds "experience" into precomputed ratings between rounds, weights player activity above AI activity (so the AI does not reinforce its own mistakes), and flattens old values with log or sqrt. — [Game Developer reprint](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/terrain-reasoning-for-3d-action-games)
- **Advent Rising local source, native cost hooks.** `Engine/Classes/NavigationPoint.uc` declares `var int cost; var() int ExtraCost; var transient int TransientCost; var transient int FearCost; var transient bool bEndPoint; var transient bool bTransientEndPoint;` and `event int SpecialCost(Pawn Seeker, ReachSpec Path);`. `Controller.uc` declares native `FindPathTo(vector)`, `FindPathToward(actor, optional bool bWeightDetours)`, `FindPathToIntercept(Pawn, Actor, optional bool bWeightDetours)`, `FindPathTowardNearest(class<NavigationPoint>, optional bool bWeightDetours)`, plus `RouteCache[16]` and `RouteDist`. — local source `C:\Users\john\Documents\AdventRising_src\Engine\Classes\NavigationPoint.uc` lines 22–62, `Controller.uc` lines 96–167
- **Advent stock code already writes these costs.** In `WarfareGame/Classes/SquadAI.uc:3156`, `AddTransientCosts(Bot B, float f)` sets `TransientCost = 1000*f` on the nodes other squad members are moving to, which is a path-diversity mechanism. `Bot.uc:11179` sets `FearCost = 2*FearCost + 600` on the bot's MoveTarget when it dies. `GameInfo.Timer()` decays every node's `FearCost *= FearCostFallOff` (0.95). ExtraCost is used for doors (100), lifts (400), game objectives (2000) and claimed action points (2500), and `JumpSpot`/`JumpDest` override `SpecialCost`. — local source `SquadAI.uc`, `Bot.uc`, `Engine/Classes/GameInfo.uc` lines 72, 191–196, 1575

### Inferences
- In Advent, setting `TransientCost`/`FearCost`/`ExtraCost` from script before calling native `FindPathToward` is probably the cheapest way to get tactical paths: native speed, with no script A*. Epic's own code sets these fields right before path searches (squad-mate destinations, death spots), which strongly suggests the native A* adds them to the route weight. This is the UT2004 lineage and has **not** been verified for Advent's native code (see Gaps). The catch is that these costs are per node and shared by every pawn. TransientCost must be set and cleared around each agent's search, which works because UnrealScript is single-threaded.
- `SpecialCost(Pawn Seeker, ReachSpec Path)` is per seeker and per link. It is the ideal hook for strategy-dependent costs (stealthy vs aggressive) on a custom PathNode subclass, but it only applies to nodes whose class or flag makes the native code call it. Stock PathNodes may not.
- For exposure, the Killzone polar table maps naturally onto per-PathNode data computed once per level in script with `FastTrace` and stored in a config or cache object. With 8–16 sectors × a few bytes per node, 11k nodes come to ≈100–180 KB, which is trivial.

### Gaps
- No free full text of van der Sterren's GPG3 "Tactical Path-Finding with A*" was found, so its exact cost formula is unverified here. The Straatman/van der Sterren/Beij proceedings paper PDF (Chula mirror) had unextractable font encoding, so only the GDC slides were read.
- Whether Advent's native A* (Engine.dll) reads `TransientCost`/`FearCost`, and when it calls `SpecialCost` (stock UT-era code also had a `bSpecialCost` flag, see [UT NavigationPoint class dump](https://project-archives.etc.cmu.edu/2001/fall/coyote210/Docs/undox/Engine.NavigationPoint.html)), needs a Ghidra check of Engine.dll or an in-game test: set a huge TransientCost on a mid-route node and see whether the RouteCache changes.

## 2. Influence maps and tactical position evaluation

### Takeaway
Pick positions with weighted-sum scoring over candidate points: generate, filter, weight, take the best. This is the method in Killzone's position evaluation functions, Crytek's TPS query language and Unreal's EQS. It works on the existing PathNode set, plus designer-placed or generated cover markers. Influence maps (per faction proximity and threat, combined with add, multiply, invert and normalize) give the shared "where is it safe or contested" layer that both position scoring and path cost can read. On a waypoint graph, store the influence on the nodes rather than on a grid.

### Cited Findings
- Killzone position picking: "picking a good position is half the battle in fire & maneuver combat". Factors include line of fire, partial cover, nearby cover, proximity, preferred range, wall hugging and staying outside the danger zone. A position evaluation function is a weighted sum. Worked attack-position example on waypoints: proximity weight 20; LoF to the primary threat 40 with partial cover or 20 without; cover from secondary threats 20; inside preferred range 10. Sum the annotations and pick the maximum. The function is configured per action ("what kind of position?") and per personality ("what factors are important?"), and is dynamic in the current threats and squad constraints. — [Killzone GDC05 slides](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/gdce05_killzone_ai.pdf)
- Killzone suppression fire uses the same machinery: evaluate the hidden threat's best attack positions from the threat's point of view (LoF to the attacker 20/30, nearby cover 20), keep those scoring 40 that the attacker can fire into, and merge targets that overlap in yaw and pitch. — [Killzone GDC05 slides](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/gdce05_killzone_ai.pdf)
- Crytek's Tactical Position Selection (Matthew Jack): a query has **Generation** (hidespots from the target around the agent within a radius, a grid around the agent, circles), **Conditions** (filters), and **Weights**. Each weight's criterion is normalised to 0–1 (distances clamped to a known maximum useful range), multiplied by a signed user weight, and summed. A query is a list of "options": if option 1 returns nothing, option 2 is the fallback. Example: "CoverCompromised" means find hidespots from the target within 15 m and at least 5 m away that the agent can reach before the target, preferring hard cover and closer points. The fallback is a grid with `visible_from_target = -10`. Failed queries feed back into the behaviour tree; for example, "no suitable location to our left" aborts a flank. — [Jack, "Tactical Position Selection", Game AI Pro ch. 26 (free PDF)](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter26_Tactical_Position_Selection.pdf)
- Jack's performance rules: run cheap filters first, then weights (and sort), then expensive filters such as raycasts from the highest score down, and stop at the first point that passes. Bound each point's min/max possible score from the normalised weights so expensive weights can be skipped. Time-slice with a scheduler. Once a point is chosen, keep it unless a cheap re-validation query (critical conditions only) fails, which gives inertia against twitchy re-picking. — [Game AI Pro ch. 26](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter26_Tactical_Position_Selection.pdf)
- Jack's group techniques: a squad centre as the query origin with a maximum radius gives loose leapfrogging for free, because front members' queries fail until the rear catches up. Squad-level assignment of hidespots avoids contention, as in Crysis group behaviours. Companion criteria include `crossesLineOfFire_from_player = false` and staying in the player's camera view. Killzone 3 pre-generated hidespots automatically, while Brink generated all cover dynamically in concentric rings. — [Game AI Pro ch. 26](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter26_Tactical_Position_Selection.pdf)
- Unreal EQS (UE4/5) is the same pattern: Generators produce Items, Contexts give the frame of reference, and Tests filter or score. Filter tests run before scoring tests, pathfinding tests can invalidate unreachable items, and the highest-weighted item returns to the behaviour tree. — [Epic EQS overview](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/environment-query-system-overview-in-unreal-engine); [EQS tests node reference](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/eqs-node-reference-tests-in-unreal-engine)
- Dave Mark, "Modular Tactical Influence Maps" (shipped in prototypes of two large online RPGs): one **proximity** map (where agents can get to soon) and one **threat** map (what they can hit) per faction. Influence is stamped from precomputed normalised templates. Linear falloff `I = Max − Max·(d/D)` suits proximity; `I = Max − Max·(d/D)^4` suits threat, which stays flat and then drops. A ranged unit's threat can be a ring. Line-of-sight threats are simply not propagated to cells the agent cannot see. For movement-based threat behind walls, use path distance, precomputed by a Dijkstra flood from the agent. Maps update every ~1 s (recommended range 0.5–2 s). Working maps centred on the agent combine layers, e.g. `Add(EnemyLoc,1.0); Add(AllyLoc,-0.5); Multiply(InterestTemplate)` then `GetHighestPoint`, with normalize and invert helpers. Results achieved: tanks positioned between enemies and casters, threat level at the agent's position, safe withdraw spots, AoE targeting, spacing to avoid bunching, and avoiding "piling on". — [Mark, Game AI Pro 2 ch. 30 (free PDF)](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter30_Modular_Tactical_Influence_Maps.pdf)
- Killzone 2 multiplayer bots use an influence map on a **strategic graph** of areas (clusters of waypoints, auto-generated at export by greedily merging waypoints to minimise inter-area links). Positive or negative influence comes from bots, turrets and deaths, is smoothed over the graph, and is used for hiding, regrouping and defending. — [Champandard, Verweij, Straatman, "Killzone 2 Multiplayer Bots" slides](https://www.slideshare.net/guerrillagames/killzone-2-multiplayer-bots)
- Other free Game AI Pro chapters for further reading: Lewis, "Escaping the Grid: Infinite-Resolution Influence Mapping" (GAP2 ch. 29); Dill, "Spatial Reasoning for Strategic Decision Making" (GAP2 ch. 31); Couvidou, "Flooding the Influence Map for Chase in Dishonored 2" (Online 2021 ch. 6); Johnson, "Taming Spatial Queries" (Online 2021 ch. 5); Lewis, "Choosing Effective Utility-Based Considerations" (GAP3 ch. 13). — [gameaipro.com index](https://www.gameaipro.com/)
- Dave Mark's Infinite Axis Utility System is a data-driven utility architecture in which all behaviours are scored together. It was deployed in Guild Wars 2: Heart of Thorns (GDC 2015, "Building a Better Centaur"), and designers built NPC AI packages in as little as 7 minutes. I found no description of IAUS-specific position scoring in the sources read. — [gameai.com IAUS page](https://gameai.com/iaus.php) (via search snippet); [Wikipedia: Utility system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_system)

### Inferences
- On Advent's graph, a "node influence map" is the natural form: a float array indexed by PathNode, with threat stamped by flooding over the ReachSpec graph (Dijkstra up to a radius) or along precomputed per-node visibility lists. This avoids building a grid and directly gives A* an extra cost term: `cost += w_threat * threat[node]`.
- Advent already has designer markers that can act as the candidate-point database: `CoverPointPrimary/Secondary`, `AmbushPoint`, `AssaultPath`, `AlternatePath`, `AttackPathPoint` and `ActionPoint` classes exist in the exported source (`AdventRising_src/*/Classes`). A TPS/EQS-style scorer can generate candidates from these plus PathNodes within a radius.
- Strategy switching becomes query switching: each order (push, fall back, flank, hide) maps to a query (weights and filters) plus a path-cost profile, as in Jack's "context = behaviour × environment" library.

- No primary source found for Kevin Dill's or Dave Mark's position-specific utility curves beyond the influence-map chapter. Dill's GAP2 ch. 31 PDF is listed but was not read.
- Matthew Jack's chapter cites Crytek TPS documentation online and the CryEngine Free SDK. Current availability and licence were not checked.

## 3. Strategy-switchable paths, path diversity, flanking and pincers

### Takeaway
Switching strategy means switching the cost profile and the destination query, not the algorithm. Path diversity for squads works by penalising nodes or links that earlier squad members (or squads) already use. Killzone 2 did this per squad with "corridors", and Advent's own SquadAI does it with TransientCost. Flanking is a destination off the enemy's facing plus a path that heavily penalises the enemy's line of fire or frontal arc. A pincer is two flanks with opposite side constraints and mutually penalised corridors.

### Cited Findings
- Killzone 2 multiplayer bots: each squad runs its own strategic pathfinder (single-source, on the strategic area graph plus the influence map, caching distances and a spanning tree, refined incrementally with mixed Dijkstra / Bellman-Ford-Moore). **Squad corridors**: a squad's costs include the shortest paths of previously planned squads, so "squads actively pick paths to avoid other squads". Sub-objectives can carry multiple approach routes "for flanking". Individual bots then pathfind on waypoints inside the chosen areas. Individual AI is an HTN planner at 5 Hz, with "continue" branches to prevent twitchy re-planning. Domain size is 360 methods and 1,048 branches; load is about 500 plans/s for 14 bots plus turrets and drones. — [Killzone 2 Multiplayer Bots slides](https://www.slideshare.net/guerrillagames/killzone-2-multiplayer-bots); thesis basis: [Verweij 2007, "A Hierarchically-Layered Multiplayer Bot System for a First-Person Shooter"](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/VUA07_Verweij_Hierarchically-Layered-MP-Bot_System.pdf); follow-up: Straatman et al., "Hierarchical AI for Multiplayer Bots in Killzone 3", Game AI Pro (2013), pp. 377–390 (per search snippet; not read).
- Advent's stock `SquadAI.AddTransientCosts(Bot B, float f)` puts `TransientCost = 1000*f` on the move targets of other squad members currently in latent MoveToward. It is a simple version of the same diversity idea. — local source `C:\Users\john\Documents\AdventRising_src\WarfareGame\Classes\SquadAI.uc:3156`
- Practitioner recipe for flanking: run A* in "flank mode" with a high cost on all nodes in an arc in front of the target, which drives the route around and behind it. Vary costs by mode: stealth raises the cost of open-area nodes; combat lowers the cost of nodes next to cover; a sniper lowers the cost of nodes that see many nodes. — [GameDev.net forum thread "shooter game flanking algorithm"](https://humor.gamedev.net/forums/topic/673823-shooter-game-flanking-algorithm-or-way-of-implementation) (informal source)
- A Unity shooter-AI project raycasts from the player to every grid node and tags each as hidden, visible in front, or visible outside the FOV, then uses the tags for cost. — [Unity forum "AAAI – Advanced 3rd person shooter AI"](https://discussions.unity.com/t/aaai-advanced-3rd-person-shooter-ai/590573) (informal)
- GDC 2012 "Believable Tactics for Squad AI" covers flanking, suppression, leading, and centralised vs distributed squad synchronisation (only the abstract was seen). — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1016076/Believable-Tactics-for-Squad)
- Crytek TPS flanking uses a context query plus a failure signal: when a "flank left" query finds no valid cover to the left, flag it on the blackboard and abort or replan the squad action. — [Game AI Pro ch. 26](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter26_Tactical_Position_Selection.pdf)
- Mark's influence maps state that allied (positive) and enemy (negative) influence produce a front line (the zero crossing). "Agents could be made to move toward the battlefront, stay out of conflict, or try to flank the enemy." — [Game AI Pro 2 ch. 30](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter30_Modular_Tactical_Influence_Maps.pdf)

### Inferences
- Strategy profiles, given per-node `exposure[n]` (from threats via the visibility table), `threat[n]` (influence), `coverNear[n]` and `allyLoF[n]`, plus per-link length `L`, the cost of link (a→b) for each profile is:
  - **Push / assault:** `L * (1 + 0.5*exposure)`, short and accepting some exposure. Destination: the best attack position (Killzone weights).
  - **Stay hidden / stealth:** `L * (1 + 8*exposure + 2*lightLevel)`, plus a hard filter on nodes visible to any enemy's current position.
  - **Fall back:** `L * (1 + 4*exposure)` plus a penalty for decreasing distance to threats. Destination: maximise `distance_from_threat` and cover, staying within ally influence (Mark's "safe withdraw" query).
  - **Flank:** destination at a node whose bearing from the enemy is more than about 70° from the enemy's facing or the line to the squad. Path cost adds a large penalty for nodes in the enemy's front arc or LoF (forum recipe) and for nodes on the squad's main assault corridor.
  - **Pincer:** two flank queries constrained to opposite sides of the enemy→squad line, with the second planned after the first's corridor nodes get a TransientCost-style penalty (Killzone 2 corridors).
  
  These numbers are illustrative starting weights, not sourced values. Keep multipliers at or above 1 so the straight-line heuristic stays admissible (per Brewer).
- Diversity for N squad members: after each member's path is planned, add `k * L` to the links or nodes it uses (k ≈ 0.5–2) before planning the next. This is cheap, deterministic, and naturally yields fan-out or "flowing around" movement.

### Gaps
- No primary shipped-game source found for an explicit "pincer" algorithm. The sources only imply it via Killzone 2's multiple approach routes and corridors.
- The EVA London 2024 paper on squad flanking positions was found only as a repository listing ([Greenwich repository](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/47528/)), so its content is unknown.

## 4. Scale and smoothing: HPA*, JPS, flow fields, string pulling, steering/avoidance, formations

### Takeaway
At a few hundred to 11k nodes, plain A* over the PathNode graph in script is feasible if searches are time-sliced and rare. Hierarchy (area clusters as in Killzone 2) helps squad-level routing. JPS and flow fields are grid techniques and largely irrelevant to a sparse waypoint graph. Local steering (Reynolds-style separation plus simple avoidance, or RVO/ORCA ideas) matters more for how groups look than global path quality.

### Cited Findings
- HPA* (Botea, Müller, Schaeffer 2004) splits a grid into clusters with precomputed intra-cluster crossing costs, searches the abstract graph, then refines locally. It is up to 10× faster than optimised A*, with paths within 1% of optimal. — [Botea et al., "Near Optimal Hierarchical Path-Finding" (PDF)](https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/%7emmueller/ps/hpastar.pdf); results summarised in [Alberta papers DB](https://papersdb.cs.ualberta.ca/~papersdb/view_publication.php?pub_id=585)
- Killzone 2's equivalent on waypoints: auto-clustered "areas" form the strategic graph, and squads path on areas, then individuals on waypoints within them. — [Killzone 2 MP bots slides](https://www.slideshare.net/guerrillagames/killzone-2-multiplayer-bots)
- Jump Point Search (Harabor & Grastien, AAAI 2011) is optimal, adds no memory, and is an order of magnitude or more faster than A*, but only on **uniform-cost grids**. JPS+ (Rabin, GAP2 ch. 14) precomputes it for static uniform grids. — [AAAI 2011 paper](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/7994); [GAP2 ch. 14](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter14_JPS_Plus_An_Extreme_A_Star_Speed_Optimization_for_Static_Uniform_Cost_Grids.pdf)
- Flow fields (Supreme Commander 2): Emerson's "Crowd Pathfinding and Steering Using Flow Field Tiles" (Game AI Pro 1 ch. 23, free PDF). A 2012 community reverse-engineering post argues SupCom2's runtime is effectively a static per-tile vector field plus local steering rather than dynamic continuum crowds (community analysis, not official). — [GAP ch. 23](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter23_Crowd_Pathfinding_and_Steering_Using_Flow_Field_Tiles.pdf); [Wildfire Games forum analysis](https://wildfiregames.com/forum/topic/16018-supreme-commander-2-pathfinding/)
- The graph analogue of a flow field is a single-source Dijkstra tree. Killzone 2's per-squad pathfinder caches distances plus a spanning tree to the objective, so any bot can look up its next hop. — [Killzone 2 MP bots slides](https://www.slideshare.net/guerrillagames/killzone-2-multiplayer-bots)
- RVO2 / ORCA (UNC): Apache-2.0 since v2.0.2 (4 May 2016), with C++, C#, Java and 3D versions. It was licensed for Warhammer 40,000: Space Marine (Relic). — [RVO2 project page](https://gamma.cs.unc.edu/RVO2/); [downloads/licence](https://gamma.cs.unc.edu/RVO2/downloads/). An explainer is Sunshine-Hill, "RVO and ORCA: How They Really Work" (GAP3 ch. 19). Alternatives: Fray, "Context Steering" (GAP2 ch. 18); Guy & Karamouzas, "Guide to Anticipatory Collision Avoidance" (GAP2 ch. 19); Mars & Chanut, "Hierarchical Architecture for Group Navigation Behaviors" (GAP2 ch. 20, formations). — [gameaipro.com index](https://www.gameaipro.com/)
- Recast & Detour (navmesh plus Detour's path corridor and string pulling) is zlib-licensed; demo third-party files carry other licences. — [Debian copyright file](https://metadata.ftp-master.debian.org/changelogs/main/r/recastnavigation/stable_copyright); [recastnav.com](https://recastnav.com/)
- Path smoothing: Langerak, "Optimization for Smooth Paths" (GAP3 ch. 20). — [gameaipro.com index](https://www.gameaipro.com/)
- The density trade-off on waypoint graphs: lethal weapons, slow movement and no respawns call for denser graphs; tactical spot scoring assumes roughly uniform spacing; more waypoints near key spots reduce missed cover. — [van der Sterren, Terrain Reasoning](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/terrain-reasoning-for-3d-action-games)

### Inferences
- For Advent: a script A* over ≤11k nodes with a binary heap is likely fine if each search is budgeted (a node-expansion cap per tick, resumed next tick) and results are cached. Squad-level Dijkstra trees to an objective (one search serves all members) are cheaper than N separate A* runs.
- String pulling on a waypoint graph can be approximated by skipping ahead: if `actorReachable`/`pointReachable` (native, in Controller.uc) or `FastTrace` succeeds to node i+2, drop node i+1. This is the classic "line-of-sight smoothing" adapted to the native reachability tests.
- JPS and grid flow fields are not worth porting to a PathNode graph.

### Gaps
- No measurement of UnrealScript A* throughput on UE2 was found. It should be benchmarked in Advent itself (script is generally reported to be far slower than native, but no sourced number was found).

## 5. Recent research: learned heuristics, MARL navigation, LLM-directed agents

### Takeaway
Learned heuristics such as TransPath give about 4× fewer expansions on grids but need GPU inference and large training sets, which makes them academic for a UE2 script mod. MARL navigation is likewise academic. The practical part of the LLM work is the **architecture**: a slow high-level planner (human or LLM) issues symbolic orders (flank left, fall back to X, hold), and a fast deterministic layer (cost profiles plus position queries plus A*) executes them. Killzone 2 already shipped this layering without an LLM.

### Cited Findings
- TransPath (Kirilenko et al., AAAI 2023) uses a CNN-plus-attention network to predict a heuristic correction factor or path probability map for grid A* (Weighted A*, Focal Search). It reduces A* effort by up to 4× with costs less than 0.3% above optimal, trained on 640,000 instances. Grid-only and academic. — [arXiv 2212.11730](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.11730)
- StarEvolve / SC2Arena (arXiv 2508.10428, 2025): an LLM Planner produces high-level strategic commands, an Executor turns them into low-level actions, and Verifier modules check feasibility, all for StarCraft II. — [arXiv 2508.10428](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.10428v1)
- SwarmBrain (StarCraft II) uses an LLM for macro strategy, with a separate condition-response state-machine reflex layer because of LLM latency. — [Moonlight literature review of SwarmBrain](https://themoonlight.io/review/swarmbrain-embodied-agent-for-real-time-strategy-game-starcraft-ii-via-large-language-models) (secondary)
- L2M2 (Geng et al., 2025, via search snippet): an LLM produces subgoals for a MARL executor, reportedly needing less than 20% of the samples of MARL-only baselines. LEHCA (an LLM "commander" over a QMIX policy on SMAC) was seen only through a survey description and could not be dated or verified. — search results only, no primary source read
- Killzone 2's shipped hierarchy: strategy layer → squad orders (Defend, Advance) → individual HTN planner, with feedback upward. — [Killzone 2 MP bots slides](https://www.slideshare.net/guerrillagames/killzone-2-multiplayer-bots)

### Inferences
- For the "squad changes strategy on request" goal, the deterministic order vocabulary (push, hold, fall back, flank-left/right, pincer, stay hidden), each mapping to a cost profile plus a position query, is the deliverable. An LLM (or the player via commands) could sit on top later, issuing those tokens at seconds-scale latency. The research shows it must never be in the per-tick loop.
- Voyager-style (Minecraft) LLM agents were not researched here in depth. They are skill-library code generators and not relevant to per-tick movement.

### Gaps
- No primary-source reading of Voyager, LEHCA or L2M2. No published shipped game uses learned pathfinding heuristics, as far as the sources found show.

## 6. Costs, precomputation, and implementation on a UE2 waypoint graph (licences)

### Takeaway
Everything above needs only (a) per-node data precomputed once per level and cached, (b) a node-indexed influence array updated every 0.5–2 s, and (c) either native FindPathToward with per-node cost fields, or a time-sliced script A* with pluggable cost profiles. The memory and CPU budgets in the sources (64 KB for 4000 waypoints; 15 searches and 250 traces per tick at 10 Hz in a 2001 engine) show this fits a 2005 UE2 game.

### Cited Findings
- Precompute budgets: Killzone's polar LoF table is 64 KB for 4000 waypoints. — [Killzone GDC05](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/gdce05_killzone_ai.pdf). Full LoS/path tables "a few MB", built in "a few tens of seconds" for ~480 waypoints; store sparse visible-lists instead of N². — [van der Sterren](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/terrain-reasoning-for-3d-action-games)
- Influence update: stamp precomputed templates; refresh every ~1 s (0.5–2 s range); zero and restamp each update. — [Mark GAP2 ch. 30](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro2/GameAIPro2_Chapter30_Modular_Tactical_Influence_Maps.pdf)
- Query cost control: cheap filters → weights → expensive raycast filters from best down; time-slice with a scheduler; re-validate the chosen spot cheaply. — [Jack GAP ch. 26](http://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter26_Tactical_Position_Selection.pdf)
- Advent native API available to script: `FindPathTo/FindPathToward/FindPathToIntercept/FindPathTowardNearest` (with `bWeightDetours`), `RouteCache[16]`, `RouteDist`, `pointReachable`, `actorReachable`, `LineOfSightTo`, `FastTrace`; NavigationPoint has `PathList` (array of ReachSpec), `ExtraCost`, `TransientCost`, `FearCost`, `SpecialCost(Pawn, ReachSpec)`, `ProscribedPaths`/`ForcedPaths`, and `bBlocked`. — local source `Engine/Classes/Controller.uc`, `NavigationPoint.uc`, `Actor.uc:1434`
- Licences: RVO2 is Apache-2.0 ([UNC](https://gamma.cs.unc.edu/RVO2/)); Recast/Detour is zlib ([Debian copyright](https://metadata.ftp-master.debian.org/changelogs/main/r/recastnavigation/stable_copyright)). Game AI Pro chapters are free to read on gameaipro.com but are book text, not code licences ([gameaipro.com](https://www.gameaipro.com/)).

### Inferences
Recommended build order for Advent (cheapest first):
1. **Test the native hooks.** Set `TransientCost` on nodes exposed to the player (via a per-node visibility table) before `FindPathToward`, then clear it. If the native A* honours it, "stay hidden" and "fall back under cover" paths cost nearly nothing. Stock code already does this for squad diversity and death spots, which is evidence it works.
2. **Per-level precompute** (on first load, saved to an ini or cache object): per PathNode, a 16-sector Killzone-style max-fire-distance table built with `FastTrace` at standing and crouch heights; a cover flag (any CoverPoint within r, or the share of sectors blocked at short range); and an area cluster ID (greedy merge, Killzone 2 style).
3. **Node influence arrays**, updated about once per second: enemy threat (stamped through visibility sectors), ally proximity, and death/fear (already in FearCost, decaying 0.95 per GameInfo Timer).
4. **Position queries**, TPS/EQS style: candidates are Advent CoverPoint/AmbushPoint/ActionPoint plus PathNodes within a radius; Killzone-style weighted sums; ordered by cost; with fallback options.
5. **Script A* with cost profiles** only if the native hooks prove insufficient (for example, when per-agent or per-link costs are needed, such as flank-arc penalties that differ per squad member). Time-slice it and cap expansions per tick.
6. **Local steering:** separation and spacing from allies, using Mark's ally-proximity term, plus `actorReachable` skip-ahead smoothing.

### Gaps
- Advent-specific limits are unknown: UnrealScript iteration limits (runaway-loop detection) for large per-level precompute loops, the save/cache mechanism for per-level data, and whether `SpecialCost` is called for custom PathNode subclasses. All need in-engine testing or a Ghidra look at Engine.dll.
- No open-source UnrealScript tactical-A* implementation with a clear licence was found.
