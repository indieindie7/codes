# Feature-first and hydrology-first terrain generation (base shapes with designer control)

Scope: methods that build the large forms of a heightmap from rivers / ridges / uplift rather than from noise + erosion, with the designer saying "river from here to there, ridge along this line, plateau here". Target use: 128–256 cell maps (1–8 km) in Python/NumPy, feeding an existing erosion + geomorphon-score pipeline. Primary sources were read in full (PDF text extracted) for Génevaux 2013, Hnaidi 2010, Cordonnier 2016, Génevaux 2015 and Schott 2023; the rest comes from abstracts, repos and blog posts. Anything marked **[inference]** is mine, not the authors'.

---

## KQ1. Génevaux 2013 "Terrain Generation Using Procedural Models Based on Hydrology" and its follow-ups

### Takeaway
Génevaux 2013 grows a river *graph* inward from mouths on the coast with a tiny 3-rule grammar gated by Horton–Strahler order, assigns rising elevations from a user "river slope map", builds ridges from the Voronoi diagram of river nodes using a "terrain slope map", then fills the space between with blended disc primitives and carves river profiles. Everything in it is implementable in NumPy/SciPy in a few hundred lines; the follow-ups (2015 primitives, 2022 gradient-domain, 2023 uplift authoring) are about richer primitives and better realism, not about the network growth.

### Cited Findings

**Inputs and pipeline**
- User gives: the terrain contour Γ (2-D polyline), river mouths, optionally some partial river sketches, and control parameters; the system grows the drainage network as a geometric graph, classifies rivers (Rosgen types), and generates the terrain as a construction tree of primitives combined by blend/carve operators — [Génevaux 2013 PDF](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf) (Sec. 3).
- Two user-defined (or procedurally generated) maps control the output: the **river slope map** "drives the dendritic shape of the river and directs the Rosgen type of each river"; the **terrain slope map** "controls the location of mountains and flatlands. The crest and ridge elevations are obtained by combining the river elevation and the terrain slope information." — [Génevaux 2013](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf) (Sec. 3).

**River network growth (Sec. 4)**
- Each node N_i = (position p_i, priority index s_i = Horton–Strahler number, Rosgen type ρ_i, flow φ_i). **Every edge has a constant user-defined length e.** — [Génevaux 2013](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf) (Sec. 4, Notations).
- Initial candidates: at river mouths on the contour, or on extremities and regularly jittered samples along user-sketched river parts; each gets a priority index. Automatic mouth placement heuristics: mouths of two large rivers are far apart, and large mouths/deltas sit in concave parts of the contour (citing Dunne & Leopold 1978) — [Génevaux 2013](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf) (Sec. 4.1).
- Loop: (1) node selection from candidate set X, (2) node expansion with geometric compatibility tests, (3) X ← (X \ {N_X}) ∪ {new nodes}; incompatible nodes are dropped — (Sec. 4.2).
- **Node selection**: find lowest candidate elevation z; take admissible subset with elevation in [z, z+ζ]; among those pick the highest priority (Horton–Strahler) index, ties broken by lowest elevation. ζ ∈ [0, ∞): "When ζ = 0, we obtain networks of similar sizes, whereas one large drainage network and several small ones are generated with ζ = 20." Small ζ → lowland networks first, more ramifications; large ζ → fewer, larger drainage networks — (Sec. 4.2.1, Fig. 4–5).
- **Expansion grammar (Table 1)**: nonterminals α (candidate) and β (instantiated), terminal τ (frozen).
  - Rule 1 (filling): α(1) → τ(1) β^p(1), p ∈ [1; 5] — when every remaining candidate has order 1 the graph keeps filling the domain.
  - Rule 2.1 (continuation, prob. P_c): α(n) → τ(n) β(n).
  - Rule 2.2 (symmetric Horton–Strahler junction, prob. P_s): α(n) → τ(n) β(n−1) β(n−1).
  - Rule 2.3 (asymmetric junction, prob. P_a): α(n) → τ(n) β(n) β(m), m < n.
  - Rule 3.1/3.2: β(n) → α(n) if geometrically valid, else ε (rejected). P_c + P_a + P_s = 1, user-defined — (Table 1, Sec. 4.2.2).
- Horton–Strahler number: leaf = 1; a node inherits the max of its children, +1 when two or more children share that max. If a high-priority node can't be expanded for geometric reasons, "all Horton-Strahler numbers are adjusted accordingly." The authors note the HS rules "could be replaced without changing the framework." — (Sec. 4.2.2).
- **Geometric validity** of a new point p with edge E to parent: p ∈ Ω and distance to contour d(p, Γ) > η with **η = ¾ e = 1500 m** in their implementation (so e = 2000 m); distance from E to all existing edges > σ·f(s) with **σ = ¾ e = 1500 m**, f(s) increasing with HS number "to maintain large rivers apart"; elevation must be higher than the parent and satisfy a Lipschitz bound |p_z − p_zi| < κ(p)·d(p, p_i) where κ is the upper bound of the slope map — "This condition prevents the creation of huge cliffs." — (Sec. 4.2.2, Fig. 7).
- **Elevation of new nodes**: computed from the local river-slope-magnitude value (scalar) of the river slope map, which "defines only the height variation and provides no information on the direction of the expansion" — (Sec. 4.2.2, "River Slope map").
- **Effect of rule probabilities (Fig. 8)**: (P_c=0.2, P_s=0.7, P_a=0.1) gives highly curved watersheds, few main streams, >75 % of streams with HS = 1; (P_c=0.2, P_s=0.1, P_a=0.7) gives watersheds of comparable size structured around longer main rivers. HS-order frequency histograms are given: roughly 82/10/4/4 % vs 59/20/10/5 % for orders 1–4 (digits read from Fig. 8 axis labels in the PDF text; treat as approximate) — [Génevaux 2013](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf).

**Watersheds, crests, flow (Sec. 5)**
- Domain decomposed into **Voronoi cells of the river node positions**; cell edges not crossed by the river graph are ridge lines; watershed of an outlet = set of upstream connected cells — (Sec. 5.1).
- Flow from area via empirical law **φ = 0.42 · A^0.69** (A in m², φ in m³/s; Dunne & Leopold 1978), A approximated by summing connected cell areas — (Sec. 5.1).
- **Crest elevation**: each Voronoi vertex q is equidistant (d) from three river nodes a, b, c; **q_z = max(a_z, b_z, c_z) + λ(q)·d, λ ∈ [0; 0.25]**, where λ is the terrain slope map ("describes which parts of the terrain will become plains, plateaus, valleys, or mountains"), optionally weighted by distance to coast and node elevation — (Sec. 5.1, Fig. 11).
- Rosgen classification (A+, A, B, C, D, DA, E, F, G) assigned per node from river slope and geodesic distance to coast; near-coast nodes become braided (D/DA); mouths with flow above a threshold become deltas; junction angles: very different flows → nearly perpendicular, similar flows → small angle; river paths refined by subdivision below a user-defined length with randomly perturbed positions/tangents per type — (Sec. 5.2, 6.1).

**Terrain primitives and construction tree (Sec. 6.2, 7)**
- Primitive centres from Poisson-disc sampling, **50 samples per Voronoi cell**, radii grown until the discs cover Ω; primitive elevation = distance-weighted combination of its projection onto the rivers (a) and onto the ridge lines (b); noise amplitude/frequency per primitive depends on distance to river d_a and on the river-to-crest height difference ("more roughness for the mountains than for the valleys"); noise "can produce small local minima, but they remain negligible" — (Sec. 6.2).
- Weight function w(p) = (1 − d²/r²)² for d < r (compact support); terrain primitive h(p) = c_z + n(p) (Perlin noise); river primitive h(p) = u_z(p) + δ(d(p)) with a 1-D piecewise profile δ per river type (bedrock/water/sand layers); blend B(A,B): h = (w_A h_A + w_B h_B)/(w_A + w_B), w = (w_A + w_B)/2; replace/carve C(A,B): h = (1 − w_B) h_A + w_B h_B; whole terrain = C(B({T_i}), B({R_i})) — (Sec. 7).
- Timings (i7 3 GHz): graph 0.1–5.3 s, cells 0.1–1.1 s, tree 1.8–4.9 s, 1024² evaluation 0.7–1.2 s for terrains of 970–3386 km² with 400–2100 km of river; ~1.5 kB per km² — (Table 2–3).
- **Stated limitations**: only terrains that have been subject to hydraulic erosion; network only subdivides upstream (no deltas/oxbows); greedy construction "can lead to meandering crests and outcroppings of unnatural-looking hills"; transitions between plains and mountains can be abrupt; "If the slope maps have strong variations flat rivers can cut through high mountains"; many parameters allow unnatural terrains — (Sec. 8, Limitations).

**Follow-ups**
- Génevaux et al. 2015 "Terrain Modeling from Feature Primitives" (CGF 34(6)): compact construction tree with skeletal primitives (disc, curve, contour) and image primitives; weight α(p) = g(d(p)) with Wyvill falloff g(x) = (1 − (x/r)²)³; operators blend (f = (α_A f_A + α_B f_B)/(α_A+α_B), α = α_A+α_B), replace (f = (1−α_B) f_A + α_B f_B, α = α_A), add (f = f_A + α_B f_B), warp (f ∘ ω⁻¹); rivers = curve skeleton + cross-section profiles; mountains = disc with ridged multifractal noise; Lipschitz property for sphere tracing — [Génevaux 2015 PDF](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2015-terrain-from-primitives.pdf).
- Guérin et al. 2016 "Sparse representation of terrains for procedural modeling" (CGF) is used by Cordonnier 2016 and Schott 2023 as an *amplification* step (×8 in Schott Fig. 1) to add detail to a coarse heightfield — [Schott 2023 HAL](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1) (Sec. 7.5); [Cordonnier 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Cordonnier-Computer_Graphics_Forum-2016-Large_Scale_Terrain_Generation_from_Tectonic_Uplift_and_Fluvial_.pdf) (Sec. 3.2).
- Guérin et al. 2022 "Gradient Terrain Authoring" (CGF 41(2), EG 2022): models terrain in the gradient domain ("slope-based representation is height independent"), enabling seamless blending of procedural/simulated/real patches, cut-and-paste with warping, brushes, and "point and curve constraints on both elevation and gradient" — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cgf.14460?af=R). Code: C++/OpenGL/GLFW, Poisson reconstruction via compute shader from three PGM maps (alpha = Dirichlet vs Poisson mask, altitude constraints, Laplacian map), outputs results/result.pgm — [eric-guerin/gradient-terrains](https://github.com/eric-guerin/gradient-terrains). Schott 2023 says of it: "great control to the user but they do not create hydrologically-correct terrains" — [Schott 2023](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1) (Sec. 7.4).
- Schott et al. 2023 "Large-scale Terrain Authoring through Interactive Erosion Simulation" (TOG 42(5)) — see KQ2; it is the uplift-domain successor to both lines.

### Inferences
- **[inference]** On a 128–256 grid at 1–8 km, Génevaux's e = 2 km edge length is far too coarse; e should be ~3–6 cells (e.g. 100–200 m at 30 m/cell), with η = σ = ¾ e, and the Lipschitz κ taken from your terrain-slope map. The algorithm is resolution-free, so this scaling is legitimate.
- **[inference]** The two slope maps *are* the design interface: paint "river slope" low where you want broad flat valleys, high where you want gorges; paint "terrain slope" (λ) high where you want mountains and ~0 for plateaus/plains. Sketched partial rivers plus mouths give the "river from here to there" control directly (they are seeded as high-priority candidates).
- **[inference]** A NumPy version can skip the construction tree entirely: rasterise the river graph elevations and Voronoi-vertex crest elevations as sparse constraints and fill the rest with a Hnaidi-style Laplace/guided diffusion (KQ3), then hand off to your existing erosion. That removes the authors' "meandering crests / unnatural hills" issue from greedy disc placement.

### Gaps
- Exact default values of P_c/P_s/P_a, ζ, f(s), the river-slope map magnitudes and Rosgen thresholds are not in the paper beyond the two Fig. 8 examples ("Default parameters were used" for Fig. 19, values unstated).
- No open-source code from the authors; the only public implementation is a third-party Python repo (KQ5).
- Guérin 2016 "Sparse representation" could not be fetched; only its role as an amplifier is documented here.

## KQ2. Uplift-first methods: Cordonnier 2016, Cordonnier 2018, Schott 2023, Cortial 2019

### Takeaway
Cordonnier 2016 turns the stream power law dh/dt = u − k A^m s^n into a generator: paint an uplift map u, start flat, iterate flow routing + implicit stream-power solve until steady state; ridges appear where uplift is high and dendritic valleys self-organise. Schott 2023 makes this an authoring tool: a vector "Uplift Tree" of primitives (ridge segments, valley-carving segments, asymmetric star primitives), uplift-domain warping/blending/copy-paste, PID-controlled dynamic uplift to hit prescribed peak/ridge elevations, inverse modelling from DEM ridge/river trees, and a GPU explicit solver with an iterative drainage-area approximation. Both guarantee pit-free, outlet-draining flow, which neither Génevaux nor Hnaidi do.

### Cited Findings

**Cordonnier et al. 2016 (CGF 35(2), EG 2016)**
- Equation: **dh/dt = u(p) − k A(p)^m s(p)^n**, with n = 1, m = 0.5 ("As in most geomorphological studies"); k depends on lithology/climate; u is the painted uplift — [Cordonnier 2016 PDF](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Cordonnier-Computer_Graphics_Forum-2016-Large_Scale_Terrain_Generation_from_Tectonic_Uplift_and_Fluvial_.pdf) (Sec. 3.1, Eq. 1).
- Input: uplift map U as a "piece-wise uniform distribution of values over the domain Ω ... given by the user as gray-scales images"; the graph is a Poisson-disc sampling + constrained Delaunay triangulation (random graph "generates more plausible results" than a regular grid because edges give more stream directions); initial h = 0; border nodes fixed at sea level as outlets; node area a_k from Voronoi cell — (Sec. 3.2, 4.1).
- Loop per step: (1) each node's receiver = lowest neighbour → stream trees; (2) lakes (internal roots) connected via lowest **passes** in a lake super-graph, processed in increasing pass height from the border inward, O(N + M log M) for M lakes; (3) drainage area A_k = a_k + Σ children, computed leaves-to-root; (4) implicit stream-power update root-to-leaves: **h_i(t+δt) = [h_i(t) + δt (u_i + k A_i^m h_j(t+δt)/‖p_i − p_j‖)] / [1 + k A_i^m δt/‖p_i − p_j‖]** (n = 1), O(N) — (Sec. 4.2–5, Eq. 2). This is Braun & Willett 2013's algorithm with a faster lake step.
- Thermal correction: if the update yields slopes > 30°, the elevation change is reduced to keep the slope in range; modulating the max slope with 3-D Perlin noise between 6° and 54° randomises valley patterns; height-dependent max slope gives cliffs on crests — (Sec. 5, 6.4, Fig. 17–18).
- Parameters used: 50 × 50 km domain, max uplift U = 5.0·10⁻⁴ m/yr, k = 5.61·10⁻⁷ yr⁻¹ (for ~2000 m peaks), δt = 2.5·10⁵ yr; empirical rule **h_max [km] ≈ 2.244 · u/k**; only u/k matters (both multiply δt); mountains "fully shaped after 50 iterations", converge in 100–300 — (Sec. 6, 6.4, Fig. 12).
- Steady-state profile shapes from m/n: h ∼ d^(1−2m/n) − (d−x)^(1−2m/n) for m/n < ½, log form for m/n = ½, etc.; "We use the result of these equations to choose the proper ratio m/n to shape the desired river profile." — (Sec. 6.4).
- Control insight: "the main impact is in the gradient of the uplift values. The actual shape of the uplift does not have a strong influence, except on the boundaries. ... a slowly decreasing gradient leads to a very straight erosion in the gradient direction, whereas a set of steps of constant values gives more random valleys ... lower values of uplift lead to smaller valleys" — (Sec. 6.4, Fig. 13).
- Performance (unoptimised C++, i7 3 GHz): 10 k nodes 0.031 s/step, 6.4 s to converge; 40 k 0.177 s/step, 35 s; 90 k 0.401 s, 78 s; 160 k 1.273 s, 252 s (~200 steps) — (Table 1).
- Can run without uplift on an existing (e.g. fractal) terrain to add hydrological realism, but "it is necessary to use small time steps and to stop the simulation after only a few iterations" because it converges to flat — (Sec. 6.4, Fig. 14).
- Validation: only a side-by-side with the San Gabriel Mountains (USGS) and one geologist co-author's visual check; "statements about the visual plausibility should be supported by some evaluation"; no sediment deposition — (Sec. 6.1, 7).

**Cordonnier et al. 2018 "Sculpting Mountains" (IEEE TVCG 24(5))**
- User controls shape and motion of tectonic plates; a geologically inspired crust model gives volume-preserving folding; it "generates a volumetric uplift map representing the growth rate of subsurface layers, and erosion and uplift movement are jointly simulated"; stratigraphy rendered on cliffs — [Purdue publication page](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/publications/cordonnier2018sculpting/). Schott 2023 reports it achieved interactive rates only on 100 × 100 grids and that "controlling the generation of the small-scale features remains difficult" — [Schott 2023](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1) (Sec. 2, 7.2).

**Schott et al. 2023 "Large-scale Terrain Authoring through Interactive Erosion Simulation" (TOG 42(5))**
- Equation with hillslope term: **∂h/∂t = u − s^n a^m + Δh**, explicit Euler on a regular grid: h_i(k+1) = h_i(k) + Δt (u_i − s_i^n a_i^m + Δh_i); uplift split **u = u₀ (static, from the Uplift Tree) + ũ (dynamic, from elevation constraints)** — [Schott 2023 HAL PDF](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1) (Sec. 3–4, Eq. 1–2).
- Flow routing: multiple-flow-direction with L_p weights w_ij = s_ji^p / Σ_k s_jk^p over 8 neighbours; p = ∞ is steepest descent, p = 1 diffusive; **p = 4 used** (Holmgren 1994: 4 ≤ p ≤ 6 most accurate) — (Sec. 4.1, Eq. 4).
- Drainage area approximated iteratively in parallel: ã_i(k+1) = 1 + Σ_{j above i} w_ji(k) ã_j(k); converges to the exact value in n to 4n iterations (avg 1.5n) for an n×n grid; on 256² the reference is reached at 130 iterations; the approximation runs *inside* the erosion loop — (Sec. 4.2, Eq. 5, Fig. 6).
- **Uplift Tree primitives**: u_P(p) = u_S (1 − d²/r²)³ for d ≤ r (Eq. 6); asymmetric "star" primitives use a pseudo-distance d̃ = ‖q − p‖/‖q − m‖ with a boundary curve Γ around skeleton S ("regions with a high gradient always create a high gradient in the elevation, i.e. a steep slope flank"); segment mountain-range primitive with varying radius r(x) and crest value u_S(x); **valley primitive** = segment with a profile low in the valley and high outside, width **w ∝ a^0.4** from drainage area — (Sec. 5.1, 6.2, Fig. 11–12, 16).
- Operators: warp u_ω(A) = u_A ∘ ω⁻¹ (faults/folds), blend u = (1−α) u_A + α u_B, max for assembling ridge primitives; discontinuities are fine in uplift ("approximation of faults") whereas blending/warping elevation "yields unrealistic artificial-looking terrains with inconsistent drainage network featuring numerous pits" — (Sec. 5.2, 7.1, Fig. 24–25).
- **Ridge networks**: divide tree (peaks + saddles, Argudo 2019) segments → mountain-range primitives, radii/values ∝ divide-tree elevations, combined with max; **river networks**: river tree edges → valley primitives ("By limiting the amount of tectonic uplift within river and valley areas, we force the flow routing to those specific zones"); inconsistent user networks still yield a coherent (if partly disobeyed) network — (Sec. 6.1–6.2).
- Procedural ranges: user tensor/vector field → Poisson-disc discs → anisotropic off-lattice Eden growth (edge accepted if |(p_k − p_i)·v(m_ik)|/‖·‖ ≤ cos α) → clusters → segment primitives (Zagros-like parallel ranges) — (Sec. 6.3).
- **Elevation constraints** via PID on ũ: ũ(k+1) = K_p e(k) + K_i Σ e(j) + K_d (e(k) − e(k−1)), e = h₀ − h; **K_p = 0.01, K_d = 0.01, K_i = 0.001, update every 10 iterations**; point constraint = compactly supported smooth step of radius r at q; curves sampled into points; controllers frozen into u₀ at steady state. Limitation: neighbouring constraints with incompatible elevations and overlapping supports "may lead to instabilities" — (Sec. 6.4, 7.5, Eq. 10).
- Performance (GTX 1080 Ti, GLSL compute): 256² 5 k steps, 0.1 ms/step, 0.5 s total; 512² 5 k, 0.3 ms, 1.3 s; 1024² 11 k, 0.8 ms, 9.1 s; 2048² 39 k, 3.3 ms, 128 s; 4096² 81 k, 12.5 ms, 1036 s; adaptive Δt: ×1.01 if pit count decreasing, ×0.75 otherwise; "shape of the final terrain is observable after only a few hundred iterations" — (Table 1, Sec. 7.2, Fig. 26–28).
- Cell sizes 80–600 m; all figures 1024² (one 2000×1200); terrains need amplification afterwards; no lakes (flow forced to border); no sediment deposition so valley floors lack variety — (Table 2, Sec. 7.5).
- Validation by **Hack's law** l = c a^n: fitted c = 1.00–1.38, n = 0.542–0.584 vs literature ranges c ∈ [1, 6], n ∈ [0.45, 0.7] — (Sec. 7.3, Table 2, Fig. 30).
- Comparison: Génevaux 2013's "procedurally generated mountain ranges may not be hydrologically correct, and may not exhibit characteristic dendritic shapes"; example-based (Zhou 2007, Gain 2015) and gradient methods are not hydrologically consistent and breaching "still produces artificial canyons" — (Sec. 7.4).
- Code: [H-Schott/StreamPowerErosion](https://github.com/H-Schott/StreamPowerErosion), C++/CMake, MIT, Windows/Linux, Ctrl+click paints uplift — [repo](https://github.com/H-Schott/StreamPowerErosion); [project page](https://aparis69.github.io/public_html/projects/schott2023_Uplift.html).

**Cortial et al. 2019 "Procedural Tectonic Planets" (CGF 38(2))**
- Procedural (not physical) plate model: users control plate motion; subduction/collision deform continental and oceanic crust to produce continents, oceanic ridges, large mountain ranges, island arcs; results amplified with procedural or real elevation data; events like rifting can be triggered — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/cgf.13614); [PDF (LIRIS)](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2019-planets.pdf).

### Inferences
- **[inference]** For a 128–256 grid the Cordonnier/Schott loop is the single most useful "base shape" tool: a NumPy implementation (steepest-descent receivers via argmin over 8 neighbours, topological order by sorting on elevation, A accumulated in that order, implicit update root-to-leaves or the Schott explicit form) is ~100–200 lines; at 256² a few hundred to a few thousand steps run in seconds with vectorised NumPy, so GPU is unnecessary at your scale. Cordonnier's lake-pass connection is the hard part; at 256² you can instead use priority-flood sink filling each step (Barnes 2014) or simply let Schott-style pits erode away.
- **[inference]** Design mapping: "ridge along that line" = segment uplift primitive (Schott Eq. 6); "river from here to there" = valley primitive (low uplift corridor, width ∝ a^0.4); "plateau" = uniform uplift block (Cordonnier: step-wise constant uplift gives random valleys, smooth gradient gives straight parallel valleys); "mountain this tall" = PID point constraint. Cordonnier's rule h_max ≈ 2.244 u/k (km) gives a starting point for k.
- **[inference]** Because the method only reaches 80 m cells in Schott's work and your cells are ~8–60 m, treat the uplift result as the base shape only and keep your existing droplet/thermal erosion as the detail stage.

### Gaps
- Cordonnier 2018 and Cortial 2019 full texts not read; only abstracts.
- No published Horton-ratio or geomorphon comparison for any uplift method; only Hack's law (Schott) and one visual DEM comparison (Cordonnier).
- Schott's lake-free assumption means inland basins/lakes need a separate mechanism (Cordonnier's pass graph or post-hoc filling).

## KQ3. Ridge/valley skeleton methods: Hnaidi 2010, Zhou 2007, Gain 2009, Tasse 2014

### Takeaway
Hnaidi 2010 is the cleanest "feature curves → heightmap" method: rasterise elevation/slope/noise constraints along Bézier curves, Laplace-diffuse the gradient and noise fields, then run a guided diffusion of elevation that honours both the gradient and strict elevation constraints, and add a multifractal noise whose amplitude/roughness are themselves diffused fields. It gives exact control but no hydrological guarantee. Zhou/Gain/Tasse are example-based or deformation-based sketch methods: more realism per curve, far more code and data.

### Cited Findings

**Hnaidi et al. 2010 (CGF 29(7), Pacific Graphics)**
- Pipeline: (1) rasterise elevation, gradient, noise-amplitude and roughness constraint maps; (2) Laplace-diffuse noise, gradient and elevation constraints; (3) guided diffusion of elevation controlled by the diffused gradient; (4) detail map from the diffused noise parameters; final = smooth map + detail map — [Hnaidi 2010 PDF](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2010-terrain.pdf) (Sec. 3).
- Feature curve = piecewise cubic Bézier in the plane with constraint points P_i = ((h_i, r_i), (a_i, b_i, θ_i, φ_i), (A_i, R_i), u_i): elevation h with plateau radius r; slope angles θ, φ with extents a, b on each side; noise amplitude A and roughness R; linear interpolation along the curve except elevation (cubic, "to avoid unnatural piecewise linear ridge lines") — (Sec. 3.1).
- Recipes: **ridge** = elevation + slope angles each side, small r; **multiple ridges** share a peak with same h; **riverbed** = small elevation range, r = riverbed width, slightly upward slopes with small radius; **cliff** = opposite slope angles each side; **hill** = horizontal slope constraints (null gradient ⇒ neighbourhood at same elevation); **crack** = two rising slopes, no strict elevation — (Sec. 3.2).
- Rasterisation: quadrangles along the curve normal of lengths r, a, b; strict constraints painted uniform; slope-angle quads fade to 0 at the far edge "to avoid gradient discontinuities"; gradient stored as curve normal n and gradient norm; intersecting gradient regions are left empty and filled by Laplace diffusion — (Sec. 4.1, Fig. 9).
- Equations: Laplace ΔF = 0 where no info; gradient ∇F = G (not Poisson: "Poisson equation simplifies to a Laplace equation when we set a null gradient constraint and thus forbids the creation of horizontal angle constraints"); identification F = F̄ on strict constraints; forward-Euler discretisation; over-constrained points solved by a weighted Jacobi relaxation: **F^{k+1} = α F_L + β F_G + (1−α−β) F_I**, F_L = 4-neighbour average, F_G = F_N + G with F_N = n_x² F(i − sign n_x, j) + n_y² F(i, j − sign n_y); **α = β = ½ in gradient areas, α = β = 0 on strict elevation, α = 1, β = 0 elsewhere** — (Sec. 4.2, 5.2).
- Multigrid (Orzan 2008 diffusion-curves style): l levels, 5(l − i) Jacobi iterations at level i; first diffuse gradient norm and n, then elevation; A and R diffused together — (Sec. 5.2).
- Noise: N(x,y) = A(x,y) Σ_{k=0..n} r^{−k(1−R(x,y))} S(r^k x, r^k y), 4 octaves (n = 3), lacunarity r = 2; R damps high frequencies rather than changing feature size (which "breaks the continuity and gives unnatural ring effects") — (Sec. 4.3).
- Cost: 10 n×n float grids (40 MB at 1024²); GPU (8800 GTS) times 0.17–0.22 s at 512², 0.25–0.34 s at 1024², 0.61–0.81 s at 2048²; scenes used 26–74 curves (1.3–4.5 kB); first canyon sketch < 3 min, 45 min of tuning mostly on noise parameters — (Table 1–2, Sec. 6).
- Génevaux 2013's critique: "it does not address the geomorphological properties of a river-network creation" — [Génevaux 2013](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf) (Sec. 2).

**Zhou, Sun, Turk, Rehg 2007 "Terrain Synthesis from Digital Elevation Models" (IEEE TVCG 13(4))**
- Example-based: patches of a sample DEM are placed to match a user-sketched feature map emphasising "large-scale curvilinear features (ridges and valleys) because such features are the dominant visual elements"; patches matched to sketched features, seams by graph cuts and Poisson editing — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17495341/); project site [terrainsynthesis.org](http://terrainsynthesis.org/) (SSL error at fetch time). Génevaux: "as with every template-based algorithm, it fails to generate results that are not exemplified by the input" — [Génevaux 2013](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf).

**Gain, Marais, Strasser 2009 "Terrain Sketching" (I3D)**
- Users draw silhouette, spine and bounding curves of extruding (hills, mountains) and embedding (river courses, canyons) landforms; terrain generated by multiresolution surface deformation; "wavelet noise characteristics of silhouette strokes are propagated to the surrounding terrain" — [UCT archive](https://pubs.cs.uct.ac.za/id/eprint/516/). Hnaidi: in Gain 2009 "terrain characteristics are incrementally edited but not stored" (no vector model) — [Hnaidi 2010](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2010-terrain.pdf) (Sec. 6).

**Tasse et al. 2012/2014**
- Tasse, Gain, Marais 2012 "Enhanced Texture-Based Terrain Synthesis on Graphics Hardware" (CGF): curvilinear features from a 2-D sketched feature map constrain patch-based synthesis of real landscapes, with a patch-merging method reducing boundary artefacts — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2012.03076.x). Tasse et al. 2014 "First person sketch-based terrain editing" (GI 2014) deforms terrain to match a sketched silhouette from a viewpoint — cited in [Cordonnier 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Cordonnier-Computer_Graphics_Forum-2016-Large_Scale_Terrain_Generation_from_Tectonic_Uplift_and_Fluvial_.pdf) ref [TEC*14]. Gain, Merry, Marais 2015 "Parallel, Realistic and Controllable Terrain Synthesis" (CGF 34(2)) adds style painting, region copy-paste and curve/point manipulators to texture-based synthesis — [Schott 2023](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1) (Sec. 2).

### Inferences
- **[inference]** Hnaidi on a 256² grid in NumPy/SciPy is ~150 lines: rasterise curves with `skimage.draw`/distance transforms, build a sparse linear system (Dirichlet rows on strict elevation cells, gradient rows on slope bands, Laplace rows elsewhere) and solve once with `scipy.sparse.linalg.spsolve` instead of multigrid Jacobi; 65 k unknowns solve in well under a second. The weighted-Jacobi scheme is only needed if you want GPU-style iteration.
- **[inference]** Hnaidi's gradient constraint is what makes "plateau here" and "cliff along this line" expressible; Génevaux and Cordonnier have no direct equivalent (Génevaux approximates plateaus through λ ≈ 0; Schott through uniform uplift).
- **[inference]** Zhou/Gain/Tasse need DEM libraries, patch matching and GPU code; not worth it at 128–256 cells where a single patch would cover the map.

### Gaps
- Zhou 2007 method details (PPA feature extraction, patch sizes, timing) not retrievable: project site had an SSL error, Semantic Scholar returned 403.
- Tasse 2014 full text not read; description relies on citations in other papers.

## KQ4. Design/control knobs per method, their cost, and what fits in a few hundred lines of NumPy

### Takeaway
Three methods are cheap and complementary: Hnaidi diffusion (exact curve control, no hydrology), Génevaux network growth (controlled river *topology*, cheap, no dendritic ridges), Cordonnier/Schott uplift + stream power (hydrologically consistent dendritic relief, indirect but strong control via uplift primitives + PID constraints). Example-based methods and tectonic-plate simulation need big codebases and data.

### Cited Findings
| Method | Designer knobs (from source) | Hydrologic consistency | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Génevaux 2013 | contour, mouths, sketched river parts, node priority, ζ, P_c/P_s/P_a, edge length e, river-slope map, terrain-slope map λ ∈ [0, 0.25], Lipschitz κ — [paper](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf) | rivers monotone by construction; "greedy construction can lead to meandering crests and outcroppings of unnatural-looking hills"; noise adds small local minima | seconds on CPU (Table 2); no public author code |
| Hnaidi 2010 | per-curve elevation h + plateau radius r, slope angles θ/φ with extents a/b, noise amplitude A and roughness R; recipes for ridge, river, cliff, hill, crack — [paper](https://perso.liris.cnrs.fr/eric.galin/Articles/2010-terrain.pdf) | none (rivers are just low curves) | 10 grids, 0.25 s at 1024² on 2008 GPU |
| Cordonnier 2016 | uplift grey map (its *gradient* matters most), k, m/n (river profile shape), δt, thermal max slope (constant / noise / height-dependent) — [paper](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Cordonnier-Computer_Graphics_Forum-2016-Large_Scale_Terrain_Generation_from_Tectonic_Uplift_and_Fluvial_.pdf) | yes, incl. lakes via pass graph | 10 k nodes: 6.4 s; 160 k: 252 s (CPU) |
| Schott 2023 | Uplift Tree: segment ridges (r(x), u_S(x)), valley carvers (w ∝ a^0.4), asymmetric star primitives, warp/blend/max/erase/copy-paste; tensor-field procedural ranges; PID elevation constraints on points/curves; inverse from DEM — [paper](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1) | yes, pit-free, but no lakes | 256²: 0.5 s; 1024²: 9 s (GPU); MIT code |
| mapgen4 (Patel 2018) | paint ocean / valley / mountain with 3 brush sizes; sliders raininess, rain_shadow, evaporation, flow, lg_min_flow, lg_river_width, mountain_sharpness, mountain_jaggedness — [repo](https://github.com/redblobgames/mapgen4), [painting post](https://simblob.blogspot.com/2018/09/mapgen4-elevation-painting.html) | rivers via downslope assignment + flow accumulation; rivers noted to "flow along coastlines" and over mountain slopes sometimes — [river post](https://simblob.blogspot.com/2018/09/mapgen4-river-appearance.html) | ~120 ms live repaint, Apache-2 TS |
| Zhou 2007 / Gain 2015 / Tasse | sketched feature map; style painting, copy-paste, curve/point manipulators (Gain 2015) | none ("do not generate terrains that are hydrologically consistent" — [Schott 2023](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1) Sec. 7.4) | DEM patch libraries + GPU synthesis |
| Cordonnier 2018 / Cortial 2019 | plate shapes and motions, rifting events | yes (2018, joint erosion) | interactive only at 100² (2018) — [Schott 2023](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1) |

- Patel's own statement of the noise approach's limit: local noise "cannot support global constraints like 'there should be between 3 and 5 lakes' or global features like a river flowing from the top of the highest peak down to the ocean" — [Red Blob terrain-from-noise](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/terrain-from-noise/).
- mapgen4 elevation: three distance fields (from mountain peaks, from coastline, from mountain ranges) computed with Dijkstra (flatqueue) in ~20 ms; mountain distance uses BFS with increment = spacing · (1 + jaggedness · (rand − rand)); triangle elevation blends hills e_h = (1 + noisiness·noise4 + (1−noisiness)·noise2)·hill_height and mountains e_m = 1 − (mountain_slope/2^sharpness)·distance via e = (1−e²)·e_h + e²·e_m; downslope assigned by a priority queue from ocean triangles (elev < −0.1) upward in elevation order, giving a pre-order array t_order whose reverse accumulates flow (flow_t = flow_param·moisture², trunk += tributary, trunk elevation lowered to tributary if needed) — [mapgen4 map.ts](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/redblobgames/mapgen4/master/map.ts); [elevation painting post](https://simblob.blogspot.com/2018/09/mapgen4-elevation-painting.html). Design philosophy: "Elevation to match the desired look instead of tweaking the look to match the elevation" — [mapgen4 page](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/mapgen4/).

### Inferences
- **[inference]** Lines-of-NumPy estimates for 256²: Hnaidi sparse-solve ≈ 150; Génevaux growth + Voronoi crests (scipy.spatial.Voronoi/cKDTree) ≈ 250; Cordonnier/Schott stream-power loop with D8 receivers and sorted accumulation ≈ 150 (+100 for Schott's uplift primitives and PID). All three together are "a few hundred lines". Zhou/Tasse/Cordonnier-2018 are not.
- **[inference]** A sensible composite for your pipeline: (a) designer curves → Hnaidi gives u₀ (uplift) rather than h: ridges = high uplift bands, rivers = low-uplift corridors, plateaus = flat uplift blocks (this is exactly Schott's Uplift Tree, built with Hnaidi's rasteriser); (b) run stream power to steady state for the base shape; (c) optionally seed river *topology* with a Génevaux-style growth when you want branching rivers in specific places that uplift alone won't produce; (d) PID point constraints for exact peak heights; (e) existing erosion for detail; (f) geomorphon score to compare variants.

### Gaps
- No source gives a head-to-head on the same input comparing Génevaux vs Hnaidi vs uplift; the only explicit comparisons are qualitative statements in Schott 2023 Sec. 7.4 and Génevaux 2013 Fig. 21.

## KQ5. Open-source implementations

### Takeaway
There is MIT C++ code for the Schott uplift/stream-power authoring, a Python third-party Génevaux implementation (incomplete: no lakes/inland seas), an Apache-2 TypeScript mapgen4 with painting + rivers, GPL C++/Python FastScapeLib for stream power, and C++ particle hydrology from Nick McDonald; I found no public Hnaidi diffusion-terrain implementation.

### Cited Findings
- **bmelaiths/terrainHydrology** — Python; "Terrain Generation inspired by the 2013 paper"; inputs: binary shoreline image (white land / black ocean), river-slope greyscale (lighter = steeper), terrain-slope image; outputs out-color.png and GeoTIFF; flags -ri (m/pixel), -p (primitives per cell), -ro (output size), --num-procs; uses modified Poisson sampling from bartwronski; "currently [does] not support inland seas or lakes"; no explicit license found — [repo](https://github.com/bmelaiths/terrainHydrology).
- **H-Schott/StreamPowerErosion** — C++/CMake, MIT, release code for Schott 2023; user paints uplift (Ctrl+click), stream power simulated continuously, "equilibrium between these two forces resulting in a dendritic mountainous terrain with a correct outflowing water network" — [repo](https://github.com/H-Schott/StreamPowerErosion). Related: GPU-Gang/WebGPU-Erosion-Simulation (WebGPU port of the same idea) — [repo](https://github.com/GPU-Gang/WebGPU-Erosion-Simulation); H-Schott/MultiScaleErosion (Terrain Amplification using Multi-scale Erosion) — [repo](https://github.com/H-Schott/MultiScaleErosion).
- **eric-guerin/gradient-terrains** — C++/OpenGL/GLFW compute-shader Poisson reconstruction from PGM alpha/altitude/Laplacian maps (Guérin 2022) — [repo](https://github.com/eric-guerin/gradient-terrains).
- **fastscape-lem/fastscapelib** — "C++/Python library of efficient algorithms for landscape evolution modeling"; grids (1-D profile, 2-D raster, triangular mesh), flow graph with flow operators (single and multiple direction, sink resolvers), Stream Power Law bedrock erosion, linear-diffusion hillslope; pip and conda; GPL-3.0; docs at fastscapelib.readthedocs.io; the Fortran version is maintenance-only — [repo](https://github.com/fastscape-lem/fastscapelib); [releases](https://github.com/fastscape-lem/fastscapelib/releases).
- **weigert/SimpleHydrology** (Nick McDonald) — C++, particle-based hydraulic erosion with stream map and pool map in water.h; 729 stars; momentum/discharge maps added Jan 2023 for meandering — [repo](https://github.com/weigert/SimpleHydrology). Algorithm: stream map updated by low-pass filter waterstream = (1−lrate)·waterstream + lrate·track; particles prefer existing streams via friction/evaporation; pools via a rising "testing plane" flood fill with overflow to the lowest drain; 256² grid, volumeFactor 100, spill limit 5; "This a relatively chaotic system, and what kind of hydrology appears depends highly on the terrain" (i.e. little designer control) — [blog](https://nickmcd.me/2020/04/15/procedural-hydrology/); meandering follow-up — [blog 2023](https://nickmcd.me/2023/12/12/meandering-rivers-in-particle-based-hydraulic-erosion-simulations/). Go port of the erosion part: Flokey82/go_gens/genmap2derosion — [pkg.go.dev](https://pkg.go.dev/github.com/Flokey82/go_gens/genmap2derosion).
- **redblobgames/mapgen4** — Apache-2, JS/TypeScript (esbuild); files mapgen4.ts, map.ts (elevation/rivers), painting.ts, render.ts, worker.ts, dual-mesh/; Poisson-disc points + Delaunator; `spacing` in config.js (0.7 gives 1 M+ cells, tuned for ~25 k) — [repo](https://github.com/redblobgames/mapgen4); blog series on elevation, painting, threads, river appearance, rainfall, projection — [elevation post](https://simblob.blogspot.com/2018/08/mapgen4-elevation.html).
- Red Blob's river rendering classifies triangles as source / bend / fork / ocean and uses Bézier curves; known issues: rivers narrowing downstream, following coastlines, climbing mountain slopes, jaggedness at triangle centres — [river post](https://simblob.blogspot.com/2018/09/mapgen4-river-appearance.html).

### Inferences
- **[inference]** None of these is a drop-in for a 256² NumPy pipeline; the useful things to borrow are the algorithms (Schott's explicit loop and PID, mapgen4's elevation-ordered downslope/flow pass, Cordonnier's implicit update) rather than the code. FastScapeLib is the only pip-installable stream-power solver if you'd rather not write one, at the cost of GPL and a C++ dependency.

### Gaps
- No open-source Hnaidi-style diffusion-curves terrain generator was found (searched GitHub via web search).
- terrainHydrology's license and completeness of the primitive/ridge stage were not verifiable from the README summary.
- FastScapeLib's Python API details (class names, SPLEroder parameters) could not be fetched (docs URL 404/redirect); only the README feature list is cited.

## KQ6. Quality versus real DEMs: Horton ratios, dendritic structure, failure modes

### Takeaway
Only Schott 2023 publishes a quantitative geomorphological check (Hack's law, which the uplift method passes); Génevaux reports HS-order histograms of its own graphs but no comparison to real networks; Cordonnier offers one visual DEM side-by-side. Real drainage networks have bifurcation ratio R_B ≈ 3–5 and length ratio R_L ≈ 1.5–3.5, so these are the numbers to test any generator against. Known failures: Génevaux lacks dendritic ridges and can run flat rivers through mountains; Hnaidi/Zhou/gradient methods are not hydrologically consistent; stream-power methods lack sediment deposition (flat valley floors) and, in Schott, lakes.

### Cited Findings
- Real-network ranges: "Typical values for the bifurcation ratio (R_B) lie between 3 and 5, while length ratios (R_L) range between 1.5 and 3.5"; in a study extracting 50 real river networks from DEMs, both real rivers and Optimal Channel Networks fell within these ranges; random branching networks match Shreve's random-topology prediction R_B = 4, R_L = 2 — [Carraro & Altermatt 2022, Comm. Earth & Env.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-022-00454-1) (via search summary; full text behind a redirect at fetch time).
- Schott 2023 Hack's-law fits across nine terrains: c = 1.00–1.38, n = 0.542–0.584, within literature ranges c ∈ [1, 6], n ∈ [0.45, 0.7] (Sassolas-Serrayet 2018); small watersheds (< 10 km²) excluded "to avoid artefacts to the grid resolution" — [Schott 2023](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1) (Sec. 7.3, Table 2).
- Schott on the uplift method's own output: steady state "forms the classic dendritic cross patterns described by Bonetti et al. [2020] and Hooshyar et al. [2020]"; inverse-modelled Alps "exhibit a lack of variety in the elevation of valleys, that comes from the absence of sediment depositions" — (Sec. 4.2, 6.1).
- Schott on Génevaux: "the procedurally generated mountain ranges may not be hydrologically correct, and may not exhibit characteristic dendritic shapes" — (Sec. 7.4).
- Génevaux's own failure list: meandering crests, unnatural hill outcrops, abrupt plain/mountain transitions, flat rivers cutting through high mountains when slope maps vary strongly, no deltas/oxbows — [Génevaux 2013](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf) (Sec. 8).
- Cordonnier 2016: only a San Gabriel Mountains side-by-side; authors state visual plausibility "should be supported by some evaluation, for example by testing with human subjects"; stream-power peaks become "unrealistic sharp and high" at low drainage area without the 30° thermal cap; sediment deposition neglected — [Cordonnier 2016](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Cordonnier-Computer_Graphics_Forum-2016-Large_Scale_Terrain_Generation_from_Tectonic_Uplift_and_Fluvial_.pdf) (Sec. 5, 6.1, 7).
- Hydrological consistency of other families (Schott 2023, Sec. 7.4): procedural/example-based are not consistent; breaching (Lindsay 2016) used by Scott & Dodgson 2021 "still produces artificial canyons"; gradient-domain editing likewise needs breaching with visual artefacts — [Schott 2023](https://hal.science/hal-04049125v1).
- Génevaux's design claim that river geometry is scale-free: water-course properties are "nearly independent of the tectonic attributes and the climate [Rosgen 1994], and they look identical at different scales" [Rodriguez-Iturbe & Rinaldo 1997; Dodd & Rothman 2000] — [Génevaux 2013](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/resources/papers/Genevaux-ACM_Trans_Graph-2013-Terrain_Generation_Using_Procedural_Models_Based_on_Hydrology.pdf) (Sec. 1).

### Inferences
- **[inference]** Génevaux's grammar can be tuned to any R_B you like (P_s drives how fast order drops; Fig. 8 shows order-1 share moving from >75 % to ~59 %), but because the HS number is *imposed* rather than emergent, the resulting network statistics are what you set, not evidence of realism; measure R_B, R_L and Hack's exponent on the output with your own D8 extraction, and compare to 3–5 / 1.5–3.5 / 0.5–0.6.
- **[inference]** At 128–256 cells the Horton analysis will only have 3–4 orders; Hack's law and your geomorphon score are more robust metrics at that size than Horton ratios.
- **[inference]** Stream-power base shapes will score as "too uniformly dendritic / V-valleys everywhere" against real DEMs unless you add Cordonnier's noise-modulated thermal slope (6°–54°) or deposition; this is the known steady-state look.

### Gaps
- No paper reports Horton ratios for Génevaux, Cordonnier or Schott outputs versus real DEMs; nor any geomorphon-based comparison.
- Carraro & Altermatt 2022 full text (OCN algorithm details, R package) could not be fetched; only the summary numbers are cited.
- Galin et al. 2019 "A Review of Digital Terrain Modeling" (CGF 38(2), DOI 10.1111/cgf.13657) could not be downloaded (UCT PDF returned HTML; Wiley paywalled); its comparison tables of control vs realism were not consulted — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/cgf.13657), [UCT archive](https://pubs.cs.uct.ac.za/id/eprint/1337/).
