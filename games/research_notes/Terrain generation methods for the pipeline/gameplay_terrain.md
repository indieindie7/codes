# Gameplay-aware terrain generation and outdoor level design for action/shooter games

Scope: rules that make generated outdoor terrain *play* well (paths, slopes, cover, vistas, encounter spaces, roads) and how it integrates with placed structures. Target pipeline: 128-256 cell heightmap + layer masks + static meshes for Unreal II (UE2, 2003), driven by a story-beat spine, with erosion already available.

Legend: "[inference]" = my reasoning from the cited material, not something a source states. Research date 2026-10-06.

## Key question 1 — Level-design heuristics for outdoor combat spaces (sightlines, landmarks, cover, elevation, flanking, pacing)

### Takeaway
The published heuristics converge on a small set: one dominant landmark ("weenie") made special by contrast with its surroundings; an "approach" = a path that ends in a vista overlooking the next arena; cover kept sparse, preferably waist-high (1.0-1.25 m) so players keep reading the space; enemies partially exposed so they are noticed; exteriors deliberately give longer sightlines and flanking routes than interiors; and terrain masses (Nintendo's "triangles") used to create go-over/go-around choices and staged reveals. Hard numbers are rare; the ones that exist are listed below.

### Cited Findings
**Landmarks, weenies, vistas, composition**
- Disney "weenie": "Weenies are distant landmarks that indicate the direction and composition of a goal", coined by Walt Disney World engineers for buildings that stand above the others and draw visitors onward — [Michael Barclay, My Level Design Guidelines](https://mikebarclay.co.uk/my-level-design-guidelines/); theme-park background at [Theory of Theme Parks: The Weenie](http://theoryofthemeparks.blogspot.com/2015/08/wayfinding-in-themed-design-weenie.html)
- The Level Design Book distinguishes "Spatial composition: the overall big picture 3D arrangement of core masses" from "Shot composition: how the level looks in the player's 2D camera frame", and says spatial composition matters far more — [Level Design Book: Composition](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/massing/composition)
- Hierarchy comes from *local contrast*: height difference (tall thing among short things), density contrast (open space ringed by narrow structures), orientation breaks (an angled object in a grid), shape contrast (round among rectangular). Rule quoted: "A tall thing only seems special if it is surrounded by short things." — [Level Design Book: Composition](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/massing/composition)
- "A landmark or point of interest (POI) is a unique and memorable shape, mass, or location in a level." Landmarks must be functionally relevant; decorative skybox elements don't work as landmarks — [Level Design Book: Composition](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/massing/composition)
- "A sightline is a trajectory of empty open space that offers a possible view"; "A vista is an exceptionally deep scene composition that offers an overview of the next area"; an **approach** is "a path with a vista". Players won't use a sightline that is not relevant to their current goal — [Level Design Book: Composition](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/massing/composition)
- The book explicitly rejects "leading lines" as a design tool: they appear in any perspective screenshot and playtests show players look elsewhere; wayfinding relies on pattern recognition and mental maps — [Level Design Book: Composition](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/massing/composition)
- Wayfinding mental-map elements (Lynch): landmarks, nodes (junctions/squares), districts, paths, edges. Three attention rules: "Players don't look upwards unless something draws their eye"; "Players look in the direction they are moving"; "Players focus on contrast (in color, shape, lighting, and movement)". Sightlines are a *coarse* wayfinding method (the book rates "tall landmark in distance with wide sightline" at roughly 35-60% certainty of guiding the player); negative reinforcement = knowing where you can't go tells you where you can — [Level Design Book: Wayfinding](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/wayfinding)

**Terrain massing: Breath of the Wild "triangle rule" (CEDEC 2017, Nintendo)**
- "Using triangles carries out 2 objectives — gives players a choice as to whether to go straight over the triangle, or around, and it obscures the player's view, so designers can utilize them to surprise players." Applied at three scales: large triangles = landmarks; medium = visual screens that hide what lies beyond; small = control tempo / change inputs (climb vs run). Rectangular masses "are good for completely hiding something from sight" (hard cut rather than gradual reveal). Worked example: hill reveals a bridge structure, which conceals a tower: "a chain of interest — hill -> bridge structure -> tower." POIs of varying visibility act as "gravity" that pulls the player in several directions and lets them loop between focal points — [Matt Walker translation of CEDEC 2017 BotW talks (gist)](https://gist.github.com/idbrii/e39fe96279aa1670319bfa521d907399); secondary summary at [80.lv: Design Secrets of Breath of the Wild](https://80.lv/articles/the-design-secrets-of-breath-of-the-wild)
- Irregular triangles (extra peaks, divots) tended to hide Koroks (secrets), drawing the eye without players consciously noticing — [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/the-design-secrets-of-breath-of-the-wild)

**Cover**
- Height classes (Uncharted 4 standard, as reported by the Level Design Book): high cover 1.75 m+ (standing head height); low cover 1.0-1.25 m (waist); 0.5 m or less is not cover — [Level Design Book: Cover](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/combat/cover)
- "Less is more": too much cover creates maze-like flow and stops players noticing enemies/objectives; narrow tall cover limits options most. Low cover "drastically increased options for the player. They are now aware of things going on over the wall and can be on the lookout for flanks" (quoting Epic Games) — [Level Design Book: Cover](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/combat/cover)
- Enemy cover should leave NPCs somewhat exposed so players notice them; dense rectangular cover grids read as an artificial "cover box". Shooter layouts: enemies start behind cover, a "no man's land" middle, accessible rush routes; stealth layouts invert this (enemies exposed, centre NPC-controlled, side paths critical) — [Level Design Book: Cover](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/combat/cover)
- Max Pears (The Division, Cyberpunk 2077) gives "cover spacing of 2m" as a working metric, and on exteriors: "players will have bigger spaces to engage in combat, having flanking opportunities, as well as having a larger line of sight to deal with and keep an eye on as enemies progress." He positions some enemies "hidden from the players initial view" to reward scouting, and divides the space so information is hidden until the player moves — [Max Pears, Engaging Level Design for Combat Pt 2](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/engaging-level-design-for-combat---pt-2)
- Elevation gives a stronger height advantage depending on angle of attack (trench vs elevation); sometimes leave an area empty so systems/players fill it (Fortnite building) — [Level Design Book: Cover](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/combat/cover) (via search summary)

**Halo / Bungie encounter principles**
- Griesemer's "30 seconds of fun" is really nested loops: "a 3-second loop inside of a 30-second loop inside of a 3-minute loop that is always different". "No 30 second stretch of Halo is ever repeated; the missions are constantly changing the context on you." Encounters are tuned as a "dance": the game "waits to see what you will do, how hard you will push, and then it pushes back at just the right resistance"; when shields drop, enemies back off to allow recovery — [Engadget: Half-Minute Halo, interview with Jaime Griesemer](https://www.engadget.com/2011-07-14-half-minute-halo-an-interview-with-jaime-griesemer.html)
- The "Golden Three/Triangle" is weapons + melee + grenades (a combat-system principle, not a spatial one) — [WikiHalo: Golden Three](https://wiki.halo.fr/Golden_Three)
- Halo 3 encounters (Damian Isla, GDC 2008 "Building a Better Battle") are built from **territories**: declarative tasks ("hold this area", "man that vehicle") that squads fill in priority order ("Plinko machine" task tree), with task sets activated as the battle progresses: opening, player gains control, last stand. Three central principles: territory, a limited knowledge model, satisfying enemies — [Game Developer: Combat Evolved — the Encounter Design of Halo 3](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/combat-evolved-the-encounter-design-of-halo-3); slides at [WPI mirror of Isla's GDC 2008 slides](https://web.cs.wpi.edu/~rich/courses/imgd4000-d09/lectures/halo3.pdf); video at [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/GDC2008Isla)

**Far Cry outposts**
- Far Cry outposts support 360-degree approach: enemies placed so no approach direction is required; FC5 outposts use "intro covers" and "cover shadows", and designers decide per prop whether it is gameplay cover/vaultable or dressing — [Dmytro Nesterenko, Far Cry Arcade Outpost Level Design](https://www.artstation.com/artwork/y4mVx9) and [Mountain Outpost: Far Cry 5 Arcade Map](https://fable-and-fern.com/2021/02/01/mountain-outpost-far-cry-5-arcade-map/) (portfolio/analysis sources, not official)
- Far Cry 6 outposts: verticality above and below the main floor, establishing shots/reveals on approach, strong silhouettes, deliberate access points and vantage points — [Andrejs Verlis, FC6 World Building](https://andregeous.artstation.com/projects/oAn8mL) (portfolio source)

### Inferences
- [inference] For a heightmap generator, the actionable composition rule is: pick ONE dominant landmark per beat (a placed building or a stamped peak), and enforce contrast by *lowering/flattening* terrain in a ring around it rather than raising the landmark; then guarantee an "approach" = the player's path reaches a point from which the landmark and the next arena are both visible before the path descends into it.
- [inference] Nintendo's triangle rule maps directly onto terrain stamping: medium "triangles" (ridges/knolls 2-4x player height) placed between the approach and the arena hide the reveal; small triangles (0.5-1.5x player height bumps) inside arenas work as terrain cover; the choice "over or around" requires the over-route slope to stay walkable (see Q2).
- [inference] Combining Pears' 2 m spacing with the "less is more" rule: for a shooter arena of ~40x40 m, something like 8-15 cover pieces, mostly waist-high (1.0-1.25 m), is a sane starting density; terrain berms of ~1.1 m can replace props. Treat this as a tuning start, not a published number.
- [inference] Halo's "territory" model suggests the generator should emit named territories (a defensible high spot, a fallback area, a flank route) per arena, so that the UE2 AI scripting can be given hold/advance/retreat areas rather than raw spawn points.

### Gaps
- No primary source found for a Bungie GDC talk literally titled "Design of Halo 3's encounters"; the available talk is Isla's AI-objectives talk, which is about scripting systems rather than spatial rules.
- Valve's "Rules of Engagement" / HL2 Ep2 "funnel then open" commentary: not located this session; the commentary exists in-game (Lost Coast was the first game with it) but I found no transcript to cite. Mark the "funnel then open" pacing pattern as unsourced here.
- No published numbers for landmark visibility distances, sightline lengths, or elevation-advantage heights for outdoor arenas.
- Steve Lee's "An Approach to Holistic Level Design" (GDC 2017) was not fetched; no concrete outdoor rules extracted from him.

## Key question 2 — Procedural rules that encode playability (slopes, roads, pads, terrain cover, sightline evaluation, arena templates)

### Takeaway
Industry pipelines (Far Cry 2/5, Ghost Recon Wildlands) all do roads the same way: a slope-and-curvature-weighted pathfinder between designer waypoints, with existing roads nearly free so networks branch, then re-flow the terrain to the road and regrow vegetation/masks automatically. Settlements are placed by scoring many rules per building (slope, distance to centre/water/rail, spacing between like buildings) with landmarks placed first. Walkable-slope limits in engines default to about 45 degrees (floor-normal Z >= 0.7), so generated routes should be kept well under that, with stairs/ramps where the pad-to-pad grade exceeds it.

### Cited Findings
**Slope limits**
- Unreal Engine's Character Movement Walkable Floor Angle defaults to about 45 degrees; steeper surfaces cannot be climbed and the character slides — [Epic: Walkable Slope in Unreal Engine](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/walkable-slope-in-unreal-engine)
- Unity CharacterController Slope Limit default 45 degrees; Godot floor_max_angle default 45 degrees — [uhiyama-lab Unity notes](https://uhiyama-lab.com/en/notes/unity/unity-charactercontroller-3d-movement/) (search summary)
- UE3/UDK Pawn defaults: WalkableFloorZ = 0.7 (minimum floor-normal Z; below it the surface is not walkable), MaxStepHeight = 35 units — [Unreal Wiki: UE3 Pawn internal variables](https://wiki.beyondunreal.com/UE3:Pawn_internal_variables_(UDK)); [UnCodeX Engine.Pawn (UT3)](http://greatemerald.eu/uncodex/UT3/engine/pawn.html)
- Academic terrain generators keep slopes "within playable ranges, typically 0-60 degrees in game units" — [Doran & Parberry, Controlled Procedural Terrain Generation Using Software Agents](https://ianparberry.com/pubs/terrain.pdf) (search summary)
- Village/road generation paper: paths via A* "with cost functions that consider terrain slope, producing realistic paths that avoid slopes that are too steep" — [Procedural Content Generation of Villages and Road System on Arbitrary Terrain (SBGames 2018)](https://www.sbgames.org/sbgames2018/files/papers/ComputacaoFull/188241.pdf)

**Roads that re-flow terrain**
- Far Cry 2 (Dominic Guay, Ubisoft Montreal): when designers moved roads, "the terrain will actually smooth in according to how the rules were designed, and the vegetation will move and regrow"; designers then hand-refine. A level designer + artist built 1 km² of jungle with terrain, vegetation, roads, structures, AI and gameplay in about 4 hours as a proof of concept. Tools took ~15x more effort than the engine; vegetation system ~3 years after an 8-month prototype; one undocumented late parameter tweak raised asset density across the 50 km² world by 25% — [Game Developer: MIGS — Far Cry 2's Guay on procedural content](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/migs-i-far-cry-2-i-s-guay-on-the-importance-of-procedural-content); the GDC 2008 talk itself is [GDC Vault: Procedural Data Generation in FAR CRY 2](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/355/Procedural-Data-Generation-in-FAR) (not fetched)
- Ghost Recon Wildlands (Ubisoft Paris, Houdini): "The road builder toolset is basically a customized pathfinding algorithm that follows the terrain features", run in a loop, adding roads sequentially onto the network. Costs: steep slopes cost more than shallow grades; tight turns cost more than sweeping curves; following an existing road is "dirt cheap" so new roads branch from existing ones. Quote: "By defining in the pathfinder features such as steep slopes or making lots of tight turns in the road costs more than shallow roads with less turns, we make the pathfinder look for cheaper alternatives." Terraforming outputs heightmaps, materials and masks via a render farm in minutes — [80.lv: Procedural World Building in Ghost Recon Wildlands](https://80.lv/articles/procedural-world-building-in-ghost-recon-wildlands)
- Wildlands terrain: 50 cm precision heightmap plus hardware tessellation; custom in-editor sculpting replaced World Machine tiles — [80.lv: Procedural Technology in Ghost Recon Wildlands](https://80.lv/articles/procedural-technology-in-ghost-recon-wildlands); GDC 2017 talk "Terrain Tools and Technology" — [SideFX forum thread](https://www.sidefx.com/forum/topic/48536/)

**Building/settlement placement**
- Wildlands settlement algorithm: towns split into road-separated areas; 70+ placement rules evaluated per building (proximity to town centre, water, railways; terrain slope; designer districts; density of like buildings, e.g. churches not within ~150 m of each other); landmark structures placed first, then secondary fill; large towns took up to 20 min to cook — [80.lv: Procedural World Building in Ghost Recon Wildlands](https://80.lv/articles/procedural-world-building-in-ghost-recon-wildlands)
- Horizon Zero Dawn (GDC 2017, Jaap van Muijden): artists define placement rules in a graph editor; a "footprint" sets the average spacing between placed pointers; ecotypes group prototypes with placement rules; density controlled by masks/noise; the system lets you "paint in a tree line and redirect roads" as easily as moving mountains — [Guerrilla: GPU-based procedural placement](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/read/gpu-based-procedural-placement-in-horizon-zero-dawn); [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024700/GPU-Based-Run-Time-Procedural); [80.lv summary](https://80.lv/articles/the-procedural-nature-of-the-horizon-zero-dawn)

**Path-constrained terrain (academic)**
- Andereck, "Procedural Terrain Generation Based on Constraint Paths" (OSU MSc 2014, advisor Crawfis): user-designed paths are hard constraints — "terrain shape should not manipulate the heights specified by these paths"; an iterative solver finds the best-fitting terrain interpolating between and beyond path points; extra constraints: real elevation data, painted heights, tile boundaries — [OhioLINK ETD](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/etd/r/1501/10?clear=10&p10_accession_num=osu1388357258)
- Noise-based terrain with elevation constraints from GPS routes, points and areas of interest — [Procedural modelling of terrains with constraints (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343724705_Procedural_modelling_of_terrains_with_constraints) (abstract only; page returned 403 on fetch)
- Agent-based generation (coastline, mountain, river agents) to satisfy designer constraints while keeping novelty — [Doran & Parberry](https://ianparberry.com/pubs/terrain.pdf)

**Sightline evaluation (isovists/viewsheds)**
- Isovist = set of points visible from a vantage point (Tandy 1967, Benedikt 1979); metrics: area, perimeter, occlusivity, variance, skewness, circularity; visibility graph analysis extends this to whole layouts — [Turner, Doxa, O'Sullivan, Penn 2001: From isovists to visibility graphs](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/160/1/turner-doxa-osullivan-penn-2001.pdf); survey of landscape visibility tools — [MDPI 2023 survey](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/20/3/1788)

### Inferences
- [inference] Unreal II is UE2, whose Pawn movement uses the same floor-normal-Z test as later Unreal (the 0.7 constant is MINFLOORZ in UE1/UE2 native code as far as I recall); acos(0.7) ≈ 45.6 degrees. I did not find a UE2-specific document, so verify in the Unreal II Pawn/Engine source. Practical generator limits: keep designed routes under ~30 degrees (comfortable, matches the brief's 30-35 degree figure, which I could not source directly), allow up to ~40 degrees on optional shortcuts, treat >45 degrees as a wall/edge.
- [inference] Road recipe consistent with FC2/Wildlands: A* over the heightmap with cost = length x (1 + k_s x slope^2) + k_turn x heading change, existing roads at near-zero cost; then set the road centreline height to a smoothed profile (clamp grade to a max, e.g. 15-20% for vehicles) and blend terrain to it with a cosine falloff over 1-3 cells either side; re-run masks (cliff/grass/dirt) afterwards; run erosion *before* roads so the road flattening is not washed out.
- [inference] Building pads: cut-and-fill to the mean height of the footprint, extend the flat zone 1-2 cells beyond the footprint, blend over 2-4 cells; score candidate sites by slope (< 10 degrees), distance from the road, and spacing from like buildings, as Wildlands does; place the landmark building first.
- [inference] Terrain-as-cover: stamp berms/knolls of 1.0-1.25 m (waist high) inside arenas and 1.75 m+ at arena edges; keep terrain cover sparse (a few per arena) per the "less is more" rule; orient berms so they protect against the expected enemy direction but leave flank angles open.
- [inference] Arena templates that match the terrain massing rules: a bowl (enemies on the rim, player enters low = "trench" disadvantage, use for ambush beats), a saddle between two knolls (two flank routes), a ridge approach (player high, vista over the enemy camp = "approach" + elevation advantage). Each template declares an approach point whose viewshed must include the landmark.
- [inference] Sightline check: sample isovist/viewshed area along the route on the heightmap (ray-march per sample point, ~360 rays, eye height ~1.7 m). Expect low area in "funnel" segments and a sharp rise at the approach point; if the landmark is not in the viewshed from the approach point, raise the approach or lower the intervening terrain.

### Gaps
- Far Cry 5's GDC 2018 "Procedural World Generation" (Etienne Carrier) notes page at tools.engineer could not be fetched (returned only a path stub twice); it reportedly covers terrain slope thresholds, roads, fences and biome rules. Worth retrying or watching the GDC Vault video.
- No published numeric cost weights or max grades for FC2/Wildlands roads; no published blend widths for terrain re-flow.
- The 30-35 degree comfortable-slope figure from the brief was not found in any source; engine defaults are 45 degrees.
- Horizon Zero Dawn slides (PDF/PPT linked from the Guerrilla page) were not fetched, so no slope/density numbers from them.

## Key question 3 — Games with documented procedural / semi-procedural outdoor layouts

### Takeaway
Documented cases are all "procedural assist, designer intent": Far Cry 2 (rules re-flow terrain and vegetation around designer-moved roads), Ghost Recon Wildlands (Houdini road pathfinder + rule-scored towns), Horizon Zero Dawn (GPU rule graphs for placement, roads/tree lines painted), Breath of the Wild (hand-authored but rule-driven terrain massing). Academic PCG adds path-constrained and agent-based terrain. None of them generate the *encounter layout itself* procedurally in a way I could source.

### Inferences
- [inference] The common architecture across FC2/Wildlands/HZD is: terrain first, then *linear features* (roads) that locally override terrain, then *point features* (buildings) scored against terrain + roads, then *mask regeneration* (vegetation/material) that respects both. For a 128-256 cell map this is cheap enough to run per story beat and to iterate.

### Gaps
- Just Cause 2, Death Stranding, Valheim/Enshrouded, Unexplored: not researched this session (tool budget); no sources collected.
- No source found describing any shipped game procedurally generating *combat encounter layout* (cover/flank/territory) on terrain; this appears to remain designer-authored in all cases found.

## Key question 4 — Evaluation: playability metrics and how teams tested generated terrain

### Takeaway
Published evaluation is mostly academic: agent-based simulated playtesting (PathOS, exploratory agents) and metric suites (route redundancy, line-of-sight variety, landmark visibility, reachability validators). Industry teams relied on fast iteration (minutes-long terraforming cooks, hand refinement after rules) rather than formal metrics, as far as the sources show.

### Cited Findings
- Automated level analysis arose because manual inspection and playtesting do not scale to PCG volumes; approaches include graph metrics, spatial decomposition and simulation-based analysis — [Linköping thesis (DiVA)](https://liu.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:2069185/FULLTEXT01.pdf) (search summary)
- A 31-metric map characterisation split into level-design-pattern metrics and visual-impression metrics; quality scores include repeated room count, route redundancy, line-of-sight variety, distance between rest points, landmark visibility, consecutive minutes at the same intensity — [On the Evaluation of Procedural Level Generation Systems (arXiv 2404.18657)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.18657) and the Linköping thesis above (search summaries; attribution of each metric to a specific paper not verified)
- Reachability analysis: not every area is reachable given physics/avatar; areas classified as reachable vs danger — [Comprehensive Model of Automated Evaluation of Difficulty in Platformer Games (ACM)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3705013) (search summary)
- Procedural personas (MCTS with evolved heuristics) generate artificial playtraces to give designers feedback on authored levels — [Holmgård et al., Automated Playtesting with Procedural Personas (arXiv 1802.06881)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.06881)
- PathOS (Stahlke, Nova, Mirza-Babaei): free open-source Unity framework; agents with tunable player-type settings wander a navmesh over simple markup to show how players may navigate and where they get stuck — [GitHub samanthastahlke/pathos](https://github.com/samanthastahlke/pathos); [Artificial Players in the Design Process (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346639217_Artificial_Players_in_the_Design_Process_Developing_an_Automated_Testing_Tool_for_Game_Level_and_World_Design); follow-up exploratory agents — [arXiv 2409.02632](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.02632v1); [CEUR Vol-4090 paper 5](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4090/paper5.pdf)
- Hard-failure validators used in practice: goal unreachable, navmesh fails to connect required zones — [playtex.ai blog](https://www.playtex.ai/blog/how-to-use-procedural-generation-without-making-boring-levels) (low-authority vendor blog)
- Isovist metrics (area, perimeter, occlusivity, etc.) are the standard quantitative vocabulary for visibility — [Turner et al. 2001](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/160/1/turner-doxa-osullivan-penn-2001.pdf)
- Industry iteration speed as the test loop: Wildlands terraforming results "within minutes"; FC2 designers hand-refine after the rules run — [80.lv Wildlands](https://80.lv/articles/procedural-world-building-in-ghost-recon-wildlands); [Game Developer FC2](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/migs-i-far-cry-2-i-s-guay-on-the-importance-of-procedural-content)
- The Level Design Book stresses playtesting over heuristics for wayfinding (its certainty percentages are rough designer estimates) — [Level Design Book: Wayfinding](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/wayfinding)

### Inferences
- [inference] A metric set for this pipeline, derived from the above: (1) route slope histogram along the beat path, with hard fail if any required segment exceeds the UE2 walkable angle; (2) reachability of every beat anchor from the spawn via flood-fill over walkable cells (hard fail); (3) isovist area along the route (want low -> spike at the approach -> moderate in the arena); (4) landmark-visible fraction of route samples (want > some threshold on the approach, ~0 in the funnel); (5) cover count and spacing in each arena (target ~2 m min spacing, sparse); (6) fraction of the arena from which the player spawn is visible (enemy exposure / "no man's land" check); (7) route redundancy = number of edge-disjoint walkable routes between approach and arena (flanks >= 2).
- [inference] The pilot test driver already in the project (U2Pilot) can play the PathOS role: drive a bot along the beat path and log slope, stuck events and visible-landmark flags, which is cheaper than porting PathOS to UE2.

### Gaps
- No published numeric thresholds (e.g. target isovist area, cover-visibility fraction) for shooter outdoor arenas were found.
- No account from Ubisoft/Guerrilla/Nintendo of formal metrics for generated terrain; only iteration-speed anecdotes.
