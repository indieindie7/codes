## What to take, ranked for the Advent d3d8 fork
1. **TAA/SMAA T2x done narrowly** (SnNm7rSSvlg, SYUL1tt_olk): blur comes from the history weight and a wide resolve;
   blend only current + previous SMAA output, restrict the temporal blend to the edge mask, small resolve. Check our
   TAA share (0.1) against this; a longer jitter sequence is fine at high fps.
2. **GI cascades pass** (v8_JmK906fw, snXTGrjfOvQ): plane-distance + normal weighting in the cascade upsample; a jittered
   probe pixel per 16x16 tile accumulated through TAA; visibility-based sample rejection against leaks; YCoCg box clamp
   on the history. Real tracing / surface cards / compute scatter are out of reach in ps_3_0.
3. **AO**: GTAO (lighting-aware, skips directly lit pixels; ~0.5 ms on 2 TFLOP) paired with a small-radius HBAO-like
   pass to avoid halos; cheap temporal filter instead of a wide denoise; Kawase blur instead of Gaussian (N6sLJhI_Fmg,
   0njXa-OknIs, and the batch E notes below).
4. **Floor SSR**: elongate specular by view angle and roughness (Frostbite NFS 2015); specular occlusion for off-screen
   fallback reflections.
5. **Surface shaders** (our per-texture replacements): texture fetches are the cost, not ALU (Dead Space paper), so keep
   taps few, use BC1/BC3 and height in DXT5 alpha; Burley/Oren-Nayar diffuse + smooth terminator in replaced shaders
   (not for baked lightmaps); jitter LOD dither fades per object, two-position alternation stays stable without TAA.
6. **Post**: GT/GT7-style tone curve; auto exposure from diffuse-only luminance; motion blur sampling backward only with
   a depth cutoff, 3 taps + separable second pass, white noise over IGN; FXAA only at preset 39 subpix 0 if ever used.
7. **Shadows**: shadow map texel density ~30% above on-screen; floors out of the caster pass; static+dynamic in one atlas.

## SnNm7rSSvlg — Tutorial: How To Optimize Almost Every Step In Modern Game Rendering (121m)
Non-technical: Patreon pipeline chart (deferred, MSAA-based, 1080p, DX11-class) walked left to right; calls out Epic/UE5, Digital Foundry, studios as "damage control". Ignored.

### Frame start / clears
- GPU is not a place for CPU work: start-of-frame compute should stay tiny; Blueprint up to ~10x slower than C++ is the reason for GPU-driven bloat (AC Unity cited).
- Do not hardware-clear G-buffers/AO outputs that the next pass fully overwrites ("full-screen override"); only depth plus buffers with partial coverage (emissive, dual-normal/skin, MSAA coverage+motion buffer) need clears. "Hijack clear": the base pass shader writes extra outputs with clear-equivalent data for free.
- Never clear a render target with a full-screen triangle or quad; hardware clear is always faster. Clear cost varies by format/engine (one engine cleared RGBA8 2-3x faster than UE). Any saving of 0.1 ms is worth it.

### Depth pre-pass / base pass
- Shader cost = pixel area x per-pixel efficiency; depth/stencil testing (early-Z, EQUAL) beats branching or masks for skipping work. Reverse-Z, 32-bit depth.
- Partial pre-pass: only large/close geometry, pure depth, no texture sampling (no alpha test, no per-pixel displacement). Target under 0.3 ms (Alyx does a full non-alpha-tested pre-pass in urban scenes at that cost). Distant small geometry is skipped: quad overdraw (2x2 pixel quads; pre-pass can double it) makes it a net loss.
- Alpha-tested foliage: pre-pass costs about as much as its base pass because it samples opacity. Crysis 3 tree: 0.13 ms with pre-pass; base-pass-only with depth write on was ~40% faster in total (13% faster even with depth write off). Silent Hill 2 remake test: 0.6 ms with pre-pass vs 0.44 ms without; KCD2 gained performance with its pre-pass disabled. So: draw alpha-tested content first in the base pass (it supplies depth), then opaque non-pre-passed, then pre-passed content last with EQUAL depth.
- Quad overdraw, not poly count, is the artist metric. LOD transitions and shadow-map draws should use an ALU 2-position jittered Bayer dither (Days Gone good, KCD2 shadow popping bad).
- G-buffer ideas: RGB10A2 normals (Callisto uses 10-bit) with anisotropy flag + lighting-channel stencil in the 2 alpha bits; albedo+roughness packed (NFS 2015); dual material-profile ID + blend channel instead of metallic (profile slot limit ~8000); specular channel scales a high-precision IOR stored in the profile.
- Pack motion vectors + MSAA coverage in one buffer; MSAA coverage (SV_Coverage) should come from vertex-baked flat-edge info, because Crysis 3's depth/normal-derived fake mask drops too many edge points. Vector-motion pre-pass for dual-normal objects.
- After base pass: a G-buffer resolve builds a sample-frequency stencil (Crysis 3 / Days Gone style) into the hardware stencil; sky and empty samples share one stencil value. Avoid decal buffers (Fox Engine decals write all channels except shading-model ID).

### Shadows and lighting
- Perspective shadow maps (two-decade-old paper) instead of raising shadow-map resolution (4-8x depth cost); cap at 2048^2; resolution shrinks with distance ("1080p + 8% rule" from Days Gone). Cull casters that cannot reach the view.
- Stencil-volume lighting (Fox, Crysis 3, Days Gone) beat compute all-lights-in-one for per-light cost; use early stencil test with MSAA. One full-screen triangle per shading model (Days Gone) instead of light-volume geometry for the lighting draw. Skin is the only draw sampling the dual-normal buffer.
- Shadow softening is not artist-exposed (hurts bias); distant permutation swaps to noiseless high-sample screen-space shadows. Cascades: Crysis 3 six cascades ~1.2 ms (target half, worst cost from alpha-tested/high-poly far cascades); KCD2 five cascades with far ones updated across frames; far field should use approximated shadows (Days Gone foliage atlas, SDF shadows). Shadow mask in a native 8-bit buffer; floors should not cast shadows.
- GI: shadow-map-fed, then world-space trace; skip reflective shadow maps (0.3-0.6 ms/frame). Baked grids/probes (Days Gone, The Division, Alan Wake 2, MGSV) light static and dynamic objects identically and cost less memory than lightmaps; budget under 2 ms. Reflections same layered idea.
- Eyes and hair forward rendered (stencil-tested); eye layers cost under 0.2 ms in Days Gone/Callisto; want tear lines, adaptive pupils, internal pupil flare.
- Screen-space SSS: Callisto full-res RGBA16 ~1.4 ms; half-res blend 80% looks near-identical at 3x speed but shows upscale artifacts (needs ID+depth-aware upscale); a Crysis 3 style pixel-shader SSS with stencil is ~3x faster than Callisto's; ~0.3 ms typical. Use 2x2 Bayer jitter if banding.
- Lighting buffer RGBA16 (KCD2, Dead Space, MGSV look best) vs Crysis 3's two RG11B10 combined into RGBA16; keep a separate diffuse-only buffer.

### Sky, effects, tone mapping
- Sky/cloud cost should scale with base-pass area; GT7 sky presentation recommended. Fog via atlas or ALU. Half-res min-max-depth effects and sprites (MGSV) with a responsive mask in alpha for TAA.
- Tone mapping: GT curve (long linear section, contrast toe, smooth shoulder; Callisto uses it). GT7 tone mapper keeps hue shifts toward yellow/white (matches eye perception) vs Frostbite display mapping washing out. Auto exposure from a downsampled diffuse-only buffer (Fox) excluding emissives, plus auto white balance with min/max range and artist bias. 3D 64^3 RGBA16 LUT (Callisto) to speed the pass; non-linear LUT sampling to cut banding. BT.2020 working space worth a look. MGSV: 24-bit lighting, then LUT grade with raised highlights / lowered shadows (acts as a contrast toe).

### Anti-aliasing
- SMAA 4x = SMAA S2x (MLAA per MSAA subsample) + SMAA T2x temporal on top; MSAA only supersamples temporally unstable edges. Crysis 3 SMAA ~1 ms worst case (grass), much cheaper typically; SMAA author's paper gives ~40% speedups plus high-contrast edge fix (sample code) and smoother pattern handling. UE5, CryEngine 5 and Nixxes ports miss edges vs the reference.
- Temporal: blend only the current SMAA output with the previous frame's SMAA output (not the previous temporal output), so no infinite history, no ghosting at 60 fps. UE's TAA uses a wide resolve (r.TemporalAA.FilterSize 1) that blurs textures; use a narrow resolve (Days Gone does, plus slight sharpening; NIS-style sharpening can be merged). UE5.8 TSR needs ~2.2 ms (18x the SMAA temporal) at larger accumulation.
- Ghosting fixes: responsive mask for VFX; velocity weighting with real motion vectors for foliage (nearly free if written in the base pass) combined with color clamping.
- Texture blur fix: jitter textures with the camera, and temporal-blend only where the MSAA/edge mask says (TSMAA idea; FFXIV does it with CMAA2). Better: programmable sample locations (PSL) giving a fixed 4-sample pattern (Decima's horizontal/vertical-offset pattern) with no camera jitter and no frame blending.

### Motion blur, composite, UI
- Motion blur: pack depth+velocity in one native RG11B10 (tile max 120x68 generated in the same draw); skip the output clear; tile max/dilation causes blocky outlines. Remove camera velocity beyond a depth cutoff (U-shaped falloff) so near-field blur stays and far stays sharp. Sample only backward along velocity (NFS 2015) - modern engines blur symmetrically. MGSV: uniform 3 samples/direction + separable second pass at half res (a Crytek idea), smoother than noise; UE's separable pass is weaker; IGN and blue noise look worse than checkerboard.
- Composite effects (flares, bloom, rain/lens drops, grain, chromatic aberration, grade) in half-res RGBA16 buffers with half-res depth; Crysis 3's native-res composite buffer wastes time. UI on its own target with gamma correction in the overlay draw; Callisto brightness slider praised.

### For us
- Advent d3d8 fork: SMAA/light TAA already there; the new points are "blend only the SMAA output of the last frame (two-frame, narrow resolve)" and "restrict the temporal blend to the SMAA edge mask so textures stay sharp". Check our light TAA matches both (our view). Also skip clears of our own pass targets when a full-screen pass overwrites them; half-res composite for SSAO/effects is already known (Fox).
- Our SSS-free/forward setup: auto exposure from a diffuse-only (lightmap/unlit-vs-albedo) buffer to avoid dark-material bias is only partly feasible; we have no G-buffer, so approximate (our view). GT tone curve (linear mid, contrast toe, smooth shoulder) is a cheap swap for our grade LUT.
- Bevy (Avalon, Goobi): we own the renderer, so partial pre-pass skipping alpha-tested cutouts (draw cutout sprites/foliage first with depth write, no pre-pass for them), no pointless target clears, backward-only separable motion blur, camera-motion blur with depth cutoff, and 2x2 Bayer dither for LOD and shadow fades are all directly usable; most of the deferred/shadow/GI items are overkill for PS1 style (our view).

## sjZX5u3I4rM — How Textures & GPU Utilization Make Or Break Optimization (18m)
- Texture compression: PNG is 24-bit/32-bit per pixel; Half-Life Alyx scene has ~200 textures at 1024^2 or higher. Block compression (4x4 blocks) gets well below 5 bits per pixel.
- Source: Dead Space Remake texture packing paper (EA): automated texture packing system, scenes recompiled with different packing schemes for A/B tests, measured on Series X, 1080p G-buffer base pass. ALU utilisation has huge headroom (material complexity could double with little effect), draws are bound by texture memory addressing.
- Findings: BC7 performs about as slow as near-uncompressed; BC1 is extremely efficient; shipped scheme is BC1-heavy, uses lower resolution or drops the last BC4 per asset, with a BC5 + BC7 combination used because BC1-only showed block artifacts. His read: artifacts come from BC1 normals (normal data degrades BC1); BC7 with one correlated channel in alpha is much better than BC1 for that; BC4/BC5 is too expensive for its limited channel use. Micro-detail textures cost ~4 ms; tile-material workflow option cost 3.2 ms vs 2.8 ms, but he judges that quality gain is small vs his own ("TI") compression scheme from a prior video. Even the Dead Space protagonist uses BC7 tangent normals.
- Workflow point: artists supply a texture set, the engine automatically filters and packs at build time; Epic has not standardised this; industry is pushing neural texture compression instead (he says block compression is barely explored and cost of neural is high).
- Render format math: formats like RGB10A2, R11G11B10, D/S formats; deferred needs big uncompressed targets. Shadow mask should be 8-bit (Callisto Protocol: 8-bit R; Days Gone: RG8 with green holding skin/plant translucency + far/CSM split); Unreal uses 32-bit shadow mask and also reads two extra G-buffers (opacity in one alpha, shading model in another) for each shadowed light = ~24+24 wasted bits per light.
- Bandwidth analysis: lighting cost = pixel invocations x input bits. Callisto BRDF draws cost more from 200+ extra bits of inputs, not ALU; only ~4% of the screen is skin yet 240 extra skin bits are read everywhere. Better: draw the light twice - once with base G-buffer and stencil that excludes skin, once with skin inputs stenciled to skin only (cost ~4%).
- Days Gone: custom UE4, three 32-bit G-buffer sets (Unreal stock uses four), bit-shifted custom data, stencil-restricted local lights without shadow masks, custom skin SSS LUT. His UE5 shadow mask tests (1024^2 shadow map, softening off): UE's simplest projection already costs more than Days Gone's; reducing input bits didn't help on the 3060 because the draw is bound by writing the 32-bit mask (pixel-invocation bound), so extra ALU/softening taps are nearly free; shadow softening raises cost more than bigger shadow maps.
- For us: Advent fork: our replaced per-texture pixel shaders are texture-bound too (our view): prefer fewer taps from lower-bit-depth sources; our graded textures should stay DXT1/DXT5 (BC1/BC3), avoid uncompressed 32-bit or BC7-equivalents for added parallax/height maps; pack height into a DXT5 alpha. Bevy: good rule for Avalon - BC1 albedo + small BC5 normal, shadow mask R8, don't spend memory on uncompressed intermediates. Nothing new on the SSR/AA front.

## snXTGrjfOvQ — Large-Scale Global Illumination at Activision (SIGGRAPH 2021, Advances in Real-Time Rendering) (52m)
Speaker: Ari (Activision). Precomputed GI for Call of Duty (Black Ops Cold War, Warzone, MW2 ground war maps). Fully baked, no runtime tracing.
- Representation: irradiance volume, each voxel stores linear SH (RGB, L1 = 4 coeffs x 3) irradiance. Lights static and dynamic geometry, volumetrics and particles with one lookup. Chosen because it is a unified solution (3D texture fetch).
- Adaptivity by warping, not hierarchy: rays are shot from above and below the map to find where the detailed geometry is (trees, buildings, "yellow" region). The volume's height axis is warped to a normalized height so the sparse detail band gets more voxels. Lookup = compute normalized height, then one ordinary 3D texture fetch. Height changes are smoothed so there are no seams, no block or level boundaries, no patching. 2D footprint of the warped volume is the domain used later for compression.
- Light leaking cause: voxel size is larger than the geometry (roof, door, wall). Naive volumetric sampling (random position in voxel, random direction, splat to the trilinear neighbours) averages indoor (dark) and outdoor (bright) samples. Indoor/outdoor split volumes need artist plane mark-up; normal-aware reconstruction does not fix the cause.
- Fix = visibility-based sample validation: treat the player camera as having a prior (mostly outdoors/open). For every candidate sample position, shoot visibility rays; if hit distance is short (under a fixed threshold) the sample is inside geometry/enclosed and is rejected; samples that see far (sky) are kept. Only valid samples shoot the real gather rays, splatted trilinearly into voxels. Removes dark blotches on roofs and around buildings, keeps shadow detail under eaves, doorways and windows. Leftover blotches are just Monte Carlo noise.
- SH de-ringing: plain least-squares projection to L1 rings, goes negative and shifts colour (red cast). Windowing (Sloan) only makes it non-negative and still colour-shifts. Their method constrains the SH so the reconstruction stays inside a colour bounding box (mean and max of the reference, in YCoCg because it decorrelates channels), borrowed from TAA colour clamping.
- How: method of alternating projections between two convex sets. Use a reproducing-kernel (overcomplete frame) change of basis: SH coefficients <-> function values at N fixed sphere directions (kernel matrix and its canonical dual). Loop: SH -> directional values -> clamp to the box (non-negativity = another max) -> back to SH. Converges because the intersection is non-empty (the DC colour lies in it). Output is still plain SH, so no special basis at runtime. Also usable for hemisphere/cap constraints (match only the visible hemisphere better than plain LSQ).
- Memory problem: a plain linear-SH RGB volume for a Cold War campaign map is 1.5 GB source (48 bytes per voxel in their count). Direct and indirect light-transport data (direct + indirect matrices, indirect per colour channel) are stacked into a 324-dimensional vector per voxel for the compression tests.
- Compression = Moving Basis Decomposition (MBD, EGSR 2021 paper). Data ~ sum_l c_l(x) * b_l(x): both the coefficients and the basis vectors vary smoothly with position, each expanded in overlapping compact "hat" kernels. A few high-dimensional basis vectors on coarse kernels with large overlap, many low-dimensional coefficients on fine kernels. Overlap is what removes the cluster/block discontinuities of block-wise PCA, windowed PCA and cluster PCA (cluster PCA also needs a stored cluster index, so more memory). With linear hats it is already a quadratic model per cell.
- Solver: global sparse optimisation (compact kernels overlap only neighbours), quasi-Newton with diagonal Hessian, gradients by Monte Carlo; production problems 100M+ unknowns, solves in minutes. Offline only. In tests MBD had the lowest MSE at equal basis count and was robust to kernel support size, where block PCA artifacts get worse as blocks grow.
- Result in game: ~1 byte per voxel for fully directional RGB L1 irradiance, ~40:1 vs 48 B/voxel (1.5 GB -> roughly 40 MB). The basis kernels are 2D (planar projection of the warped volume), the coefficient kernels are 3D hats = the trilinear texel resampling of the volume texture. Reconstruction is done per pixel every frame (cost not given in ms). Baked point lights (night Warzone map) live in the volume, so far views run at 60 fps with no runtime lights.
- Limits/artifacts: no dynamic GI (fully baked); needs hours of offline bake + solver; leaking still depends on the camera prior (a camera inside a "rejected" space sees overly bright ambient); SH L1 is soft and directional-lit only, no specular; no ms numbers given.

What does carry: (1) visibility-based validation idea = when our world cache/"GiCache" accumulates samples per cell, reject or down-weight cells whose neighbourhood in depth is enclosed (short depth gap) to avoid dark-blotch leaking between room and outdoor. (our view, only if we see blotches in cache cells). Cheap in ps_3_0.

## v8_JmK906fw — MARKitekta "How Lumen Fakes the Light ... Screen-space Radiance Caching" (38m)

- History: irradiance caching (sparse, interpolated, splotchy) -> radiance caching (Krivanek et al. 2005): samples sit slightly above surfaces, gather radiance in all directions, store as high-order SH (order 10 = 121 coeffs, ~3.6 KB per sample vs 108 B for L2 irradiance probes), 500-5000 rays per sample; glossy reflection = look up reflection direction in the SH. - Cost of that: memory, and SH cannot hold sharp reflections. Replacement for directions: octahedral maps (Praun/Hoppe 2003): sphere onto octahedron unfolded to a square, no seams, near-uniform texel use, easy lookup. DDGI (2019) = ray-traced probes with visibility, updated over many frames in batches; Hardware RT (2018 RTX) made it feasible.
- Lumen screen probes: place one probe per tile on a regular screen grid, 16x16 pixel tiles (256 px) at the coarsest level. Probe uses G-buffer depth/normal to find its 3D point and traces from there (tracing happens in world space, placement in screen space). Budget argument: only ~0.5 ray per pixel is affordable, so 16x16 tile with 128 rays; Wright's "need ~100 rays/pixel outdoors, ~500 indoors" is why probes are shared.
- Each frame a different pixel of the tile is chosen as the probe (jitter), so history accumulates over time.
- Adaptive refinement: interpolate a pixel from its surrounding probes only if normal agrees AND the pixel is within a "zone of influence" of the probe's tangent plane (plane distance test, catches parallel non-coplanar surfaces). If it fails, add probes at 8x8 tile, then 4x4; if still failing, interpolate anyway ("flood fill") to stay in budget.
- Probe content: stratified sphere directions; rays that would go under the surface are reallocated to useful directions, weighted by the cosine/BRDF and by the previous frame's radiance mip (importance/ray guiding). Probe radiance stored as an 8x8 octahedral map; all probe maps tiled into one atlas (atlas size ~ screen / tile size x 8), then filtered (and a mip of it guides the next frame's rays).
- World-space probes are the fallback: a clip-map voxel grid of SH irradiance (visibility-aware), used beyond ~2 m from camera where angular detail is not needed, updated gradually and nearest first; they also supply a first guess for ray guiding of screen probes.
- Surface cache: offline-generated "cards" (box-like projections around static meshes; albedo, normal, roughness plus cached lighting) packed in one texture atlas, updated gradually with temporal blending. Rays hit the card, not the triangle. Gives multiple bounces and stability; explains the slow fade in the cave demo (slow temporal blend of the card lighting). Dynamic/deforming meshes have no cards: they use screen probes, world probes and SSR/AO/SDF fallbacks.
- Not covered in the video: ms costs, memory sizes of the atlas, denoiser details, the SDF/software trace (promised for a later video).
- Limits implied: probe count and rays/pixel are the main quality knob; distant and off-screen light is only from the world probes/cards; thin/layered geometry needs the plane-distance test; the whole thing needs ray or SDF tracing.

### For us
- Advent d3d8 fork: our `gi=1` pass (u2shaders.hpp ~7305) already is a screen-space radiance-cascade pass: 4 cascades (farthest first, each merging the one before), G-buffer from an INTZ depth texture, a world cache grid (gicache, 64-unit cells, ping-pong, with facing normals and the game's lights added) as the fallback, half-res option. So the Lumen "screen probes + world-probe fallback" structure is mostly already there. Concrete changes this video suggests: (1) interpolate/upsample the half-res cascades with the video's two-test weight: normal agreement plus distance to the probe's tangent plane (zone of influence) instead of depth alone; that cuts light bleeding across thin ledges and is a few ALU ops in the CascadePS/ResolvePS. (2) Jitter which pixel of each tile holds the probe per frame (fixed Bayer-like sequence) and accumulate with the light TAA we already have, so fewer probes per frame (e.g. 1 per 8x8 at 1/4 cost) converge in time. (3) Store the probe directional result as a small octahedral map (e.g. 4x4 or 8x8 texels per probe in one atlas texture) rather than per-pixel gather, so many pixels share one gather. In ps_3_0 this is possible with render-to-texture, 4 samples/cell, but costs an extra pass and VRAM; the gain is mainly cost (our view). (4) Importance-guide the rays with the previous frame's probe map mip (our view: only helps if rays per probe are the bottleneck; our cascades are fixed-direction merges, so lower priority).
- Out of reach in D3D9/ps_3_0: real ray/SDF tracing in world space (only the depth buffer is visible; no hit for off-screen geometry), the surface cache/cards (no mesh access or per-mesh card generation without engine changes), compute-shader style probe atlas scatter and indirect dispatch (we only have full-screen quad passes, ps_3_0 loops and 4 MRT limits), importance-sampled ray reallocation per probe.
- Bevy (Avalon, Goobi 2.5D): here we can do the full thing for a retro look. A small screen-probe pass (16x16 tile, 8x8 octahedral, 64-128 rays vs a low-poly SDF or voxel scene, 1 jittered probe per tile per frame) plus a world-space SH clip-map fallback fits a PS1-grade scene easily, but (our view) for a static retro town an offline-baked volume (see the Activision section) is cheaper and more stable; use Lumen-style screen probes only for dynamic lights (muzzle flash, lamps, gore glow). For the 2D/2.5D Goobi brawler, a screen-probe grid with 2D rays against a depth/lightmask texture is the only part that matters.
